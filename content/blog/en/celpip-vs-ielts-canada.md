Both tests lead to the same destination, a Canadian Language Benchmark (CLB) score on your Express Entry profile, so **CELPIP vs IELTS for Canada** is not a question of which is "better". It is a question of which test you can perform your best on, for your own programme and your own skills. This guide compares them on the points that decide it: what Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) accepts, how scores convert, how each test feels on the day, and how to test yourself before you pay.

This is language-test guidance, not legal or immigration advice. We read the IRCC Express Entry language-test page on 15 November 2026 (the page was dated 16 October 2026). Rules and numbers change, so confirm them on the official page before you book.

## What IRCC accepts for Express Entry

On the official page, the English tests listed for Express Entry are **CELPIP**, **IELTS** and **PTE Core**, with **TEF Canada** and **TCF Canada** for French. The conditions that matter:

- For CELPIP you must take the **CELPIP-General** test.
- For IELTS you must take the **IELTS General Training** option, and **IELTS One Skill Retake is not accepted** for Express Entry.
- Your results must be **less than 2 years old** when you complete your Express Entry profile and again when you submit your permanent residence application. IRCC says it will refuse an application that uses expired results.

So both tests are accepted. Your decision is not "which is allowed" but "which gives me my best CLB numbers". Other programmes, such as provincial nominee streams, citizenship or professional regulators, can have different rules, so check the page for the programme you are actually using.

## How the scores convert to CLB

IRCC converts each skill separately: Speaking, Listening, Reading and Writing. A weak skill can hold down the whole profile, so look at your lowest number, not at an average.

For the Federal Skilled Worker table and CELPIP-General, the same number carries across: **a 7 is CLB 7, an 8 is CLB 8, a 9 is CLB 9** in each skill. For IELTS General Training the conversion is not one-to-one, and the Listening skill is stricter than the others:

| CLB | IELTS Speaking | IELTS Listening | IELTS Reading | IELTS Writing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | 6.0 | 6.0 | 6.0 | 6.0 |
| 8 | 6.5 | 7.5 | 6.5 | 6.5 |
| 9 | 7.0 | 8.0 | 7.0 | 7.0 |

This table repeats the numbers on the IRCC page we read. Our [IELTS to CLB conversion guide](/en/blog/ielts-clb-score-canada/) explains how to read the full chart. The practical point is that the **CLB 8 or 9 target is harder in IELTS Listening** than in the other IELTS skills, because the bands needed are 7.5 and 8.0. If Listening is your strongest skill, that matters less. If it is your weakest, it deserves attention.

Minimum levels depend on the programme. On the Express Entry page, the Federal Skilled Worker first-language minimum is CLB 7 in all four abilities; the Canadian Experience Class asks for CLB 7 for jobs in TEER 0 or 1 and CLB 5 for TEER 2 or 3; and the Federal Skilled Trades asks for CLB 5 in speaking and listening and CLB 4 in reading and writing. Check your own category before choosing a target.

## Test format and experience: where they feel different

The following is how the formats are generally described by the test providers; confirm the current details when you book.

**CELPIP-General** is a Canadian test taken on a computer in a single sitting. Speaking is recorded by the computer rather than done face to face, and the test uses everyday Canadian situations: workplace messages, a customer complaint, a community announcement. If you are comfortable with a keyboard and talking to a screen, this can feel calm. If you perform better with a human listener who reacts, it can feel odd.

**IELTS General Training** is run worldwide, and the Speaking test is an interview with an examiner, usually face to face. Reading and Writing use practical texts and a letter in Task 1, and a short essay in Task 2. Some centres offer computer-delivered IELTS, others use paper. Listening in IELTS has traditionally included a mix of English accents, while CELPIP content is set in a Canadian context; both are fine for a prepared learner, but a candidate who learned mostly American or Canadian English may notice the difference.

A few practical differences to weigh:

1. **Speaking style.** With an examiner you can ask for clarification and receive facial feedback; with a computer you cannot. Many learners like one and dislike the other.
2. **Writing format.** Both test an email or letter and a short essay. In IELTS, handwriting or typing depends on the version you choose.
3. **Booking and results.** Availability, price and result times depend on your city and country. Compare the nearest centres before you decide, and avoid assuming either is always faster or cheaper.
4. **Reuse.** CELPIP is mostly relevant for Canada. An IELTS result can be reused for other countries, such as the UK, Australia or New Zealand, if the version and score fit their rules; see our [IELTS for immigration guide](/en/blog/ielts-for-immigration-guide/).

## CELPIP vs IELTS for Canada: how to choose in five steps

There is no universal winner. A comparison that works:

{{diagram: d2 | Choose by evidence from your own practice, not by reputation.}}

1. **Write down your target.** For the programme you are applying for, note the CLB you need in each skill.
2. **Take a level check.** A short placement test shows where your vocabulary and grammar are at A2 to C1, so you know how far you are from a CLB 7 profile. A rough result is better than a guess.
3. **Try both formats.** Take a timed sample of each for Speaking and Writing at least, using official practice materials from the test providers.
4. **Compare skill by skill.** Which format makes your lowest skill better? A computer-recorded Speaking section helps some people and hurts others.
5. **Check logistics.** Look at dates, price, location and the retake rules. IELTS One Skill Retake is not accepted for Express Entry, so a retake there means a full test.

If you are still uncertain, the extra cost of trying a practice test in each format is small compared with paying for the wrong one.

## Preparing for either test

Most of the preparation is not specific to the test. Both ask you to understand everyday and workplace English, to write clear messages and to speak in organised paragraphs. The skills that raise your CLB level are shared: listening in real speech, reading quickly for the main point, using accurate grammar, and producing complete answers under time.

- **Listening:** daily short dictations and listening to different speakers, as in our guide to [IELTS listening tips](/en/blog/ielts-listening-tips/).
- **Reading:** practise skimming and scanning for details; see [skimming and scanning](/en/blog/ielts-reading-skimming-and-scanning/).
- **Writing:** write an email and a short opinion text each week, then compare them with models; start with the [General Training letter guide](/en/blog/ielts-general-training-letter/).
- **Speaking:** record yourself answering questions for one to two minutes without stopping, and listen for hesitation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is CELPIP easier than IELTS?

Neither is easier in general. They test similar skills in different formats, and people differ in how they feel about computer-recorded versus face-to-face Speaking. Choose by practice results.

### Does CELPIP score map directly to CLB?

On the IRCC tables we read for CELPIP-General, the number in each skill is the CLB level in that skill (for example 7 for CLB 7). Check the current table before you plan.

### Can I use an IELTS result for other countries as well?

Often yes, if the version and the scores meet the other country's rules and the result is still valid. See our guide to IELTS for immigration for checks by country.

### Which test is better for Express Entry?

Both are accepted. The better one is the one that gives you the higher CLB numbers in your lowest skills, after you have tried the formats.
