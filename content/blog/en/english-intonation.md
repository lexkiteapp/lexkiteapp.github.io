Words are only half of spoken English. The other half is the **music** of the sentence: where the voice goes up, where it falls and which word gets the big move. This is **English intonation**, and it carries meaning that grammar does not. Say *"You are coming?"* with a rising voice and you are asking; say it with a falling voice and you are surprised or annoyed. For many learners, fixing intonation does more for how clear and friendly they sound than perfecting individual sounds.

This guide explains the main patterns, the rules that cover most everyday speech, and a practice routine you can do in ten minutes a day.

## What is English intonation?

Intonation is the **change in pitch** across a phrase. It works together with:

- **Word stress:** the strong syllable inside a word (*PHOtograph*, *phoTOgrapher*).
- **Sentence stress:** the important words in a sentence (*I WANT to GO*).
- **Rhythm:** English alternates strong and weak beats.

You can think of it as a melody on top of stressed words. For stress basics, read our [word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) and [sentence stress guide](/en/blog/sentence-stress-in-english-speaking-guide/) first; intonation is easier once you can hear the strong beats.

## The three patterns that matter most

{{diagram: d1 | Most everyday sentences use one of three patterns. Learn their jobs, not just their shapes.}}

### Falling intonation (↘)

The voice starts higher on the important word and falls. Use it for:

- **Statements:** *I live in Calgary.* ↘
- **Wh- questions** (what, where, when, who, why, how): *Where do you work?* ↘
- **Commands:** *Close the door.* ↘
- **Finished ideas:** a falling voice signals "I am done".

### Rising intonation (↗)

The voice goes up at the end. Use it for:

- **Yes/no questions:** *Are you ready?* ↗
- **Checking or confirming:** *Seven o'clock?* ↗
- **Polite offers and requests:** *Would you like some tea?* ↗
- **Unfinished ideas** in a list or a long sentence: *We could go to the park, ↗ or we could stay home.* ↘

### Fall-rise (↘↗)

The voice falls and then rises. It often shows doubt, politeness or "yes, but…":

- *I like it… ↘↗ (but it is expensive).*
- *Well, ↘↗ it depends.*
- *It is possible. ↘↗* (implies you are not sure)

## Intonation rules with examples

### Lists

Rising on every item except the last, which falls:

*We need milk ↗, eggs ↗, butter ↗ and bread ↘.*

If you fall on every item, it can sound as if each one is a separate sentence. If you rise on the last one, the listener feels you are not finished.

### Tag questions

Use **rising** when you really are not sure and want an answer: *You have already eaten, haven't you? ↗*. Use **falling** when you are almost sure and only want agreement: *It is a beautiful day, isn't it? ↘*

### Choices

*Would you like tea ↗ or coffee? ↘* The first option rises, the last one falls.

### Direct address and greetings

A falling pattern sounds firm: *Good morning ↘.* A rising pattern sounds friendly and light: *Good morning? ↗* is often used when you are not sure someone has heard you. In practice, a small rise in the middle and a gentle fall at the end sounds warm.

### The focus word

In each phrase, one syllable carries the biggest move. It is usually the **last stressed word**, unless you want to emphasise something else.

{{diagram: d2 | Move the big pitch change to the word you want to emphasise; the meaning changes with it.}}

Compare:

- *I did not take the BLUE one.* (I took a different colour.)
- *I did not TAKE the blue one.* (I did something else with it.)
- *I did NOT take the blue one.* (I deny it.)

Same words, three different meanings, all with intonation.

## English intonation in everyday situations

Here is how the patterns work in short real-life exchanges.

**At a café.** *"Hi, what can I get you? ↘"* (wh- question, falling) — *"A large coffee, please. ↘"* — *"For here or to go? ↘"* The choice falls on the last option because the list is finished.

**On the phone.** *"Hello? ↗"* (checking that someone is there) — *"Hi, this is Anna. ↘"* — *"Could I speak to Mr Lee? ↗"* The polite request rises.

**Asking for repetition.** *"Sorry? ↗"* is a short, friendly way to say you did not hear. A falling *"Sorry. ↘"* sounds like an apology instead.

**Agreeing and disagreeing.** *"Yes, ↘ I think so."* is clear agreement. *"Well, ↘↗ maybe."* shows doubt, and the listener understands you are not fully convinced without any extra words.

**Giving instructions.** *"First, ↗ open the file. ↘ Then, ↗ click Save. ↘"* The rise after *first* and *then* keeps the listener waiting for the action, and the fall closes each step.

Notice that in every case the meaning changes without any new words. That is why learners who only study vocabulary and grammar sometimes feel misunderstood, even when their sentences are perfect.

## Attitude: how intonation shows feelings

Flat, narrow pitch can sound bored or unfriendly. A wide, varied pitch sounds interested and warm. Compare *"Thank you."* said flat and *"Thank you ↗↘!"* said with a clear rise-and-fall. For a service job, interview or meeting, a little more pitch range goes a long way. Do not exaggerate: a natural voice sounds like you on a good day, a bit brighter and slower.

## Intonation and exams

In IELTS Speaking, pronunciation is one of four criteria. Using stress, rhythm and intonation naturally is part of a high pronunciation band; speaking in a flat monotone is a common reason for lower scores. ## A ten-minute practice routine

1. **Listen (3 minutes).** Take a short clip of someone speaking clearly. Choose three sentences and mark where the voice rises or falls.
2. **Shadow (4 minutes).** Say each sentence with the speaker, copying the melody, not just the words. Our [shadowing technique guide](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) shows how.
3. **Record (3 minutes).** Say the same sentences, record and compare. Do not worry about sounding perfect; look for one thing to improve.

A useful trick is to hum the melody with no words (*mm-MM-mm-mm-MMM*). If your hum matches the speaker's, your intonation is close.

## Self-check

Mark each sentence as falling (↘) or rising (↗) at the end.

1. "What time does the train leave?"
2. "Is this seat free?"
3. "I need a pen, a notebook and a ruler."
4. "Would you like tea or coffee?"
5. "It is cold today, isn't it?" (you are sure)

### Answers

1. **↘** (wh- question).
2. **↗** (yes/no question).
3. **↗ on pen and notebook, ↘ on ruler** (list).
4. **↗ on tea, ↘ on coffee** (choice).
5. **↘** (you expect agreement).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does English intonation have strict rules?

There are strong tendencies, not laws. Speakers vary by region and mood, but the patterns above cover most everyday speech.

### Is rising intonation always a question?

No. It is also used for lists, polite offers and unfinished ideas. Some speakers also rise at the end of statements, a pattern often called "uptalk".

### How can I improve quickly?

Imitate short clips daily, record yourself and compare. Focus on sentence stress first; intonation follows.

### Does intonation matter more than pronunciation of single sounds?

For being understood and sounding natural, they work together. Clear stress and melody often help listeners more than a perfect single sound.
