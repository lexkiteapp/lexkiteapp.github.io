Spaced repetition for language learning means reviewing each word or phrase at gradually longer intervals, for example after one day, three days, a week, then a month, so that every review happens just as the memory is starting to fade. It works because recalling something you have half-forgotten strengthens it far more than rereading something you saw an hour ago. Used daily for 10–20 minutes, it lets you keep thousands of words active without spending your whole evening on revision.

This guide explains why the method works, shows a sample review calendar, and covers how to apply it to vocabulary, grammar patterns and pronunciation, plus the mistakes that make people give up.

## What spaced repetition actually is

The idea is older than any app. In the 1880s the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus tested his own memory of nonsense syllables and described what is now called the forgetting curve: we lose newly learned material quickly at first, then more slowly. He also observed that spreading practice over time made learning more efficient than massing it into a single session.

In the 1970s the Leitner box system turned this into a classroom routine: cards you answered correctly moved to a box reviewed less often; cards you missed went back to the first box. Software later replaced the boxes with algorithms that calculate a date for every card, from the early SuperMemo formulas to modern schedulers such as FSRS.

Whatever the tool, the principle is the same:

1. Learn an item.
2. Try to recall it after a short gap.
3. If you succeed, wait longer before the next attempt. If you fail, shorten the gap.
4. Repeat until the interval is months long.

## Why spacing beats cramming

Cramming the night before a test can produce a decent score the next morning, which is why it feels effective. A week later, most of it is gone. Spacing produces slower short-term results but much longer-lasting memory. Three mechanisms explain the difference.

- **Retrieval effort.** When a word has partly faded, pulling it back requires effort, and that effort strengthens the memory trace. Seeing it again too soon requires almost none.
- **Varied contexts.** Reviews spread over days happen in different moods, places and states of mind, which gives your brain more routes back to the word.
- **Efficient use of time.** You stop reviewing what you already know well and spend your minutes on what is slipping. A well-spaced deck of 2,000 words can need less daily time than a badly managed list of 300.

There is one more benefit language learners often overlook: spacing reveals which words are genuinely difficult for you. A cognate that a French speaker remembers instantly might return every few months, while an idiom like *on the fence* keeps coming back until it finally sticks.

## A sample spaced repetition schedule

Here is what a simple fixed schedule looks like for ten words learned on a Monday. Real algorithms adjust these gaps for each card, but this calendar is a good starting point if you study with paper cards.

| Review | Day | Gap since last review | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learn | Monday, week 1 | – | Study each word with a phrase and an example |
| 1 | Tuesday, week 1 | 1 day | Recall all ten; mark any you missed |
| 2 | Thursday, week 1 | 2 days | Recall again; missed words go back to review 1 |
| 3 | Monday, week 2 | 4 days | Recall, then write one sentence with two of the words |
| 4 | Monday, week 3 | 1 week | Recall; most words should feel easy now |
| 5 | About week 7 | 4 weeks | Quick check; stable words can wait months |

### How long should the gaps be?

Research gives a useful rule of thumb. A large review of spacing studies by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) confirmed that spaced practice beats massed practice across hundreds of experiments, and their follow-up work (2008) found that the best gap depends on how long you need to remember: roughly, the further away your test, the longer the gaps can be. For an exam three months away, reviews a few weeks apart are efficient; for a test next week, gaps of a day or two make more sense. Algorithms handle this automatically by lengthening each gap after every successful recall.

Notice that the effort is front-loaded. In the first week you see each word three or four times; after a month, almost never. That is why adding a steady, modest number of new words each day keeps the workload predictable. If you would rather not track dates yourself, an algorithm like the one described in [the FSRS algorithm explained](/en/blog/fsrs-algorithm-explained/) does it card by card.

### What a real daily session looks like

Picture a learner three weeks into a routine of twelve new words a day. When she opens her deck in the morning, about forty cards are due: a handful from yesterday, a larger group from last week, and a few older words resurfacing after a month. She works through them in about twelve minutes, missing five. Those five come back the next day; the others disappear for days or weeks. Only then does she add twelve new words.

Two details matter here. First, reviews always come before new material, because a missed review costs more than a postponed new word. Second, the number of due cards stays roughly stable from week to week, since old words keep moving further out while new ones join at the front. If your queue keeps growing instead, you are adding cards faster than your memory can absorb them.

## Applying spaced repetition for language learning: what goes on a card

Spaced repetition only strengthens what is on the card, so card design matters as much as timing.

**Test production, not just recognition.** Seeing *deteriorate* and recognising it is easier than producing it when you need a word for "get worse". Have at least some cards that show the meaning or a gapped sentence and ask for the English word.

**Put the word in a phrase.** Instead of *commute*, use *a long commute to work* or *I commute by train*. Phrases carry grammar and collocation for free; the guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) explains which partnerships are worth a card of their own.

**Add sound.** Include audio or at least the stressed syllable: *comMUnicate*, *communiCAtion*. A word you only know from print is often unrecognisable in fast speech.

**One fact per card.** If a card asks for meaning, preposition and plural at once, you will rate it inconsistently. Split complex items into two or three cards.

**Use it for grammar patterns too.** Spaced repetition is not only for vocabulary. A card such as "I'm looking forward ___ meeting you" (answer: *to*, followed by *-ing*) targets a classic error, *looking forward to meet*, that survives years of exposure because the learner never has to produce the pattern under pressure. A few dozen cards like this, each testing one pattern, fix errors that rereading a grammar book does not.

## Combining spaced repetition with other techniques

Spaced repetition decides *when* to review. Other techniques decide *how* to review and how to turn knowledge into skill.

- **Active recall** is the "how". Always try to produce the answer before revealing it. The two methods together are far stronger than either alone; see [active recall for vocabulary](/en/blog/active-recall-for-vocabulary/).
- **Reading and listening** show words in natural contexts and act as unplanned extra reviews. Meeting a word from your deck in an article is one of the best things that can happen to it.
- **Speaking practice** turns retrieval into fluent production. Techniques like [shadowing](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) help phrases from your cards come out at natural speed.
- **A clear word-selection strategy** keeps the deck relevant. The seven-step plan in [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/) shows how to choose words worth spacing.

## Common mistakes when starting out

- **Adding too many new cards.** Fifty new cards today means roughly fifty extra reviews tomorrow and more the day after. Start with 10–15 a day and increase only if reviews stay under 20 minutes.
- **Skipping days, then quitting.** A missed week creates a frightening pile. Building [daily English habits for IELTS](/en/blog/daily-english-habits-for-ielts/) with habit stacking and streak freezes helps prevent this problem entirely. Do not delete your deck; review a manageable chunk each day, pause new cards, and the pile clears in a few days.
- **Rating too generously.** Pressing "Good" after a long hesitation tells the scheduler you know the word, so it returns too late.
- **Cards without context.** Single-word cards with a translation are quick to make but give you nothing to use in a sentence.
- **Treating the deck as the goal.** Reviews keep words alive; reading, listening, writing and speaking make them useful.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does spaced repetition work for subjects other than languages?

Yes. It works for any material that needs to be remembered over time, such as medical terms, formulas, historical dates or legal definitions. Language learning is simply one of the areas where the volume of items makes it especially useful.

### How much time should I spend on reviews each day?

For most learners, 10–20 minutes of reviews plus a small batch of new cards is sustainable. If reviews take longer than that for several days, reduce new cards until the load settles.

### If I miss a few days, do I have to start over?

No. Overdue cards are still in your memory to some degree. Review them over the next few days, add no new cards meanwhile, and the schedule will recover.

### How many words can I learn with spaced repetition?

At a steady 10–15 new words a day, you add roughly 300–450 words a month while reviews stay at 10–20 minutes. Over a year that is several thousand words, which is the range where reading in English starts to feel comfortable. If you are not sure which words to add first, the free [vocabulary lists by topic](/en/vocabulary/) are a practical starting point.

### Do I need an app, or will paper cards work?

Paper cards in a Leitner box work well for small sets. Once your collection grows to hundreds of words, an app saves time by calculating each card's date and keeping audio and examples in one place.
