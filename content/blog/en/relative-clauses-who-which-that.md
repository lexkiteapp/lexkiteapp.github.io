**Relative clauses** let you add information about a noun without starting a new sentence: *the teacher **who** marked my essay*, *the app **that** I use every day*. They are short clauses that begin with a relative word (*who, which, that, whose, where, when*) and sit right after the noun they describe. Get them right and your writing sounds connected and mature; get them wrong and examiners notice at once, because the errors (a missing comma, a doubled pronoun, *that* after a comma) are easy to spot.

This guide covers the five relative words you need, the difference between defining and non-defining clauses, when you can drop the pronoun, and the mistakes that learners make most often.

## What is a relative clause?

Compare these two ways of giving the same information:

- *I met a woman. She speaks six languages.*
- *I met a woman **who speaks six languages**.*

The second version joins the ideas. *Who speaks six languages* is a relative clause: it has its own verb (*speaks*), it describes the noun before it (*a woman*), and the relative pronoun *who* replaces *she*. That last point matters: the pronoun **replaces** the subject or object, so you never keep both.

- ✗ *I met a woman who she speaks six languages.*
- ✓ *I met a woman who speaks six languages.*

## Who, which, that, whose, where: choosing the right word

{{diagram: d1 | Pick the relative word by what the noun is: a person, a thing, a possession, a place or a time}}

**Who** is for people: *the engineer who designed the bridge*. In formal writing you may see **whom** when the person is the object (*the candidate whom we interviewed*), especially after a preposition: *the colleague **to whom** I sent the report*. In everyday English, *who* or *that* is normal in object position.

**Which** is for things, animals and ideas: *the laptop which I bought last year*. It can also point back to a whole clause after a comma: *The train was cancelled, **which** meant we missed the meeting.*

**That** works for people and things, but only in **defining** clauses (explained below): *the doctor that treated me*, *the rule that confuses everyone*. In American English, *that* is the usual choice for things in defining clauses; British English uses both *that* and *which*.

**Whose** shows possession and works for people *and* things: *a student **whose** essay won a prize*, *a company **whose** profits doubled*. Do not confuse it with *who's* (= *who is* / *who has*).

**Where** and **when** replace a place or a time phrase: *the café **where** we first met* (= *at which*), *the year **when** I moved abroad* (= *in which*). If the clause needs an object, use *which* instead: *the city **which** I visited* (I visited *the city*; there is no "at" or "in"), not *the city where I visited*.

## Defining vs non-defining relative clauses

{{diagram: d2 | Commas change the meaning: without them the clause identifies the noun; with them it only adds information}}

This distinction causes most of the errors in exams, so it is worth slowing down.

A **defining** (or restrictive) clause tells us *which one* we mean. Remove it and the sentence loses essential information:

- *Students **who submit late** lose marks.* (Only the late ones, not all students.)

A **non-defining** (non-restrictive) clause adds extra information about a noun that is already clear. Remove it and the main message stays the same:

- *My manager, **who has worked here for twenty years**, is retiring.* (I have one manager; the clause is a bonus fact.)

Three rules follow:

1. **Commas.** Non-defining clauses go between commas (or a comma and a full stop). Defining clauses take no commas.
2. **No *that*.** Non-defining clauses use *who*, *which* or *whose*, never *that*. ✗ *London, that is the capital, is huge.* ✓ *London, which is the capital, is huge.*
3. **No dropping.** In non-defining clauses the pronoun must stay (see the next section).

The commas can change the meaning completely:

- *The players **who were injured** stayed at the hotel.* → Only some players were injured; those stayed.
- *The players, **who were injured**, stayed at the hotel.* → All the players were injured, and all stayed.

Proper names, unique things (*the sun*, *my mother*) and nouns already identified usually need a non-defining clause: *Mount Everest, which is 8,849 metres high*, not *Mount Everest which is…*.

## Dropping the relative pronoun

In a **defining** clause you can leave out the pronoun when it is the **object** of the clause verb:

- *The book (**that**) you lent me was brilliant.* → *you* is the subject of *lent*; *that* is the object, so it can go.
- *The people (**who**) we met in Goa were friendly.*

You **cannot** drop it when it is the **subject**:

- ✗ *The man lives next door is a pilot.*
- ✓ *The man **who** lives next door is a pilot.*

A quick test: if the relative word is followed directly by a verb, it is the subject and must stay. If it is followed by a new subject (*you, we, she, the manager*), it is the object and may be dropped.

### Reduced relative clauses

Advanced writers often shorten subject clauses with a participle:

- *Students **who live** on campus…* → *Students **living** on campus…* (active, -ing)
- *The data **which was collected** in 2024…* → *The data **collected** in 2024…* (passive, past participle)

These reduced clauses are common in academic English and IELTS Writing. They connect closely with the [passive voice](/en/blog/passive-voice-in-english/), so if *collected* in the second example looks odd, review that first.

## Prepositions in relative clauses

When the clause verb needs a preposition, English gives you two positions:

- **Informal:** *the person (who) I spoke **to***, *the project (that) she is working **on***
- **Formal:** *the person **to whom** I spoke*, *the project **on which** she is working*

After a preposition, use only *whom* or *which*, never *who* or *that*: ✗ *the person to who I spoke*, ✗ *the project on that she is working*. Most everyday English uses the informal pattern. In formal essays the preposition-first version sounds more polished, but only use it if it feels natural; a stiff *with whom* in a casual email sounds strange.

## Common mistakes with relative clauses

Many errors come from the learner's first language. Languages such as Hindi, Japanese, Korean or Chinese place descriptions **before** the noun or use a different linking structure, so learners translate word by word and end up with extra pronouns or wrong order.

| Mistake | Correction | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *The laptop which I bought it is slow.* | *The laptop which I bought is slow.* | *which* already replaces *it*. |
| *My sister, that is a nurse, works nights.* | *My sister, who is a nurse, works nights.* | Non-defining clauses never use *that*. |
| *The city where I visited was beautiful.* | *The city (that) I visited was beautiful.* | *visit* takes a direct object; no place adverb. |
| *Everyone which came enjoyed it.* | *Everyone who came enjoyed it.* | People need *who* or *that*. |
| *The girl who's bag was stolen…* | *The girl whose bag was stolen…* | *who's* means *who is*. |
| *I passed the test what made me happy.* | *I passed the test, which made me happy.* | *what* is not a relative pronoun after a noun or clause. |

Another subtle issue is **verb agreement**. The verb in the clause agrees with the noun the pronoun replaces: *one of the students **who are** late* (the students are late) vs *the only one of the students **who is** late*. Native writers argue about the first one, but in exams the logical agreement is safest.

## Relative clauses in IELTS and academic writing

Relative clauses are one of the clearest ways to show grammatical range, a criterion examiners look for in IELTS Writing and Speaking. They let you pack definitions, reasons and examples into one controlled sentence:

- *Countries **which invest heavily in public transport** tend to have lower urban emissions.*
- *Remote work, **which became common after 2020**, has changed how cities plan office space.*

Two warnings. First, accuracy matters more than length; a long sentence with a comma error loses more than a short correct one. Second, do not stack clauses: *the report which the team which I joined wrote* is hard to read. If a sentence has more than two relative clauses, split it. To see controlled relative clauses in context, read a few of our [IELTS model essays](/en/samples/) and underline every *who*, *which* and *that*. ## Self-check: test yourself

Fill each gap with *who, which, that, whose, where* or **—** (nothing), and add commas where needed.

1. The colleague ___ desk is next to mine is on leave.
2. My grandmother ___ is 90 still walks every day.
3. Is this the restaurant ___ you recommended?
4. The village ___ my father was born has no school now.
5. She finally replied to my message ___ was a relief.
6. Anyone ___ wants a refund should keep the receipt.
7. The phone ___ I ordered arrived broken.
8. Tokyo ___ hosted the Olympics in 2021 is a huge city.

### Answers

1. **whose** (possession: the colleague's desk)
2. **, who** … *My grandmother, who is 90, still walks every day.* (Non-defining: you have one grandmother.)
3. **that / which / —** (object of *recommended*, so the pronoun is optional)
4. **where** (= *in which* my father was born)
5. **, which** … *She finally replied to my message, which was a relief.* (Refers to the whole idea.)
6. **who / that** (subject of *wants*; cannot be dropped)
7. **that / which / —** (object of *ordered*)
8. **, which** … *Tokyo, which hosted the Olympics in 2021, is a huge city.* (A proper name needs commas, so *that* is not possible.)

If you missed 2, 5 or 8, the comma rule is your weak point. If you missed 6, review dropping the pronoun.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it wrong to use "which" in a defining clause?

No. *The car which I bought* is correct and very common in British English. American style guides prefer *that* for defining clauses and keep *which* for non-defining ones, so if you write for a US audience, *that* is the safer choice.

### When should I use "whom"?

Use *whom* when the person is the object of the clause, mainly in formal writing and always directly after a preposition: *to whom*, *with whom*. In speech and informal writing, *who* or *that* (or no pronoun) sounds more natural.

### Can "that" refer to people?

Yes, in defining clauses: *the woman that I met* is standard English. *Who* is slightly more common for people, especially in formal writing, and only *who* is possible in non-defining clauses.

### How do I know if a clause needs commas?

Ask whether the clause identifies the noun or just adds information. If the reader already knows exactly who or what you mean (a name, *my mother*, *the sun*), use commas. If the clause tells the reader which one you mean, leave the commas out.
