In **TOEIC Part 3 and 4**, you listen to short workplace conversations and talks and answer three questions about each. Together they make up 69 of the 100 Listening questions, so they are the largest block of marks in the Listening section. The audio is played once, the questions follow the order of the information, and the answers tend to come from main purpose, problems, requests and next steps. This guide explains the format, a three-question routine, the special item types and the traps that catch candidates.

## What TOEIC Part 3 and 4 look like

{{diagram: d1 | The two parts use the same question skills with different kinds of audio}}

In Part 3 you hear a conversation between two or three speakers, such as colleagues planning a project or a customer calling a shop. In Part 4 you hear one speaker: a recorded message, an announcement, a short tour introduction or a news item. Accents include North American, British, Australian and others. For the overall layout of all seven parts, see the [TOEIC test format guide](/en/blog/toeic-test-format/).

## The three-question routine

{{diagram: d2 | Read the questions while the directions play, then follow the audio in order}}

You have only a few seconds between sets, so the routine must be quick:

1. **Preview the three questions and options.** Do it while the directions are read or the previous answers are finishing, not after the audio starts.
2. **Predict.** Is the question about who is speaking, where they are, what problem they have, what they will do next? That tells you what to listen for.
3. **Listen in order.** The first question usually matches the beginning, the second the middle, and the third the end. If you lose one, answer the next.
4. **Mark immediately.** Do not store answers in your head.

If you are still working on the earlier parts of the test, the practice in [TOEIC listening Part 2 tactics](/en/blog/toeic-listening-part-2-tactics/) builds the same prediction skill.

## Question types and what they test

| Question | What to listen for | Example (original) |
|---|---|---|
| Main topic or purpose | The opening sentence | *Why is the man calling?* |
| Place or job | Words that suggest the setting | *Where does the conversation take place?* |
| Detail | Names, times, numbers, objects | *When will the delivery arrive?* |
| Problem | *Unfortunately, I'm afraid, there's an issue* | *What problem does the woman mention?* |
| Request or suggestion | *Could you, why don't we, would you mind* | *What does the man ask the woman to do?* |
| Next action | The last few lines | *What will the speaker do next?* |

Most of these questions are about the situation, so you do not need to understand every word. You need the main idea, the problem and the plan.

## Original example: a Part 3 conversation

Script: *Woman: Hi Marcus, did the printer on the third floor get repaired? Man: Not yet. The technician said he needs a new part, which won't arrive until Thursday. Woman: That's a problem, because I have to print the conference handouts tomorrow. Man: Why don't you use the printer in the marketing department? I can send you the access code.*

Questions: *What does the man say about the third-floor printer? What problem does the woman have? What does the man offer to do?*

The answers are: it is waiting for a part that will arrive on Thursday; the woman must print handouts tomorrow while the printer is broken; the man offers to send an access code for another printer. Notice that the questions follow the order of the conversation, and the final answer comes from the last speaker's offer.

## Original example: a Part 4 talk

Script: *Attention, passengers on flight 218 to Boston. Because of heavy fog, boarding will be delayed by about forty minutes. The new boarding time is nine fifteen at gate twelve. Passengers with connecting flights should speak to an agent at the service desk, who will check your options.*

Questions: *Who is the speaker most likely addressing? What is the reason for the delay? What are some passengers advised to do?*

The answers are travellers waiting for a flight, fog, and speak to an agent at the service desk. In Part 4, the first sentence usually names the audience and the situation, so previewing a question such as *Who is the speaker addressing?* lets you catch it immediately. The reason for a change normally follows within the next sentence, introduced by words such as *because of* or *due to*. Advice or instructions usually come at the end, introduced by *should*, *please* or *make sure*.

## Graphics questions

Some Part 3 and Part 4 sets include a table, map, schedule or chart. You must connect what you hear with what you see. Before the audio starts, look at the graphic: which labels, times or prices are there? Then, as the speaker mentions one item, find the matching row. A frequent trap is that the speaker mentions several items, and the question asks for the one that changed or the one that is cheaper. Read the question first so that you know which detail to track.

## Speaker intention questions

In these items you hear a line from the audio and are asked what the speaker means. The words alone can mislead: *I'm sure that will be fun* may be sincere or ironic. Listen to the context before and after the line, and choose the answer that fits the situation, not the literal sentence. Everyday expressions such as *I'll take care of it* (accepting a task) or *Let me see what I can do* (not promising) appear often. Practising [daily English conversation sentences](/en/blog/daily-english-conversation-sentences-guide/) and [business English meeting phrases](/en/blog/business-english-meeting-phrases-guide/) builds this ear.

## Traps in Parts 3 and 4

| Trap | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Similar sounds | *a copy* versus *a coffee* | Choose the meaning that fits the situation |
| Distractor words | An option repeats a word from the audio but answers a different question | Check the whole sentence |
| Time changes | A meeting moved from Tuesday to Thursday | Listen for the final arrangement |
| Pronoun confusion | *He* and *she* in a three-person conversation | Track who does what |
| Lost place | Staying on a missed question | Skip and listen for the next one |

For speed and sound reduction, see [how to catch fast speech](/en/blog/how-to-catch-fast-speech-in-ielts-listening/); the same sound patterns appear in TOEIC.

## A two-week practice plan

| Days | Practice |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | Preview and predict only; read three questions and say what you expect to hear |
| 4–6 | Part 3 sets with a transcript review |
| 7–9 | Part 4 sets, one type at a time (announcements, voicemail, tours) |
| 10–11 | Graphics and speaker-intention items |
| 12–14 | Timed sets of both parts; review every error by type |

## Frequently asked questions

### How many questions are in TOEIC Part 3 and Part 4?

Part 3 has 39 questions and Part 4 has 30, so together they have 69 of the 100 Listening questions. Each conversation or talk has three questions.

### Is the audio played twice in TOEIC Listening?

No. It is played once, so previewing and predicting before the audio starts is essential.

### Should I read the options before listening?

Yes, if you can. Read the questions first and glance at the options, so that you know what kind of information to listen for.

### How do I improve quickly in Parts 3 and 4?

Practise the preview and predict habit daily, review every wrong answer with the transcript, and listen to varied workplace audio so that different accents become familiar.
