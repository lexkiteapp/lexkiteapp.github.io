In IELTS Reading True False Not Given questions, choose **True** if the passage confirms the statement, **False** if the passage says the opposite, and **Not Given** if the passage does not give enough information to decide either way. The hardest part is separating False from Not Given, and one question settles it: *does the passage contain information that contradicts the statement?* If yes, it is False. If the passage is simply silent, or only partly covers the idea, it is Not Given.

That rule is easy to state and surprisingly hard to apply under time pressure. This guide shows why, gives a four-step method, walks through examples from an original passage, and finishes with a short practice set.

## What True False Not Given tests and why it is tricky

The question type appears in the Reading test, which lasts 60 minutes and contains 40 questions across three passages in the Academic version. A related type, Yes/No/Not Given, asks about the writer's views or claims rather than facts; the logic is the same.

These questions are not testing what you know about the topic. They test whether you can compare two pieces of language precisely. Three things make that hard:

- **Background knowledge interferes.** If a statement sounds true in real life, you are tempted to mark True, even if the passage never says it.
- **Paraphrase hides matches.** The passage rarely repeats the statement's words. *Fell considerably* may appear as *dropped sharply*; practising [how to paraphrase in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) trains you to spot these pairs.
- **Small words change everything.** *Some*, *all*, *always*, *the first*, *only* and *mainly* can turn a nearly matching statement into False or Not Given.

## The False vs Not Given test: one question to ask

When you are stuck between False and Not Given, ask:

> **"Can I point to words in the passage that make this statement impossible?"**

- If you can underline a phrase that contradicts it, the answer is **False**.
- If you cannot, because the passage never addresses that specific point, the answer is **Not Given**.

Consider this sentence from a passage:

> *The bridge, completed in 1932, was at that time the longest of its kind in the country.*

| Statement | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The bridge opened in the 1930s. | True | 1932 is in the 1930s. |
| The bridge was completed before 1930. | False | 1932 contradicts "before 1930". |
| The bridge was expensive to build. | Not Given | Cost is never mentioned. |
| The bridge is still the longest of its kind in the country. | Not Given | The passage says "at that time"; it says nothing about today. |
| No other bridge of its kind in the country was longer in 1932. | True | "The longest of its kind" at that time means exactly this. |

The fourth statement is the classic trap. Many candidates mark it False because "at that time" *suggests* things changed. But a suggestion is not a contradiction. The passage does not say a longer bridge was built later, so the correct answer is Not Given.

### Small words that flip the answer

| Word in statement | Watch for in passage |
|---|---|
| *all, every, always* | *most, many, often* → usually False if the passage limits it |
| *the first, the only* | the passage may say *one of the first* → False |
| *mainly, mostly* | the passage may list several causes equally → often Not Given |
| *proved, confirmed* | the passage may say *suggested, indicated* → False or Not Given depending on wording |
| *more than, less than* | check the exact comparison and numbers |

Pay special attention to hedged language in academic passages. *Researchers believe* is not the same as *it has been proven*. The guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) explains these signals from the writer's side, which makes them easier to spot as a reader.

## A 4-step method for True False Not Given

**Step 1: Read the statement and mark the key idea plus the qualifiers.** Underline the main subject and the limiting words: *only*, *before 1900*, *most*, *increased*.

**Step 2: Locate the relevant part of the passage.** Questions of this type usually follow the order of the text, so the answer to question 3 is normally after the answer to question 2. Scan for names, numbers and dates first, because they do not get paraphrased easily. You can refine your search speed with the proven techniques in [skimming and scanning in IELTS Reading](/en/blog/ielts-reading-skimming-and-scanning/) and maintain full paragraph comprehension through [active reading for IELTS](/en/blog/active-reading-strategies-for-ielts/).

**Step 3: Compare meaning, not words.** Read the sentence before and after the matching point as well. Use the methods in [how to read long sentences in English](/en/blog/sentence-parsing-for-ielts-reading/) to separate parenthetical clauses from the core assertion. Ask: does it confirm, contradict or ignore each part of the statement, including the qualifiers?

**Step 4: Apply the False vs Not Given test and move on.** If it is confirmed, True. If you can underline a contradiction, False. Otherwise, Not Given. Do not spend more than about a minute and a half on one statement; mark your best answer and return if time allows.

### When you are stuck

- If you cannot find the location at all after a reasonable search, the answer is more likely to be Not Given, but first check that the next question's answer is not where you expected this one to be.
- If you find yourself saying "the passage implies…", be careful. Use only what the text states or clearly entails.
- Strong vocabulary makes location much faster. Many paraphrases in Reading draw on academic words, so working through the [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/), reviewing [sample passages](/samples/), and incorporating reading drills into your [IELTS self-study plan](/en/blog/ielts-self-study-plan/) pays off directly in this task.

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

**Answering from general knowledge.** Statements like "Exercise improves health" sound obviously true. Ignore what you know; only the passage counts.

**Treating "not mentioned" as False.** If the passage never mentions a topic, it cannot contradict it. That is Not Given by definition.

**Matching words instead of meaning.** A statement can reuse three words from the passage and still be False because one qualifier changed. Conversely, a True statement may share no words at all with the passage.

**Ignoring time markers.** *Was*, *is now*, *has become*, *at that time*, *by 2010*: a change in time frame often creates Not Given.

**Writing Yes or No instead of True or False.** Use the exact labels the instructions ask for. An answer of *Yes* to a True/False/Not Given question is marked wrong even if you meant True. On paper, write the full word or the abbreviation the instructions allow; on computer, you simply select the option.

## Practice: an original passage with answers

Read the short passage, then decide True, False or Not Given for each statement.

> *Community gardens first appeared in the city in the early 1970s, when residents began planting vegetables on empty lots. Most of the early gardens were organised by neighbourhood groups rather than by the local council. Although the council later offered small grants, many gardeners turned the money down in order to remain independent. Today, the gardens are used mainly for growing vegetables, though some also host workshops on composting.*

1. Community gardens in the city date back to the early 1970s.
2. The local council organised most of the first gardens.
3. The council offered grants from the very beginning.
4. Some gardeners chose not to accept council support.
5. Composting workshops are the main activity in the gardens today.
6. The gardens have become more popular in recent years.

**Answers:**
1. **True.** "First appeared … in the early 1970s."
2. **False.** Most were organised "by neighbourhood groups rather than by the local council".
3. **False.** The council "later offered" grants, which contradicts "from the very beginning". (If the passage had said only "offered grants", with no time reference, this would be Not Given.)
4. **True.** "Many gardeners turned the money down" confirms that some refused council support.
5. **False.** The main use is "growing vegetables"; workshops happen only in some gardens.
6. **Not Given.** The passage says nothing about changes in popularity.

If statement 3 caught you out, notice how a single adverb (*later*) creates the contradiction. That is exactly the kind of detail the test is built on.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the fastest way to tell False from Not Given?

Ask whether you can point to words in the passage that contradict the statement. If you can, it is False. If the passage does not deal with that specific point, it is Not Given.

### Do True/False/Not Given questions follow the order of the passage?

Usually, yes. The answers normally appear in the same order as the questions, which helps you narrow the search area for each statement.

### How is Yes/No/Not Given different?

Yes/No/Not Given asks whether statements agree with the writer's opinions or claims, while True/False/Not Given asks about factual information. The decision process is the same.

### Is there always at least one True, one False and one Not Given?

No rule guarantees it. A set of five statements often contains all three answers, but you should never change a well-supported answer just because one label has not appeared yet. Decide each statement on its own evidence.

### How long should I spend on each statement?

Aim for about a minute on average. The whole Reading test is 60 minutes for 40 questions, so if one statement takes much longer, choose your best answer, mark it and come back later.
