A good **follow up email in English** is short, specific and easy to answer. It reminds the reader who you are, names the exact thing you need or want to confirm, and makes the next step obvious. It is not an apology for existing in someone's inbox, and it is not a complaint.

Most learners find follow-ups harder than first emails, because the tone matters more: you are writing to someone who has already not answered, or who has already decided something about you. This guide covers four situations (after an interview, after a meeting, chasing a reply, after networking), with subject lines, five templates you can adapt, sensible timing, and the tone differences that cause the most trouble, such as "just checking in" and "per my last email". For the basic layout of any professional message, read our [guide to writing a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/) first; this article builds on it.

## Anatomy of a good follow up email in English

{{diagram: d2 | Four parts of a follow-up: subject line, opening, the point, and a closing that makes replying easy.}}

**Subject line.** It should work even if the reader sees nothing else. Name the topic and, if useful, a date or a role. Compare:

- Weak: *Following up* / *Hi* / *Quick question*
- Better: *Following up: Marketing Analyst interview on Tuesday*
- Better: *Action items from today's project meeting*
- Better: *Quick question about the Q3 budget draft*
- Better: *Nice to meet you at the supply chain meetup*

Using *Re:* in the subject of a new email to fake a reply is a bad habit; people notice, and it feels like a trick. If you are genuinely replying in the same thread, keep the thread and add your message at the top.

**Opening.** One line of context. *Thank you for your time yesterday.* *I wanted to follow up on the draft I sent last Wednesday.* Do not begin with a long apology.

**The point.** One request or one summary. If you need three things, number them, but try for one.

**Closing.** Make replying easy: *A quick yes or no is fine.* *Could you let me know by Friday?* Then a polite sign-off such as *Best regards*, *Kind regards* or *Thanks*. Our guide lists [greetings and closings by level of formality](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/).

## Five follow-up email templates you can adapt

All names and details below are made up. Replace the bracketed parts and keep your own voice.

### Template 1: after a job interview

> **Subject:** Thank you – Marketing Analyst interview
>
> Dear Ms Okafor,
>
> Thank you for taking the time to speak with me yesterday about the Marketing Analyst role. I enjoyed learning how your team uses customer data to plan campaigns.
>
> Our conversation about reducing reporting time made me even more interested in the position. In my current role I built a weekly dashboard that replaced several manual spreadsheets, and I would be glad to bring the same approach to your team.
>
> Please let me know if I can provide anything else. I look forward to hearing about the next steps.
>
> Kind regards,
> Minh Tran

Why it works: it thanks, refers to something specific from the interview (so it is not a copy-paste), adds one relevant point, and ends calmly. It does not ask "Did I get the job?". For what to say in the interview itself, see our guide to [job interview questions and answers in English](/en/blog/job-interview-questions-and-answers-in-english/).

### Template 2: after a meeting (confirming actions)

> **Subject:** Action items from today's project meeting
>
> Hi everyone,
>
> Thanks for your time this morning. Here is a quick summary of what we agreed:
>
> 1. Sara will send the revised timeline by Thursday.
> 2. Kenji will check the supplier's delivery dates and report back on Friday.
> 3. I will update the project plan and share it by the end of next week.
>
> If I have missed anything or got a date wrong, please reply and let me know. Our next meeting is on Monday at 10:00.
>
> Best regards,
> Anna

Why it works: names, tasks and dates; an invitation to correct. This is the follow-up with the highest value in daily work, because it turns a conversation into a record. For the spoken side, see [business English meeting phrases](/en/blog/business-english-meeting-phrases-guide/).

### Template 3: first reminder (no reply yet)

> **Subject:** Quick question about the Q3 budget draft
>
> Hi David,
>
> I wanted to follow up on the budget draft I sent last Wednesday. Have you had a chance to look at it?
>
> If it helps, I can walk you through the main changes in ten minutes. A quick yes or no on the revised figures would be enough to move forward.
>
> Thanks very much,
> Priya

Why it works: it is specific (which document, which day), it offers help, and it makes the answer small. *Have you had a chance to...?* is friendly and does not accuse.

### Template 4: second, firmer reminder

> **Subject:** Following up: supplier contract, deadline Friday
>
> Hi David,
>
> I am following up again on the supplier contract, as the deadline with the supplier is this Friday. I need your approval before I can reply to them.
>
> Could you let me know by Wednesday afternoon whether you are happy to go ahead, or whether you would like changes? If someone else should approve this, please point me to them.
>
> Thank you for your help.
>
> Best regards,
> Priya

Why it works: the reason for urgency is real and stated, the request has a time, and there is a way out (*someone else should approve*). It is firm without being rude.

### Template 5: after meeting someone at an event

> **Subject:** Nice to meet you at the supply chain meetup
>
> Hi Elena,
>
> It was great talking with you at the meetup on Thursday. I especially enjoyed hearing how your team handled the warehouse move.
>
> I mentioned an article about route planning that I found useful, so I have put the link below. If you ever have time for a short coffee or call, I would love to hear more about how you got into logistics.
>
> Either way, it was a pleasure to meet you.
>
> Best wishes,
> Tomas

Why it works: personal detail, something of value (the article), a low-pressure invitation, and "either way". More on starting these conversations in our guide to [networking in English at conferences](/en/blog/networking-in-english-at-conferences-guide/).

## When to send: timing that often works

There is no universal rule, and anyone who gives you an exact number of hours or days as fact is guessing. These are common patterns:

- **After an interview:** often within a day. If the interviewer told you when to expect news, wait until that date has passed before you chase.
- **After a meeting:** often the same day or the next morning, while memories are fresh.
- **Chasing an unanswered email:** often after a few working days, depending on how urgent it is and how busy the person usually is. A reply to a simple question might be expected sooner; a request for a decision, later.
- **After networking:** often within a few days, before the person forgets the conversation.

A few practical points. Count *working* days, not calendar days. Check the person's time zone and local holidays. Do not send at 2 a.m. their time if you can schedule it for their morning, since an email at the top of the inbox is more likely to get read. And do not send three reminders in two days; it looks desperate and damages the relationship.

## Tone: "just checking in" vs "per my last email"

English follow-ups fail more often because of tone than grammar. Here are the phrases learners meet most, and how they land.

> **Soft and friendly:** *Just checking in on this.* / *Just a gentle reminder.* / *Have you had a chance to look at this?*
>
> **Neutral and professional:** *I wanted to follow up on...* / *I am writing to follow up on...* / *Could you let me know by Friday?*
>
> **Cold or passive-aggressive:** *Per my last email...* / *As I mentioned before...* / *As per my previous message...* / *Please advise.* (used when someone has ignored you)

*Per my last email* is not rude in a literal sense, since it means "as I said in my previous message". But many native speakers read it as a polite way of saying "I already told you this, why are you asking again?". It is common in some offices and sounds sharp in others. If you want to refer to an earlier message, a safer version is: *I sent the details in my email on Tuesday, and I have attached them again below for convenience.*

*Just checking in* is warm and very common, but it can be vague. Its weakness is that it gives the reader nothing to do. *Just checking in to see if you had any questions about the proposal. I would be glad to arrange a call this week* is better.

Some regional tendencies, which are habits and not rules:

- **US and Canada:** emails are often warmer and more direct. First names are normal, *Hi David* is fine, and phrases like *Hope you're doing well* and *Thanks so much* are common. A firm reminder can still be friendly: *Could you get back to me by Thursday? Thanks!*
- **UK:** often a little more understated and indirect. *I just wondered whether you had had a chance to...* and *I hope you do not mind me chasing this* are typical. *Chasing* is a normal British word for following up on something overdue, and it is not negative on its own. In the US, people more often say *following up*.
- **Australia:** usually relaxed and brief; first names even with senior people, and *Cheers* as a sign-off is common.
- **Germany, Japan, Korea:** if you are writing to a company in English from these countries, you may be used to more formality or longer polite openings. English-speaking business readers generally prefer a short, direct message. Cutting a long apology is usually kind to the reader.

## Mistakes learners often make in follow-ups

1. **Starting with "Sorry to disturb you".** A short *I hope you are well* or going straight to the point is enough. Constant apology makes you sound unsure.
2. **Writing "I am waiting for your reply".** It sounds like pressure. Say *I would appreciate your reply by Friday* or *Could you let me know by Friday?*
3. **Using "kindly revert".** In some countries *revert* is used to mean "reply". Many native speakers do not use it this way and may find it confusing, since to *revert* means to go back to a previous state. Write *reply* or *get back to me*.
4. **Hiding the request.** Put the question in the first or second sentence, and in a single place.
5. **Sending the same message again.** If your first email was not answered, re-sending it word for word usually does not help. Shorten it, add the key detail, and change the subject line slightly if the situation changed.
6. **Forgetting attachments.** If you refer to a document, attach it again or add a link. Our abbreviation guide to [workplace English acronyms in email](/en/blog/workplace-english-abbreviations-email-acronyms-guide/) explains short forms like ASAP, EOD and FYI; use them lightly in follow-ups, and avoid *ASAP* with people you do not know well because it can sound like an order.

## Self-check: fix the follow-up

1. Which subject line is better: *Hi* or *Following up: Sales Assistant interview on Monday*?
2. Rewrite to be friendlier: *Per my last email, I need the report.*
3. What is wrong with this: *I am waiting for your reply since three days.*
4. After an interview, the employer said they would decide by the end of next week. When should you chase?
5. Which closing makes replying easier: *Let me know.* or *A quick yes or no is fine.*
6. In British English, which verb is common for following up on something overdue: *chase* or *run*?

**Answers**

1. *Following up: Sales Assistant interview on Monday.* It names the topic, so the reader knows what it is before opening.
2. For example: *I sent the report request on Tuesday. Could you let me know by Friday whether you can share it?* It states the date and the request without blame.
3. Two problems: *waiting for your reply* sounds like pressure, and *since three days* should be *for three days*. Better: *I would appreciate your reply when you have a moment.*
4. After the end of that next week. Chasing before the date they gave you can seem impatient.
5. *A quick yes or no is fine.* It makes the answer small and clear.
6. *Chase.* *I'm just chasing the invoice* is normal in the UK; Americans often say *following up on*.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I wait before sending a follow up email in English?

It depends on the situation and what the other person told you. After an interview, many people write within a day; to chase a reply, they often wait a few working days. If a deadline or decision date was given, wait until it has passed. Check the person's holidays and time zone too.

### Is "just checking in" rude?

No, it is friendly and common, especially in the US and Canada. It can feel vague, though, so add a specific question or offer, such as *Just checking in to see if you had any questions about the proposal*. Avoid it if you need a firm answer.

### Should I follow up more than once?

Usually one or two polite reminders are reasonable, with clear details each time. After that, consider another route, such as a different contact, a phone call, or letting it go. A final short message that says you will close the matter on your side is often a good, respectful ending.

### Is it acceptable to follow up after a rejection?

Often yes, briefly. A short thank-you that asks for feedback or says you would like to stay in touch is usually received well. Keep it positive, and do not argue with the decision.
