To reach **IELTS Speaking band 7**, you do not need perfect English or unusual vocabulary. You need to speak at length without long breaks, use a flexible range of words and grammar, and make only occasional errors. This guide shows original answers for all three parts, each with a weaker and a stronger version, so that you can see exactly what changes between them. Use the transcripts to understand the shape and the techniques, then speak about your own life; examiners listen for natural, personal answers, not memorised ones.

## What IELTS Speaking band 7 sounds like

The Speaking test lasts 11 to 14 minutes and is recorded, with an examiner marking four equally weighted criteria: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Text cannot show pronunciation, so the notes below deal with the other three; for sound, read [sentence stress in English](/en/blog/sentence-stress-in-english-speaking-guide/) and [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/).

{{diagram: d1 | The step from band 6 to band 7 is mostly development and control, not rare words}}

At band 7 you speak at length with some hesitation, use idiomatic language sometimes, and can handle complex structures with occasional mistakes. If you need to know how scores combine, read [IELTS band scores explained](/en/blog/ielts-band-scores-explained/).

## Part 1: short questions about your life

Part 1 lasts four to five minutes and covers familiar topics. A good answer is two to four sentences.

{{diagram: d2 | Answer first, then give one reason and one example}}

**Question: Do you enjoy cooking?**

*Weaker:* "Yes, I like cooking. It is good."

*Stronger:* "Yes, I do, although I'm not very experienced yet. I started cooking when I moved out for university, mainly because eating out was expensive, and now I actually find it relaxing. At the weekend I usually try one new recipe, which has taught me a lot about flavours."

**Why it works:** it answers directly, gives a reason (*because eating out was expensive*), adds an example, and uses different grammar: present simple, past simple, present perfect (*has taught me*). It also includes *although* and *which*. The guide to [IELTS Speaking Part 1](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-tips/) has more questions to practise.

## Part 2: the long turn

Part 2 gives you a card, one minute to prepare and up to two minutes to speak. Use your notes only as keywords.

**Card (original):** *Describe a place where you go to relax. You should say where it is, how often you go there, what you do there, and explain why it helps you relax.*

**Sample answer (about 200 words, roughly a minute and a half; in the exam, keep adding detail until the examiner stops you at two minutes):**

"I'd like to talk about a small park near my home, which I go to about twice a week. It isn't famous, but it has a pond, a few old trees and a wooden bench where I usually sit.

Most of the time I go after work, when my head is full of tasks. I walk around the pond twice, then sit down and just watch the ducks. Sometimes I bring a book, although honestly I often forget to open it.

What I enjoy most is the atmosphere. During the week it's very quiet, apart from a few joggers and elderly people practising tai chi, so I can hear the wind and birds instead of traffic. That contrast with my busy office is exactly why it helps me unwind.

I remember one evening last spring when it started to rain softly, and the whole park smelled of wet grass. I stayed for an hour without checking my phone, and I walked home feeling completely refreshed.

So, for me, it's more than just a green space. It's a place where I can slow down and think clearly, and I'm very glad it's within walking distance."

**Why it works:** it follows the bullets in order, uses a personal example with sensory detail (*smelled of wet grass*), stays on topic with a clear order and uses linking naturally (*although*, *apart from*, *so*). It avoids rare vocabulary; *unwind*, *refreshed* and *atmosphere* are accurate, which counts more than difficulty. See [IELTS Speaking Part 2](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-tips/) for the preparation method.

## Part 3: discussion questions

Part 3 asks more abstract questions. A good answer gives an opinion, a reason, an example or a comparison, and a balanced thought.

**Question: Why do some people prefer relaxing alone rather than with others?**

*Weaker:* "Because they like quiet. It is better for them."

*Stronger:* "I think it depends on personality. People who spend most of their day with colleagues or family often need time alone to recover, so a quiet walk can feel like a reset. On the other hand, those who live alone may find company more relaxing. Personally, I'm somewhere in between: I like solitude on weekdays but I enjoy meeting friends at weekends."

**Why it works:** it opens with a considered view, contrasts two groups, and adds a personal comment at the end. Notice the language of tentative opinion (*I think it depends on*, *often*, *may find*), which is appropriate for abstract questions. The method for extending an answer is explained in [the domino effect in IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/the-domino-effect-in-ielts-speaking/) and [Part 3 strategies](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-strategies-for-extending-answers/).

## Fluency and hesitation: what examiners actually listen for

Many candidates think fluency means speaking fast. It does not. Fluency at band 7 means you keep going without noticeable effort, and when you do pause it is to think about ideas, not to search for basic words. Fillers such as *well*, *let me think* and *that's a good question* are natural in moderation, provided you do not repeat the same one in every answer. Repetition of *you know* or *actually* ten times in a minute is distracting.

Self-correction is also normal. If you say *I go there yesterday* and then fix it to *I went there yesterday*, that is a sign of awareness, not of failure. What matters is that the correction is quick and the story continues.

Finally, watch for answers that are too short. Two sentences are fine in Part 1, but one-word answers in Part 3 leave the examiner nothing to assess. If a question seems difficult, buy time with a phrase, then give a reason.

## Techniques that move you toward band 7

1. **Develop, don't recite.** Answer, give a reason, add an example.
2. **Use a mix of structures.** Combine simple sentences with *although*, *which*, *when* and conditional forms.
3. **Paraphrase when you forget a word.** *The thing you use to cook rice* is better than silence.
4. **Correct yourself calmly.** A quick self-correction is natural and does not hurt your score.
5. **Avoid memorised answers.** Examiners are trained to recognise them. See [why memorising sample answers ruins IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need advanced vocabulary for IELTS Speaking band 7?

No. Accurate, natural vocabulary on the topic matters more than rare words. A few well-chosen collocations and good paraphrase are enough.

### How long should Part 2 last?

Aim for the full two minutes. If you stop much earlier, the examiner may ask you a question, but you will have lost the chance to show your range.

### Can I give false information in the test?

Yes. The test assesses your English, not your life. If you cannot think of a real memory, invent a believable one and describe it clearly.
