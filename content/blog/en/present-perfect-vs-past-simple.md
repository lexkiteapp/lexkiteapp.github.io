The difference between **present perfect vs past simple** comes down to one question: is the time frame you are talking about still open, or is it finished? Use the past simple (*I visited Rome in 2019*) for actions inside a closed, finished period. Use the present perfect (*I have visited Rome twice*) when the period connects to now, such as "in my life so far", "this week" or "since January". The action itself may be over in both cases; what changes is the window of time you are looking through.

## Open time frames and closed time frames

Picture two windows. The first is closed and sits entirely in the past: *yesterday*, *last summer*, *in 2015*, *when I was a child*. Anything you place in that window takes the **past simple**.

The second window is still open; its right-hand edge is the present moment: *today* (while it is still today), *this year*, *ever*, *so far*, *since I moved here*. Events placed in that window take the **present perfect**.

{{diagram: d1 | The closed window ends before now; the open window reaches the present moment}}

Compare:

- *I **wrote** three emails this morning.* (said in the afternoon: the morning is over)
- *I'**ve written** three emails this morning.* (said at 11 a.m.: the morning is still going on)

Both sentences describe completed emails. The tense tells the listener whether the speaker sees the morning as finished or ongoing. That is why the rule "past simple for finished actions" is incomplete: the present perfect also describes finished actions, just inside an unfinished period.

## Why learners mix them up

The difficulty has different roots depending on your first language.

- **Languages that do not change the verb for time**, such as Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai or Indonesian, often use a separate word to show that something is already done. Learners may map that word onto *have done* and use the present perfect for every completed action: *I have gone to the market yesterday.*
- **Languages with a perfect-looking past**, such as French (*j'ai mangé*), German (*ich habe gegessen*) or Italian (*ho mangiato*), use "have + past participle" to tell finished stories in everyday speech. Speakers transfer the form directly: *I have seen him last week.*
- **Learners taught signal-word lists** often believe that *already*, *just* or *yet* always force the present perfect, which is not true in American English (see below).

In every case, the fix is the same: stop asking "is the action finished?" and start asking "is the time frame finished?"

## Four core uses of the present perfect

{{diagram: d2 | Every present perfect sentence is built from have or has plus the past participle}}

### 1. Experience up to now

The time frame is your life so far, and the exact time is not important or not known.

- *She **has lived** in three different countries.*
- *Have you ever **tried** durian?*

Add a specific past time and the frame closes: *She **lived** in Canada in 2018.*

### 2. A past action with a present result

The action happened earlier, but you are interested in its consequence now.

- *I'**ve lost** my keys.* (I can't get into the house now.)
- *Someone **has parked** in my space.* (The car is still there.)

If the result no longer matters, English speakers switch to the past simple: *I **lost** my keys last month, but I found them later.*

### 3. A situation that started in the past and continues

With state verbs such as *be*, *know*, *have* (possession) and *live*, the present perfect plus *for* or *since* shows duration up to now.

- *We **have known** each other since primary school.*
- *He **has worked** here for six years.* (He still works here.)

Compare: *He **worked** here for six years.* (He no longer works here; the six years are a closed block.)

### 4. New information and recent news

When announcing something for the first time, English often uses the present perfect, then switches to the past simple for the details.

- *The company **has opened** a new office in Lagos. It **opened** on Monday with about forty staff.*

This pattern, present perfect for the headline and past simple for the story, appears constantly in news reports and in everyday conversation.

## Time expressions: clues, not formulas

Time expressions help, but they do not choose the tense for you. The table shows typical partners.

| Usually past simple | Usually present perfect | Can go with either |
|---|---|---|
| yesterday, last week, in 2010 | ever, never (life so far), so far | today, this week, this year |
| ago, when I was…, at 6 p.m. | since + point in time | already, just, yet (see note) |
| What time…? When…? | lately, in recent years | for + period, recently |

The middle column contains the open frames; the left column, closed ones. The right column depends on whether the speaker sees the period as finished: *I didn't see him this week* (the week has ended, e.g. said on Saturday) versus *I haven't seen him this week* (the week continues).

**Note on American English:** in informal American speech, *already*, *just* and *yet* often appear with the past simple: *Did you eat yet?* *I just finished.* British English prefers the present perfect here. For IELTS, either variety is acceptable as long as you are consistent; for formal writing, the present perfect is the safer choice.

**A critical rule:** *when* questions and expressions with *ago* always close the frame. *When have you arrived?* and *I have arrived two hours ago* are wrong. Use *When did you arrive?* and *I arrived two hours ago.*

## Present perfect vs past simple: right and wrong pairs

| ✗ Incorrect | ✓ Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I have met her last Friday. | I met her last Friday. | *Last Friday* is a closed frame. |
| I live here since 2020. | I have lived here since 2020. | Duration up to now needs the present perfect. |
| Did you ever go to Japan? (BrE context) | Have you ever been to Japan? | Life experience, open frame. |
| She has finished university in 2022. | She finished university in 2022. | A specific past year. |
| I didn't finish my report yet. (formal BrE) | I haven't finished my report yet. | *Yet* with an open frame (BrE). |
| How long are you married? | How long have you been married? | Duration up to now. |

Notice the *been* versus *gone* distinction too: *She has **been** to Paris* (and returned) contrasts with *She has **gone** to Paris* (she is there now).

## Three patterns that still catch advanced learners

**"It's the first time…"** This frame always takes the present perfect, because you are counting experiences up to now: *It's the first time I've **eaten** sushi* (not *I eat* or *I ate*). Shift the whole sentence into the past and the tense shifts with it: *It was the first time I'd **eaten** sushi.*

**Present perfect simple vs continuous.** With activity verbs, the simple form counts results and the continuous form stresses the activity itself: *I've **written** three emails* (a finished count) versus *I've **been writing** emails all morning* (the activity, possibly unfinished, which may explain why you look tired). With *for* and *since*, many activity verbs work in either form (*I've worked / I've been working here since May*), but state verbs such as *know* and *own* stay simple: *I've known her for years*, never *I've been knowing her*.

**A dead person or a finished era.** The frame closes when the subject's "window" closes. *Einstein **won** the Nobel Prize* (his life is over), but *Our team **has won** three awards* (the team still exists and could win more).

## A quick decision checklist

When you hesitate, run through these questions in order:

1. **Is there a finished time expression** (*yesterday*, *in 2019*, *ago*, *when I was…*)? → **Past simple**.
2. **Is the question "When…?"** → **Past simple**.
3. **Does the situation continue now** with *for* or *since*? → **Present perfect**.
4. **Is it life experience** with no specific time? → **Present perfect**.
5. **Is the present result the point**, or are you announcing news? → **Present perfect**.
6. **Are you telling a story** with a sequence of events? → **Past simple** after the first sentence.

Most real sentences are settled by question 1 or 2.

## Using the two tenses in exams

In **IELTS Writing Task 1**, graphs with past years need the past simple: *Sales rose sharply between 2005 and 2010.* The present perfect is wrong there because the years form a closed frame. Our [IELTS line graph](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) guide shows the tense choices in a full model answer.

![Lexkite Writing Task 1 Academic page with line, bar and pie chart tasks, a four-paragraph structure and trend vocabulary](media:task1 "Charts with finished years call for the past simple: sales rose, not have risen, between 2005 and 2010")

In **IELTS Writing Task 2** and **Speaking**, the present perfect is natural for trends that continue to now: *Remote work has become far more common in recent years.* In a **Speaking Part 2** story you will move between both: *I've always enjoyed cooking, but the first time I really cooked for guests was when I was sixteen.* See our [IELTS Speaking Part 2](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-tips/) guide for story structure.

In **TOEIC Part 5**, look for the time clue first. *Since*, *for* and *so far* usually point to the present perfect; *last*, *ago* and a specific date point to the past simple. The [TOEIC test format](/en/blog/toeic-test-format/) guide explains where these grammar questions appear.

## Self-check

Choose the correct form.

1. I (lost / have lost) my phone, so I can't call you.
2. They (moved / have moved) to Dubai three years ago.
3. How long (did you know / have you known) your best friend?
4. (Did you see / Have you seen) the news? A storm is coming.
5. Shakespeare (wrote / has written) many plays.
6. We (didn't have / haven't had) any rain so far this month.
7. When (did you start / have you started) learning English?
8. She (worked / has worked) at the hospital for ten years, then retired.

### Answers and explanations

1. **have lost** – the present result (no phone now) is the point.
2. **moved** – *ago* closes the frame.
3. **have you known** – a relationship that continues to now.
4. **Have you seen** – news, and the frame is "recently, up to now".
5. **wrote** – Shakespeare's life is a closed frame; he cannot write more plays.
6. **haven't had** – *so far this month* is open.
7. **did you start** – a *when* question asks for a closed point in time.
8. **worked** – the ten years are a finished block that ended with her retirement.

If you scored 7 or 8, you have the concept. If not, reread the section on open and closed frames, then retry tomorrow without looking; delayed retesting is what makes rules stick, as explained in [active recall for vocabulary](/en/blog/active-recall-for-vocabulary/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the present perfect always used with "already"?
In British English it usually is: *I've already eaten.* In informal American English, *I already ate* is common and acceptable. Choose one variety and use it consistently, especially in exams.

### Does "for" always mean present perfect?
No. *For* shows duration, and the frame decides the tense. *I've lived here for five years* (still here) uses the present perfect; *I lived there for five years* (moved away) uses the past simple.

### Is "Did you eat yet?" wrong?
Not in American English, where it is common in informal speech. In British English, *Have you eaten yet?* is the standard form. Both are understood everywhere.

### Will using the present perfect improve my IELTS grammar score?
Using a tense correctly and naturally is what counts, not using it often. Forcing the present perfect into sentences with past dates creates errors that lower Grammatical Range and Accuracy.
