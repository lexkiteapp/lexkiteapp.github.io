Most students will write an **IELTS opinion essay** at some point in Writing Task 2. It is the question that begins with *"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"* or *"Do you think the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"* The task looks simple, yet many candidates lose marks because they never really answer the question, mix up their position or fill paragraphs with general statements. This guide gives you a clear plan, useful phrases and a self-check you can reuse for every essay.

## What an opinion essay asks you to do

In an opinion essay you must **state your view and defend it** with reasons and examples. The Task 2 instructions usually ask for at least 250 words, and many candidates aim for 260 to 300. You have about 40 minutes of the 60-minute Writing test for this essay, because Task 2 carries more weight than Task 1.

Typical prompts:

- *To what extent do you agree or disagree?*
- *Do you agree or disagree with this statement?*
- *Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?*
- *Is this a positive or negative development?*

The examiner marks four criteria: **Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range and Accuracy**. For an opinion essay, Task Response is about having a clear position throughout and fully developed arguments. Check the official IELTS website for the current band descriptors; for more about the whole task see our guide to [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/).

## IELTS opinion essay structure

{{diagram: d1 | Four short paragraphs are enough for a strong band 7 essay. Quality of ideas matters more than length.}}

### 1. Introduction (2–3 sentences)

- **Paraphrase** the topic in one sentence.
- Give your **clear opinion**.
- Optional: a brief outline of your two reasons.

Example question (our own): *Some people say that cities should ban cars from the centre. To what extent do you agree?*

*It is often argued that private vehicles should be removed from city centres. I strongly agree with this view, because it would improve air quality and make streets safer and more pleasant for residents.*

Notice that the opinion is in the second sentence. There is no *"In this essay I will discuss both sides"*, which would be wrong for an opinion essay.

### 2. Body paragraph 1: your first reason

Use a simple pattern: **point, explain, example**.

*Firstly, banning cars would reduce air pollution. Exhaust fumes are a major source of harmful particles in dense areas, which can cause breathing problems, especially for children and older people. For example, when some European cities closed central streets to traffic for a day, residents reported cleaner air and less noise.*

You do not need real statistics. A reasonable, general example is enough; do not invent exact numbers or sources.

### 3. Body paragraph 2: your second reason

*Secondly, car-free centres would be safer and more attractive. Without heavy traffic, pedestrians and cyclists can move freely, and shops and cafés can use the space for outdoor seating. As a result, local businesses may even benefit because people stay longer.*

### 4. Conclusion (1–2 sentences)

Restate your position with different words and add a final thought: *In conclusion, I believe that restricting cars in city centres brings clear health and social advantages, so governments should support the idea.* Do not add new arguments here.

## Choosing and holding your position

The most common error in an opinion essay is an **unclear position**. Examiners must be able to say after the introduction what you think. If you partly agree, say so clearly, then keep that exact position in the essay.

{{diagram: d2 | The right-hand column tells the examiner exactly what you think.}}

Three safe positions:

1. **Strongly agree or disagree:** easiest to organise: two reasons for one side.
2. **Partly agree:** explain what you accept and what you do not, but stay consistent.
3. **Balanced with a final view:** present both sides, then clearly choose one.

Do not change sides between paragraphs. Do not write *"I think both are true"* and stop.

## Useful phrases for an opinion essay

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Giving your opinion | *I strongly believe that…*, *In my view,…*, *I am convinced that…* |
| Partial agreement | *I agree to some extent, but…*, *Although I accept that…, I still think…* |
| Adding reasons | *Firstly… Furthermore… In addition…* |
| Giving examples | *For instance,…*, *A good example of this is…* |
| Concession | *Admittedly,…*, *It is true that…, however,…* |
| Conclusion | *In conclusion,…*, *Overall,…*, *To sum up,…* |

Do not use the same linking word in every paragraph. Our guides to [linking words for IELTS Writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/) and [complex sentences](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/) show how to vary your language. For vocabulary choices see [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) and the [lexical resource guide](/en/blog/lexical-resource-ielts/).

## Common mistakes

1. **No position.** The essay lists points but never says "I agree".
2. **Memorised introductions.** Examiners recognise templates; make the paraphrase fit the actual question. Read [why memorising sample answers hurts](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/).
3. **Vague examples.** *"For example, many people like it"* is not an example. Be specific, even if it is hypothetical.
4. **Too many ideas.** Two well-developed reasons beat four short ones.
5. **Overlong introductions and conclusions.** Spend your time on the body.
6. **Informal language.** Avoid *"kids"*, *"a lot of"* repeated, contractions and exclamation marks.
7. **Not checking the time.** Plan for five minutes, write for thirty and check for five.

## A short note on timing and length

Many candidates run out of time because they spend too long on the introduction or rewrite sentences repeatedly. Write the introduction in under three minutes, give each body paragraph about ten minutes and keep the last five minutes for checking. When you check, look for three things only: a clear position in the first paragraph, one example in each body paragraph, and small errors such as missing articles, plural forms and verb agreement. If you are short on time, a complete essay with a simple conclusion is better than a brilliant body with no ending.

## Plan in five minutes

Use this plan before writing:

1. Underline the topic and the instruction words.
2. Write your position in one sentence.
3. List two reasons with one example each.
4. Note one possible objection and your answer (optional, for body paragraph 2).
5. Start writing.

## Self-check

1. Write a one-sentence position for: *Remote work is better than office work.*
2. Which sentence is a better introduction: "I will discuss both sides" or "I strongly disagree"?
3. Complete with a concession word: *____ online classes are cheaper, they lack personal contact.*
4. Where should new arguments appear?
5. Rewrite the vague example: *"Many people like parks."*

### Answers

1. For example: *I strongly agree that remote work is better, because it saves time and improves focus.*
2. **I strongly disagree**, because it states a position (the other one is for discussion essays).
3. **Although** online classes are cheaper, they lack personal contact. (*Admittedly* also works with a comma and *but*.)
4. **Only in the body paragraphs**, not in the conclusion.
5. For example: *City parks give workers a place to relax at lunch, which can reduce stress.*

## Frequently asked questions

### How many words should an IELTS opinion essay have?

At least 250. Aim for 260 to 300 and do not count every word; focus on ideas.

### Can I use "I" in an opinion essay?

Yes, in an opinion essay it is normal: *I believe*, *In my view*. Avoid overusing it.

### Do I need to include both sides?

Not unless the question asks. A strong one-sided essay with a short concession is fine.

### Can I use personal examples?

Yes, but general or hypothetical examples often work better. Keep them relevant and brief.
