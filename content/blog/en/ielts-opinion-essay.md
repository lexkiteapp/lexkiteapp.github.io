An **IELTS opinion essay** asks one thing: do you agree or disagree with a statement, and how well can you defend your view? The question usually ends with *To what extent do you agree or disagree?* or *Do you agree or disagree?* There is no right answer. Examiners reward a clear position, ideas that are explained and supported, and an essay that stays on that position from the first sentence to the last. This guide shows three ways to answer, a model essay with notes, and the mistakes that blur an otherwise good argument.

## What the question is really asking

An agree/disagree prompt contains a statement, often with a strong word such as *should*, *always*, *must* or *only*. Underline it. Your job is to decide how far you accept the statement, and to say so clearly. If the instruction says *to what extent*, a partly agree position is allowed, but it must still be specific: say where you agree and where you do not.

Opinion questions are one of five common Task 2 types, described in [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/). The main difference from a discussion question is that you do not need to give both views equal space; you need to defend yours.

## Three ways to answer

{{diagram: d1 | Choose one position and keep it from the introduction to the conclusion}}

**Fully agree or fully disagree** is the simplest and often the clearest. You give two strong reasons. It works when you have two ideas that you can develop with examples.

**Agree or disagree with limits** is more flexible. It suits statements with absolute words. Original statement: *Public transport should be free for everyone.* You could argue that free transport is a good idea for students and the elderly, but that making it free for everyone would strain city budgets. This is a legitimate position as long as the essay explains the line you draw.

**Agree with a condition.** *I agree, provided that...* gives you a built-in structure: one body paragraph on why the idea is good, another on the condition that makes it work.

Whichever you choose, state it in the introduction and again in the conclusion, and let both body paragraphs support it. A position that drifts is a common way to lose marks.

## Plan before you write

{{diagram: d2 | Five minutes of planning saves more time than it costs}}

Write your plan on the question paper. Two reasons with one example each is enough for 260 to 290 words. If you cannot think of an example, use a general one: a type of city, a type of job, a common experience. Examples do not need to be famous or statistical; they need to be believable and relevant.

## A model IELTS opinion essay

*Question: Some people think that public transport should be free for everyone in order to reduce traffic in cities. To what extent do you agree or disagree?*

> Free public transport is often proposed as a way to ease congestion. I agree that lower fares can reduce traffic, but I believe that completely free travel is not the best solution, because it could weaken the quality of the service and place an unfair burden on taxpayers.
>
> The strongest argument in favour of cheaper transport is that it encourages people to leave their cars at home. Commuters often compare the cost of a bus ticket with the cost of fuel and parking, so when fares fall, more of them switch. A student who pays much less for a monthly pass, for example, may stop driving to campus altogether. Fewer cars on the road also means cleaner air and shorter journeys for those who must still drive, such as delivery workers. Therefore, reducing the price clearly has the potential to cut traffic and pollution.
>
> However, making travel entirely free would create problems. Transport systems depend on fares to pay for drivers, maintenance and new routes. If that income disappears, the money must come from taxes, which means that people who never use the buses still pay for them. In addition, when a service costs nothing, some passengers may use it for very short trips that they could have walked, making vehicles crowded at the busiest times and reducing comfort for those who really need to travel.
>
> In conclusion, although lower prices can help to reduce traffic, I do not agree that transport should be free for all, since quality and fairness would suffer. A better approach is to offer discounts to groups who need them most while investing in frequent and reliable services.

The essay is about 270 words.

## How the essay answers the question

**Introduction.** The first sentence introduces the topic without copying the question. The second gives a position that agrees with lower fares but not with free travel, and names two reasons for the limit. The reader knows exactly what will follow.

**Body 1.** The topic sentence presents the best argument for the other side, which shows balance. The paragraph gives an explanation (comparing ticket price with fuel and parking), an example and a result.

**Body 2.** *However* signals the turn. It explains two problems and uses a consequence (*which means that*). Each idea has an explanation, so the paragraph does more than list.

**Conclusion.** It restates the position and offers a practical idea without introducing a new argument.

## Useful language for opinions

| Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|
| State a position | *I strongly believe that..., In my view..., I agree to some extent* |
| Give a reason | *This is because..., One reason is that..., Mainly because...* |
| Add an example | *For instance..., A good illustration is..., Consider...* |
| Concede | *Admittedly..., It is true that..., Although some argue...* |
| Conclude | *In conclusion..., On balance..., Overall...* |

Use these sparingly and vary them. For more connectors, see [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/).

## Mistakes that blur your position

1. **Changing sides.** You agree in the introduction and argue the opposite in the second body paragraph.
2. **Saying "both sides" without choosing.** An opinion question needs a view.
3. **Writing reasons without explanation.** *Cars cause pollution* is a claim; *cars cause pollution because exhaust fumes stay close to the ground in crowded streets* is an explanation.
4. **Copying the question word for word.** Paraphrase it; the guide to [paraphrasing in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) shows how.
5. **Memorised phrases.** Template sentences unrelated to the topic read as unnatural. See [why memorising answers hurts](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/) for the same problem in Speaking.
6. **Over-long introductions.** Two sentences are enough.

Check your own writing against [IELTS writing mistakes that cost marks](/en/blog/ielts-writing-mistakes/) before you submit.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I choose to partly agree in an IELTS opinion essay?

Yes, if the question allows it, for example with *to what extent*. State precisely what you agree with and what you do not, and keep that position throughout.

### How many reasons do I need?

Two well-developed reasons are usually better than three thin ones. Each should have an explanation and, ideally, an example.

### Should I write in the first person?

Yes. Phrases such as *I believe* or *in my opinion* are acceptable in an opinion essay, but you do not need them in every sentence.

### Do I need to give real statistics?

No. Examiners do not check facts, and invented statistics can sound unnatural. Use believable examples and clear reasoning instead.
