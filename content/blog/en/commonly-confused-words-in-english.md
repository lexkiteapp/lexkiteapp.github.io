**Commonly confused words** are pairs that look alike, sound alike, share a root or translate into the same word in your language, yet are used differently in English. The fastest way to master them is not a long alphabetical list but grouping them by *why* they are confusing, then learning one clear test for each group: spelling, word family, grammar pattern or meaning. Below are 30 pairs organised that way, each with a short rule, an original example and, where it helps, a memory trick.

## Why commonly confused words trip learners up

Most confusion has one of four causes:

1. **Similar spelling or sound.** *Affect* and *effect* differ by one letter and are pronounced almost identically in fast speech.
2. **Same root, different suffix.** *Economic* and *economical* both come from *economy* but mean different things.
3. **Different grammar.** *Rise* and *raise* share a meaning, but one takes an object and one does not.
4. **One translation, two English words.** Many languages use a single verb for *make* and *do*, or for *say* and *tell*, so learners pick at random.

Knowing the cause tells you what to check. For group 1, check spelling; for group 2, check the precise meaning; for group 3, check the sentence pattern; for group 4, check collocations.

## Group 1: similar spelling or sound (12 pairs)

| Pair | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| affect / effect | *affect* = verb (to influence); *effect* = noun (a result) | Noise affects sleep. It has a strong effect on mood. |
| accept / except | *accept* = agree to receive; *except* = not including | She accepted every offer except one. |
| advice / advise | *advice* = noun /s/; *advise* = verb /z/ | I advise you to take her advice. |
| practice / practise | BrE: *practice* noun, *practise* verb; AmE: *practice* for both | Practise daily; practice makes progress. |
| lose / loose | *lose* = fail to keep; *loose* = not tight | Don't lose that loose button. |
| quiet / quite | *quiet* = low noise; *quite* = fairly | The library was quite quiet today. |
| desert / dessert | *desert* = dry land; *dessert* = sweet course | We ate dessert after crossing the desert. |
| stationary / stationery | *stationary* = not moving; *stationery* = paper, pens | The truck was stationary outside the stationery shop. |
| principal / principle | *principal* = main / head of a school; *principle* = rule or belief | Honesty is her principal principle. |
| complement / compliment | *complement* = complete or match; *compliment* = praise | The sauce complements the fish; the chef got a compliment. |
| weather / whether | *weather* = sun, rain; *whether* = if | Check whether the weather will change. |
| then / than | *then* = after that; *than* = comparison | Finish the essay, then compare it with others. It's longer than mine. |

**Memory tricks.** *Stationery* has an **e** like **e**nvelope. *Dessert* has two **s**'s because you always want a **s**econd **s**erving. *Affect* is the **a**ction (verb), *effect* is the **e**nd result. *Desert* and *dessert* also differ in stress (see the table below). Our guide to [word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) explains why stress changes like this matter.

**Hear the difference.** Several Group 1 pairs are easier to keep apart once you know they do not sound the same:

| Pair | Pronunciation | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| lose / loose | /luːz/ vs /luːs/ | voiced /z/ vs voiceless /s/ |
| advise / advice | /ədˈvaɪz/ vs /ədˈvaɪs/ | verb ends in /z/, noun in /s/ |
| quite / quiet | /kwaɪt/ vs /ˈkwaɪət/ | one syllable vs two |
| desert / dessert | /ˈdezət/ vs /dɪˈzɜːt/ | stress on the first vs second syllable |
| affect / effect | /əˈfekt/ vs /ɪˈfekt/ | only the weak first vowel differs, so rely on grammar |

One trap inside the trap: *desert* as a verb ("to abandon") is pronounced exactly like *dessert*, /dɪˈzɜːt/. US speakers say /ˈdezərt/ and /dɪˈzɝːt/, with the /r/ pronounced.

**Apostrophe traps (a bonus three).** Native speakers misspell these more than learners do, and they are the first thing an examiner or employer notices: *it's* = it is / it has, *its* = belonging to it (*It's clear the company has lost its way*); *you're* = you are, *your* = belonging to you; *they're* = they are, *their* = belonging to them, *there* = a place or "there is". Test: if you can expand the word to two words, it needs the apostrophe.

**A note on *effect*:** it can be a verb in formal English meaning "to bring about": *The new director effected major changes.* In most writing, though, *affect* is the verb you need.

## Group 2: same root, different meaning (6 pairs)

- **economic / economical.** *Economic* relates to the economy: *economic growth*. *Economical* means not wasteful: *an economical car*.
- **historic / historical.** *Historic* means important in history: *a historic agreement*. *Historical* means relating to the past: *historical records*.
- **sensible / sensitive.** *Sensible* means practical and reasonable: *a sensible plan*. *Sensitive* means easily affected or aware of feelings: *sensitive skin, a sensitive topic*. Speakers of Spanish, French and Portuguese often misuse *sensible*, because a similar-looking word in their language means "sensitive".
- **classic / classical.** *Classic* means typical or excellent of its kind: *a classic mistake*. *Classical* refers to ancient Greece and Rome or to a style of music: *classical architecture*.
- **continual / continuous.** *Continual* means repeated with breaks, often annoyingly: *continual interruptions*. *Continuous* means without a break: *continuous rain for six hours*.
- **beside / besides.** *Beside* means next to: *Sit beside me*. *Besides* means in addition or apart from: *Besides English, she speaks Swahili*.

In academic writing, the *economic/economical* and *historic/historical* pairs are worth extra care, because the wrong choice changes your meaning rather than just your style.

## Group 3: same idea, different grammar (6 pairs)

| Pair | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| rise / raise | *rise* (no object): rise – rose – risen; *raise* + object: raise – raised – raised | Prices rose. The shop raised its prices. |
| lie / lay | *lie* (no object): lie – lay – lain; *lay* + object: lay – laid – laid | I lay on the sofa. She laid the baby in the cot. |
| fewer / less | *fewer* + plural countable; *less* + uncountable | fewer mistakes, less time |
| borrow / lend | *borrow* (from someone); *lend* (to someone) | Can I borrow your pen? Can you lend me your pen? |
| say / tell | *say* (something) *to* someone; *tell* someone (something) | She said hello to me. She told me a secret. |
| remind / remember | *remind* someone (to do / of something); *remember* (by yourself) | Remind me to call Dad. I remember the call. |

*Rise* and *raise* matter most in IELTS Writing Task 1, where trends are described constantly; see the [IELTS line graph](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) guide for the full vocabulary set. *Lie* and *lay* are tricky partly because *lay* is also the past tense of *lie*. If tense choices themselves give you trouble, read [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/). *Remember* also changes meaning with *to* versus *-ing*, which is covered in [gerunds and infinitives](/en/blog/gerunds-and-infinitives/).

Strictly, *less* with plural nouns (*less people*) is common in casual speech, but in exams and formal writing, *fewer people* is the expected form.

## Group 4: one translation, two English words (6 pairs)

- **make / do.** *Make* is usually about creating or producing: *make a decision, make a mistake, make progress*. *Do* is about tasks and activities: *do homework, do research, do business*. These are fixed collocations, so learn them as chunks; see [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/).
- **job / work.** *Job* is countable (a position): *She has a new job*. *Work* is usually uncountable (activity or place): *I have a lot of work. I'm at work.* Not ~~*a work*~~ in this sense.
- **trip / travel.** *Trip* is a countable journey plus the stay: *a business trip*. *Travel* is mainly a verb or an uncountable noun: *Travel broadens the mind*. Not ~~*a travel*~~.
- **interested / interesting.** *-ed* describes how a person feels; *-ing* describes the thing that causes the feeling. *I'm interested in history because it's interesting.* The same applies to *bored/boring*, *tired/tiring*, *confused/confusing*.
- **fun / funny.** *Fun* means enjoyable: *The party was fun*. *Funny* means it makes you laugh: *a funny story*.
- **between / among.** *Between* for clearly separate items, often two: *between the bank and the café*. *Among* for a group seen as a whole: *popular among teenagers*.

## Quick reference: what to check

| Cause of confusion | What to check | Pairs |
|---|---|---|
| Spelling or sound | Part of speech, spelling trick | affect/effect, lose/loose, stationary/stationery |
| Same root | Exact meaning in a dictionary | economic/economical, sensible/sensitive |
| Grammar | Object? Countable? Who receives? | rise/raise, fewer/less, borrow/lend |
| One translation | Collocation and countability | make/do, job/work, trip/travel |

## Self-check

Choose the correct word.

1. The new law will (affect / effect) small businesses.
2. Unemployment (rose / raised) by 2% last year.
3. Could you (lend / borrow) me your charger?
4. A smaller car is more (economic / economical) to run.
5. There are (fewer / less) students in the class this term.
6. I need some (advice / advise) about my visa.
7. We need to (make / do) a decision today.
8. The film was so (bored / boring) that I left early.

### Answers and explanations

1. **affect** – a verb is needed after *will*.
2. **rose** – no object follows; *rise* is intransitive.
3. **lend** – the speaker receives, so the other person lends.
4. **economical** – meaning cheap to run, not related to the economy.
5. **fewer** – *students* is a plural countable noun.
6. **advice** – a noun is needed after *some*.
7. **make** – *make a decision* is a fixed collocation.
8. **boring** – the film causes the feeling.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I learn confusing pairs from a list or in context?
Use both. A list helps you notice the difference once; meeting the words in sentences and testing yourself later is what makes the choice automatic. Write one sentence of your own for each pair.

### Is "effect" ever a verb?
Yes, in formal English *effect* can mean "to bring about": *to effect change*. It is much less common than *affect* as a verb, so if you mean "influence", use *affect*.

### Why do "rise" and "raise" appear so often in IELTS Writing Task 1?
Because Task 1 describes changes in data, and both verbs describe increases. *Figures rose* (no object) and *the company raised salaries* (with an object) both appear naturally, so mixing them up is a common and visible error.

### How can I remember "stationary" and "stationery"?
Link the **e** in *stationery* to **e**nvelope, which is a kind of stationery. The **a** in *stationary* can remind you of a car that is st**a**nding still.
