The most reliable **IELTS Writing Task 2 structure** is four paragraphs: an introduction that paraphrases the question (see [how to paraphrase in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/)) and gives your position, two body paragraphs that each develop one main idea, and a short conclusion that restates your answer. That frame fits all five common question types; what changes is the job each paragraph does. Below you will find the frame paragraph by paragraph, a five-minute planning routine, a worked plan for an original question and the structural mistakes that quietly pull scores down.

## Why structure matters so much in Task 2

Task 2 asks for at least 250 words in about 40 minutes, and it carries twice the weight of Task 1 in your Writing score. Examiners use four criteria: Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Structure feeds directly into the first two. A clear position that runs from the introduction to the conclusion is part of Task Response; logical paragraphing, with one central idea per paragraph, is part of Coherence and Cohesion.

Put simply, a good structure will not turn weak English into strong English, but a weak structure can hide good English. Plenty of candidates write accurate sentences and still lose marks because the reader cannot tell what they think, or why one paragraph follows another.

## The five Task 2 question types

Before you plan anything, identify the type. The final sentence of the prompt usually tells you.

| Type | Typical instruction | What your essay must do |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion (agree/disagree) | To what extent do you agree or disagree? | Take a clear position and keep it throughout |
| Discussion | Discuss both views and give your own opinion. | Explain each view fairly, then say which you support |
| Advantages/disadvantages | Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages? | Weigh the two sides and give a verdict |
| Problem–solution | What problems does this cause, and how can they be solved? | Answer both questions with matching ideas |
| Two-part (direct questions) | Why is this happening? Is it a positive development? | Answer each question directly |

The most common structural error is answering a different question from the one printed. "Discuss both views" without your own opinion leaves the task half done. "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" answered with "there are advantages and disadvantages" never delivers the verdict that was requested. Similarly, avoid common [IELTS writing vocabulary traps](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) that sacrifice argument clarity for pretentious phrasing.

## The 4-paragraph IELTS Writing Task 2 structure

Here is the frame, with a guideline length for each part. Totals of roughly 260–300 words are comfortable: long enough to develop ideas, short enough to leave time for checking.

| Paragraph | Purpose | Approx. words |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Paraphrase the topic + state your position or outline | 40–55 |
| Body 1 | Main idea 1: point, explanation, example | 90–110 |
| Body 2 | Main idea 2 (or the other side): point, explanation, example | 90–110 |
| Conclusion | Restate the position and summarise the two reasons | 30–45 |

### Introduction: two jobs only

The introduction has two jobs: tell the reader what the essay is about, in your own words, and tell them what you will argue. One sentence for each is enough. Background sentences about "the modern world" use up time and add nothing an examiner can reward.

Original question: *Some people think that cities should ban private cars from their centres. To what extent do you agree or disagree?*

Introduction: *A number of people argue that private vehicles should be excluded from city centres altogether. I largely agree with this view, because a car-free centre is safer and cleaner, although some exceptions are necessary for disabled drivers and deliveries.*

Notice that the second sentence already contains the plan for the whole essay: two reasons and one concession.

### Body paragraphs: point, explain, example

Each body paragraph should develop one main idea using a simple sequence:

1. **Point** – a topic sentence that states the idea and links it to the question.
2. **Explain** – one or two sentences on *why* or *how* this is true.
3. **Example** – a specific, realistic illustration (it does not need to be a real statistic).
4. **Link back** (optional) – a short sentence showing how this supports your position.

Example (Body 1):

*The strongest argument for a ban is safety. When cars share narrow streets with pedestrians and cyclists, a single moment of driver inattention can cause serious injury. In a pedestrian-only centre, children can walk to school and older residents can cross roads without waiting for gaps in traffic. Removing private cars would therefore make the centre safer for exactly the people who use it most on foot.*

That is about 70 words. Notice that every sentence serves the topic sentence (safety); air quality waits for Body 2. A second explaining sentence or a more detailed example takes it to the 90–100 range.

### Conclusion: answer again, add nothing new

The conclusion repeats your answer in fresh words and reminds the reader of the main reasons. New arguments, new examples or a sudden change of opinion at this point damage coherence. Two sentences are plenty:

*In conclusion, I believe banning private cars from city centres would bring clear benefits for safety and air quality. Provided that sensible exceptions are made, the advantages of such a policy far outweigh the inconvenience to drivers.*

### Adjusting the frame for each question type

- **Opinion:** both body paragraphs support your view, or Body 1 briefly concedes the opposing view and Body 2 explains why you still reject it.
- **Discussion:** Body 1 covers view A, Body 2 covers view B, and your opinion appears in the introduction, the conclusion and ideally in the paragraph you agree with.
- **Advantages/disadvantages:** Body 1 for advantages, Body 2 for disadvantages; the verdict goes in the introduction and conclusion.
- **Problem–solution:** Body 1 for problems (or causes), Body 2 for solutions that answer those problems one by one.
- **Two-part:** Body 1 answers question one, Body 2 answers question two.

## How to plan in five minutes

Five minutes of planning feels expensive when the clock is running, yet it usually saves more time than it costs, because you never stop mid-essay wondering what to say next.

1. **Minute 1 – underline.** Mark the topic, the specific angle and the instruction words. "Cities should ban private cars from their centres" is not the same as "cars are bad for cities".
2. **Minute 2 – decide.** Choose your position and confirm the question type. Write it as one line: *Mostly agree – safety, pollution; exception: disabled drivers, deliveries.*
3. **Minutes 3–4 – ideas.** For each body paragraph, note a point, a reason and one example in a few words each.
4. **Minute 5 – language.** Jot down two or three topic words you want to use accurately (*pedestrianised, emissions, congestion*) and one linking phrase per paragraph.

Then write. If the essay takes 30 minutes, you still have roughly five minutes to check articles, verb forms and plurals, which are the slips examiners notice fastest. If articles are a weak spot, our [a, an, the guide](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/) has a quick checking routine.

## When five paragraphs make sense

A five-paragraph essay (three body paragraphs) is not wrong, but it is rarely necessary. Three body paragraphs in 40 minutes often means three thin ideas instead of two well-developed ones, and development is what Task Response rewards. Use a third body paragraph only when the question clearly has three parts, or when a discussion essay benefits from one paragraph per view plus a separate paragraph for your own argument. Otherwise, depth beats breadth.

## Structural mistakes that cost marks

Teachers see these issues again and again, whatever the writer's first language:

- **Memorised openings.** Lines like "Nowadays, with the rapid development of society…" appear in countless scripts. Examiners recognise template language, and it shows nothing about your real ability.
- **No clear position.** "There are many opinions about this topic" is not a position. If the question asks what you think, say it in the first paragraph.
- **One giant paragraph, or eight tiny ones.** Paragraphing is part of Coherence and Cohesion. Four or five paragraphs separated by a blank line make your logic visible.
- **Listing instead of developing.** "Firstly, safety. Secondly, pollution. Thirdly, noise." with one sentence each is a list, not an argument. Two developed ideas are worth more.
- **Mechanical linking.** Starting every sentence with *Moreover* or *Furthermore* reads as forced. Our guide to [linking words for IELTS Writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/) shows lighter ways to connect ideas.
- **Overclaiming.** "Banning cars will completely solve pollution" invites doubt. Phrasing such as *is likely to reduce* sounds more academic; see [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/).
- **A new idea in the conclusion.** If it matters, it belongs in a body paragraph.

## Quick self-check

Identify the question type and the main structural problem in each case.

1. "Discuss both views and give your own opinion." The essay explains both views well but ends: "Both sides have valid points."
2. An opinion essay opens with 95 words of background about technology.
3. A problem–solution essay lists four problems and offers one solution in its final sentence.
4. Body 2 begins: "In conclusion, another reason is cost."
5. A two-part question; the essay answers only the first part.

**Answers**

1. Discussion – the writer's own opinion is missing, so part of the task is unanswered.
2. Opinion – the introduction is far too long and probably hides the position; cut it to two sentences.
3. Problem–solution – the solutions are underdeveloped; give Body 2 to solutions that match the problems.
4. Misused linker – "In conclusion" signals the end of the essay; write "A further reason…" instead.
5. Two-part – half the task is ignored, which limits Task Response however good the language is.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many words should a Task 2 essay be?
At least 250. Most strong essays land between about 260 and 300 words. Going far beyond that rarely helps, because longer essays leave less time for checking and tend to contain more errors.

### Do I have to write exactly four paragraphs?
No. Four paragraphs is a reliable default, not a rule. Five work well when the question has three parts. What matters is that each paragraph has one clear central idea and that the paragraphs follow a logical order.

### Is the Task 2 structure the same for General Training?
Yes. General Training Task 2 is also an essay of at least 250 words, assessed on the same four criteria, so the same four-paragraph frame works. Topics tend to be of more general interest, but the planning routine does not change.

### Should I write Task 1 or Task 2 first?
Either order is allowed. Because Task 2 counts for more, many candidates start with it so that it gets their freshest attention, then spend about 20 minutes on Task 1. Whichever you choose, watch the clock.

### Do my examples need real statistics?
No. Examples can be realistic situations from everyday life, work or study. Inventing precise figures that sound official is risky; a believable, specific scenario is more convincing.
