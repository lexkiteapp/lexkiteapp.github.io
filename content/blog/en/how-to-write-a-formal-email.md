Here is how to write a formal email in one sentence: state your purpose in the first line, give only the details the reader needs, make one clear and polite request, and close in a way that matches your greeting. Everything else, from the subject line to the sign-off, supports those four moves. A good formal email is short, specific and easy to act on.

This guide walks through each part with phrases at different levels of formality, explains the habits that confuse international readers, and gives three model emails you can adapt.

## The structure of a formal email

Most professional emails follow the same six parts:

1. **Subject line**
2. **Greeting**
3. **Opening line: your purpose**
4. **Body: the necessary details**
5. **Request or next step**
6. **Closing and signature**

### Subject line: specific and short

The subject line decides whether your email is opened today or next week. Make it specific enough that the reader knows what it is about and what is needed.

- Weak: *Question* / *Hello* / *Urgent!!!*
- Better: *Request for invoice copy – Order 4521*
- Better: *Interview availability – Data Analyst role*
- Better: *Reminder: budget figures due Thursday*

### Opening line: get to the point

Many learners start with long warm-ups: *I hope this email finds you well. My name is… I am writing this email to you because…*. One short courtesy line is fine; three are too many. Put the purpose in the first or second sentence.

- *I am writing to ask about the status of my application for the Data Analyst position.*
- *I'm contacting you regarding the delivery delay on Order 4521.*
- *Following our call on Monday, I'm sending the revised project timeline.*

If you have never contacted the person before, add one line about who you are: *I'm a final-year engineering student at…*, *I manage purchasing for…*.

## Greetings and closings by level of formality

Match your greeting and your closing. A very formal greeting with a casual closing, or the reverse, looks careless.

| Situation | Greeting | Closing |
|---|---|---|
| Very formal, name unknown | Dear Sir or Madam, / Dear Hiring Manager, | Yours faithfully, (UK) / Sincerely, |
| Formal, name known | Dear Ms Rahman, / Dear Dr Okafor, | Yours sincerely, (UK) / Sincerely, / Kind regards, |
| Semi-formal, colleague or client you know | Dear Carlos, / Hello Carlos, | Best regards, / Kind regards, |
| Friendly, internal | Hi Carlos, | Thanks, / Best, |

Notes:

- *Yours faithfully* (name unknown) and *Yours sincerely* (name known) are British conventions. American English generally uses *Sincerely* in both cases.
- Use titles with the surname, not the first name: *Dear Ms Rahman*, not *Dear Ms Aisha*. If you are unsure of someone's gender, use their full name: *Dear Alex Morgan*.
- *Ms* is the safe default for women; avoid guessing *Mrs* or *Miss*. British English writes titles without a full stop (*Ms Rahman, Dr Okafor*); American English adds one (*Ms. Rahman, Dr. Okafor*).
- *To whom it may concern* still appears in reference letters and very general notices, but in an email it sounds distant. If you can find a name or a team (*Dear Admissions Team*), use it.

### The last line before the sign-off

The sentence just above *Kind regards* tells the reader what happens next, so make it do a job:

- *I look forward to hearing from you.* (formal, neutral: you expect a reply)
- *Please let me know if you need anything else.* (offers help, invites a reply only if needed)
- *Thank you for your help with this.* (after a request that has been granted or is routine)
- *Thank you in advance* is common, but some readers find it presumptuous because it thanks them for something they have not agreed to. *Thank you for considering my request* is safer in a first contact.

## Polite requests: a scale of directness

In English, requests are usually softened, even when you have a right to ask. The more you are asking, or the less you know the person, the softer you go.

| Directness | Example |
|---|---|
| Direct (internal, routine) | Please send the report by Friday. |
| Polite | Could you send the report by Friday? |
| More polite | Would you be able to send the report by Friday? |
| Very polite | I would be grateful if you could send the report by Friday. |
| Tentative | I was wondering if it might be possible to receive the report by Friday. |

A frequent mistake is a request that sounds like an order: *Send me the documents.* or *You must reply today.* In many languages that directness is normal and polite in context; in English emails it often reads as rude. Adding *could you*, *please* and a reason (*so that we can confirm the booking*) changes the tone completely.

Equally, do not bury your request in so much politeness that it disappears. Make sure the reader can find the request and its deadline in one glance.

## How to write a formal email that international readers understand

English is used for business across the world, and every region has its own email habits. Some are perfectly normal locally but confuse or amuse readers elsewhere. If your emails go to an international audience, consider these alternatives:

| Regional habit | Possible problem | Internationally clearer |
|---|---|---|
| *Kindly do the needful.* | Vague: what exactly is needed? | *Could you please approve the invoice?* |
| *Please revert.* / *revert back* | *Revert* means "go back to a previous state" in many varieties | *Please reply* / *Please let me know.* |
| *Respected Sir,* | Sounds old-fashioned outside some regions | *Dear Mr Lee,* |
| *PFA* | Abbreviation unknown to many readers | *Please find attached…* / *I've attached…* |
| *Please be advised that…* | Stiff and indirect | *Please note that…* or just state the fact |
| *Waiting for your positive response.* | Can sound like pressure | *I look forward to hearing from you.* |

Other common errors:

- **Writing in capital letters** for emphasis. It reads as shouting. Use bold sparingly instead.
- **Very long paragraphs.** Keep paragraphs to two to four lines and use a short list for several questions.
- **Missing articles**: *Please send me invoice* → *Please send me **the** invoice.* Our guide to [articles a, an and the](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/) explains the rules.
- **Verb pattern errors** in fixed phrases: *I look forward to hear from you* → *to hearing*. See [gerunds and infinitives](/en/blog/gerunds-and-infinitives/) for why.
- **Confusing pairs**: *advice/advise, affect/effect, ensure/insure*. Check our list of [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

## Three model emails

### Email 1: Asking for information (formal, first contact)

> **Subject:** Enquiry about part-time MBA entry requirements
>
> Dear Admissions Team,
>
> I am writing to ask about the entry requirements for the part-time MBA programme starting in September.
>
> I have a bachelor's degree in commerce and four years of experience in retail management. Could you please confirm whether applicants without a GMAT score are considered, and whether an IELTS certificate is required for graduates of English-medium universities?
>
> I would also be grateful if you could tell me the deadline for the September intake.
>
> Thank you for your time. I look forward to hearing from you.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> Daniela Ruiz

### Email 2: A reminder (semi-formal, colleague)

> **Subject:** Reminder: Q3 sales figures due Thursday
>
> Hi Tomasz,
>
> Just a quick reminder that the Q3 sales figures for the northern region are due this Thursday, so that we can include them in the board report.
>
> If you need more time or any data from my side, please let me know by Wednesday morning and I'll adjust the schedule.
>
> Thanks,
> Mei

### Email 3: Apologising for a delay (semi-formal, client)

> **Subject:** Update on your order 4521 – new delivery date
>
> Dear Mr Adeyemi,
>
> I'm sorry to let you know that your order 4521 will arrive later than planned. Our supplier has delayed one of the components, and the new delivery date is 14 November.
>
> We understand this may affect your plans. As a gesture of goodwill, we have waived the delivery charge on this order. If the new date does not work for you, please reply to this email and we will discuss alternatives.
>
> Thank you for your patience, and again, my apologies for the inconvenience.
>
> Kind regards,
> Priya Nair
> Customer Accounts Team

Notice what the three have in common: a specific subject line, purpose in the first sentence, short paragraphs, one clear request or next step, and a closing that matches the greeting.

## Checklist before you press Send

- Is the subject line specific?
- Does the first or second sentence state your purpose?
- Is your request easy to find, with a deadline if needed?
- Do the greeting and closing match in formality?
- Have you spelled the recipient's name correctly?
- Is the attachment actually attached?
- Did you remove any phrase that might be unclear to an international reader?
- Have you read it once aloud? Mistakes you miss on screen are often obvious when spoken.

## Self-check

Improve each line for a formal email.

1. *Dear Mr John,*
2. *Kindly revert at the earliest.*
3. *Send me the price list.*
4. *I look forward to meet you.*
5. *Dear Sir or Madam, … Cheers, Ana*

### Answers and explanations

1. *Dear Mr Smith,* (title + surname) or *Dear John,* if you are on first-name terms.
2. *Could you please reply as soon as possible?* or, better, give a deadline: *by Friday at noon*.
3. *Could you please send me your current price list?*
4. *I look forward to meeting you.* (*to* is a preposition here.)
5. The closing does not match. Use *Yours faithfully,* or *Sincerely,* with *Dear Sir or Madam*.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I always need "Dear" in a formal email?

For a first contact, an external client or an official enquiry, yes: *Dear* is the safe choice. With colleagues or regular contacts, *Hello* or *Hi* plus their name is normal. Follow the tone the other person uses when they reply.

### What is the difference between "Best regards" and "Kind regards"?

Very little. Both are polite, semi-formal closings used across the English-speaking world. *Kind regards* feels slightly warmer; *Best regards* slightly more neutral. Either works for most business emails.

### How long should a formal email be?

As short as possible while still complete. Most work emails fit in three short paragraphs. If you need more, consider a list, an attachment or a call.

### How do I write a polite follow-up email?

Reply in the same thread so the reader sees the original message, wait a reasonable time first (often three to five working days for an external contact), and keep it to two or three lines: *I'm following up on my email of 3 March about the MBA entry requirements. I'd be grateful for any update when you have a moment.* Avoid *As per my last email*, which many readers hear as irritation.

### Is it fine to use email templates?

Yes, as a starting point. Templates help with structure and standard phrases. Always adapt the purpose, details and request to the real situation, because a generic email is easy to ignore.
