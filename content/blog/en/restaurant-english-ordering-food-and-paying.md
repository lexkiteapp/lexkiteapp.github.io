If you want **English phrases for ordering food at a restaurant**, start with one frame: *I'd like the…, please* to order, *Could I have…?* to ask, and *Could we have the check, please?* (US/Canada) or *the bill* (UK/Australia) to pay. Add *Excuse me* to get attention and *Does this contain…?* for allergies, and you can handle most meals anywhere.

Restaurants are where learners freeze, because the staff are busy, the menu is long and the pressure is public. The good news is that the script is short and very repetitive. This guide follows the meal in order: booking, arriving, understanding the menu, ordering, asking about ingredients, changing a dish, paying, tipping and complaining politely.

## Booking a table

For a busy restaurant, you often book ahead by phone or online. A phone call is short:

> **You:** Hello, I'd like to book a table for four, please.
>
> **Restaurant:** Certainly. What day and time?
>
> **You:** Saturday at seven thirty, if possible.
>
> **Restaurant:** I'm afraid we're full at seven thirty. We have a table at eight or at six forty-five.
>
> **You:** Eight o'clock would be fine. The name is Tran, T-R-A-N.
>
> **Restaurant:** Lovely. Is there anything we should know, such as allergies?
>
> **You:** Yes, one of us is vegetarian, and we'd like a high chair, please.

Useful lines:

- *Do you have a table for two this evening?* / *Do you take reservations?*
- *Could we sit outside?* / *Could we have a quiet table?*
- *We'll be about ten minutes late. Could you hold the table?*
- In the UK and Australia you *book* a table; in the US and Canada people usually *make a reservation*, though both are understood everywhere.

When you walk in without a booking, the host asks *How many?* or *Do you have a reservation?* Answer *A table for two, please* (not *Two persons*). If there is a queue, you may hear *The wait is about twenty minutes.* Reply *That's fine, we'll wait* or *We'll try somewhere else, thanks.*

## Menu words that cause confusion

Menus use their own vocabulary, and some words mean different things in different countries. This is the single most common reason learners order the wrong dish.

{{diagram: d1 | The same menu ideas have different names by region. Check the local word before you order.}}

Notes the table cannot show:

- **Starter, appetizer, entrée, main.** In the US and Canada, an *appetizer* (or *starter*) comes first and the *entrée* is the main dish. In the UK, the first course is the *starter* and the *main* or *main course* follows. In Australia and New Zealand, *entrée* usually means the small first course, so the same word can lead to a very different meal. If you are unsure, ask: *Is the entrée a small dish or a main?*
- **Side, dessert, special.** A *side* (or *side dish*) comes with or next to the main: *fries*, *salad*, *rice*. *Dessert* is the sweet course. *Today's specials* are dishes not on the regular menu.
- **Takeaway, takeout, to go.** For food you eat elsewhere, British and Australian speakers say *takeaway*; Americans and Canadians say *takeout* or *to go*. *Is this for here or to go?* (US) means *Will you eat in or take it?*
- **Tap water, sparkling, still.** Ask *Could we have tap water, please?* if you want free water where that is normal. *Still* is water without bubbles, *sparkling* has bubbles.
- **Rare, medium, well done.** For steak, say how you like it cooked. *Medium rare* is between rare and medium.
- **Vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free.** *Vegetarian* means no meat or fish; *vegan* means no animal products at all, including dairy and eggs; *gluten-free* means made without wheat, barley or rye.

## English phrases for ordering food at a restaurant

The server usually asks *Are you ready to order?* or *Can I get you started with something to drink?* If you need more time, say *Could we have a few more minutes, please?* That is polite and normal.

{{diagram: d2 | A polite order has four parts: attention, a softener, the dish and a change or a need.}}

Compare these:

| Too blunt | Natural and polite |
|---|---|
| *Give me the soup.* | *I'd like the soup, please.* |
| *I want the burger.* | *Could I have the burger, please?* |
| *Water.* | *Could we have some water, please?* |
| *What is good?* | *What would you recommend?* |

*I want* is understandable, but it sounds like a demand. *I'll have the…* is also very natural and slightly more casual. *I'd like…* works everywhere. For more on softening requests, read our guide to [modal verbs can, could, should and must](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/).

A typical dinner:

> **Server:** Good evening. Are you ready to order?
>
> **You:** Yes, I think so. For starters, we'll share the garlic bread.
>
> **Server:** Great. And for your main?
>
> **You:** I'd like the grilled salmon, please. And could I have the rice instead of the fries?
>
> **Server:** Of course. How would you like the salmon, any sauce on the side?
>
> **You:** The sauce on the side would be great, thanks.
>
> **Server:** Anything to drink?
>
> **You:** Just tap water for me, please.

Two small habits help. First, repeat the numbers if the table is noisy: *Two soups and one salad.* Second, say *please* and *thank you* each time; in English-speaking restaurants this matters more than perfect grammar.

## Asking about ingredients, allergies and diets

This is the part where clear English matters most. A food allergy can be serious, so do not use vague words and do not rely on guessing.

- *I have a peanut allergy. Does this dish contain nuts?*
- *Is this gluten-free?* / *Can this be made gluten-free?*
- *Does it contain dairy?* / *Is there egg in it?*
- *I'm allergic to shellfish. Could you check with the kitchen, please?*
- *Is it cooked in the same oil or on the same grill as meat / nuts?* (cross-contact)
- *I don't eat pork.* / *I'm vegetarian. Is the soup made with meat stock?*

Notice the grammar: **allergic to** something (*I'm allergic to eggs*), and **an allergy to** something (*an allergy to eggs*). Do not say *allergy with*. Also remember that *intolerant* is different from *allergic*; *lactose intolerant* means dairy upsets your stomach. If you are unsure whether the staff understood, say it again simply: *Please, no nuts at all. It is an allergy.*

Many menus mark allergens with letters or symbols, but those systems vary and can be out of date. Always ask the server, and for a severe allergy, make it clear that it is a medical allergy and not a preference. This article only covers the language; follow your own doctor's advice about what is safe for you.

## Changing a dish politely

Asking for a change is normal, especially with sauces, sides and cooking style.

- *Could I have the salad without onions?*
- *Could I swap the fries for a side salad?*
- *Could I have the dressing on the side?*
- *Is it possible to make this less spicy?*
- *Could I have extra bread, please?*
- *I changed my mind. Could I have the chicken instead?*

**Without** means *not included*; **with** means *included*. Learners often say *no onion* on its own; that works, but *without onions* sounds smoother. If something costs extra, the server may say *That would be an additional charge.* A good reply is *That's fine* or *No problem, thanks.*

For a quick take-away order, the same frame applies: *Could I get that to go, please?* (US) or *Could I have that as a takeaway, please?* (UK/AU). If you have leftovers: *Could I get a box for this, please?* (US) or *Could I take this home?* (UK).

## Asking for the check or bill, and paying

In the US and Canada, servers often bring the check without being asked; if yours does not come, ask for it. In the UK and many other places, you usually ask for the bill at the table. Say it calmly and make eye contact.

- *Could we have the check, please?* (US/Canada)
- *Could we have the bill, please?* (UK/Australia/NZ)
- *Could we get the check?* is common in the US. *Check, please* is a short version that is fine in a friendly cafe but sounds abrupt in a formal place.
- *Could we split the bill?* / *Separate checks, please.* / *Could we pay separately?*
- *Do you take cards?* / *Can I pay by card?*
- *Keep the change.* (when paying cash and leaving the extra)

If you think the amount is wrong: *Excuse me, I think there may be a mistake on the bill. Could you check it with me?*

**Tipping.** Customs differ a lot. In the US and Canada, tipping the server is widely expected, and the usual amount is often discussed in guidebooks, but the exact norm varies by place, so check locally. In the UK, tipping is common in many sit-down restaurants and a service charge may already be added; look at the bill. In Australia and New Zealand, tipping is usually optional and less expected.

In Germany and Japan, the culture differs again: in Japan tipping is not normal, and in Germany people often round up. When in doubt, ask: *Is service included?* If a service charge is already on the bill, you generally do not need to add another tip on top.

## Complaining politely

Complaints work best when you are calm, specific and ask for a solution. Staff usually want to fix a problem, and a friendly tone gets faster results.

> **You:** Excuse me, sorry to bother you. I ordered the chicken without sauce, but this has sauce on it.
>
> **Server:** Oh, I'm so sorry. Let me take it back.
>
> **You:** Thank you. I'm allergic to that sauce, so could you check with the kitchen?
>
> **Server:** Of course, right away.

More polite complaint lines:

- *Excuse me, this isn't quite what I ordered.*
- *The soup is cold. Could you heat it up, please?*
- *We've been waiting a while for our food. Could you check on it?*
- *Could I speak to the manager, please?* (only if the first step does not work)
- *I think there's a mistake on the bill.*

Avoid *This is terrible* or *You made it wrong*. Use *isn't quite right* and describe the facts. ## Common mistakes learners make

1. **Saying "I want".** Try *I'd like…* or *Could I have…?* instead.
2. **Calling the server loudly.** Raise a hand or make eye contact and say *Excuse me.* Do not shout or snap your fingers.
3. **Confusing entrée.** In the US it is the main dish; in Australia it is the starter, and UK menus use it in different ways. When unsure, ask.
4. **Saying "I am allergic with nuts".** Say *I'm allergic to nuts.*
5. **Forgetting articles.** *Could I have the bill?* not *Could I have bill?* More in our [guide to a, an and the](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/).
6. **Saying "Can I have the menu?" and nothing else.** It is fine, but *Could we see the menu, please?* is warmer, and *Do you have an English menu?* helps when travelling.

2. In the UK, how do you ask to pay? *Could we have the ____, please?*
3. Fix this sentence: *I am allergic with peanuts.*
4. In the US, what is an *entrée*?
5. You want to eat your food at home. What do British and American speakers say? (two words)
6. You want the sauce served separately. What do you say?

**Answers**

1. *I'd like the soup, please.* It is a polite request, not a command.
2. **bill.** In the US and Canada, people say *check*.
3. *I'm allergic to peanuts.* The correct preposition is *to*.
4. The main dish. In the UK, the first course is the *starter*; in Australia, *entrée* often means the small first course.
5. *Takeaway* (UK/Australia) and *takeout* or *to go* (US/Canada).
6. *Could I have the sauce on the side, please?*

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most polite way to order food in English?

Use *I'd like…, please* or *Could I have…, please?* and say *Excuse me* before you start. A calm tone and a smile matter as much as the exact words.

### What is the difference between check and bill?

They mean the same thing at the end of a meal. Americans and Canadians usually say *check*; British and Australian speakers usually say *bill*. Both are understood everywhere.

### How do I ask about allergies in English?

Say clearly *I have an allergy to…* or *I'm allergic to…*, then ask *Does this dish contain…?* For a serious allergy, ask the server to check with the kitchen and say that it is a medical allergy.

### How much should I tip in a restaurant?

It depends on the country and sometimes on the place, and the rules change over time. Look at the bill for a service charge, ask *Is service included?* and check local guidance rather than assuming.
