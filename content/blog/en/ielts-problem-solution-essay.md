An **IELTS problem solution essay** has two jobs, and the question tells you exactly which two. *What problems does this cause, and what solutions can you suggest?* means one body paragraph on problems and one on solutions. *What are the causes and what can be done?* means causes first, then actions. Many candidates lose marks because they answer a slightly different question than the one printed. This guide shows how to read the wording, plan the essay, and write a model with clear links between each problem and each solution.

## Read the wording carefully

{{diagram: d1 | The two question parts decide the two body paragraphs}}

Look at the nouns and verbs in the final sentence. *Problems*, *causes*, *effects*, *consequences*, *challenges* all belong in the first body paragraph. *Solutions*, *measures*, *steps*, *what can be done* belong in the second. If the question says *Who should be responsible?*, you must answer that too: governments, schools, companies or individuals.

Not every prompt uses the same pairing, so adapt the layout. A question that asks for only one part, such as *What can be done to solve this problem?*, still needs developed ideas, but you may give two solutions in two body paragraphs. For the other question types, see [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/).

## Choose problems you can solve

Write the plan before the essay. List two problems or causes and two solutions that correspond to them. If you choose a problem that you cannot solve in a sentence, change it.

Original question: *In many cities, young people spend more time on screens and less time outdoors. What problems does this cause, and what can be done?*

| Problem | Matching solution |
|---|---|
| Less physical activity leads to weaker health | Schools make daily outdoor exercise part of the timetable |
| Face-to-face friendships suffer | Local clubs and parks offer free group activities |

Notice that each solution answers one problem. This matching makes the essay coherent, and the examiner can follow the argument without searching for the connection.

## A model IELTS problem solution essay

> Young people in many cities now spend much of their free time on screens rather than outdoors. This trend creates serious problems for their health and social lives, but there are practical steps that schools and local authorities can take.
>
> The most obvious problem is physical inactivity. Teenagers who sit for hours playing games or watching videos burn far fewer calories than those who play sports, and over time this can lead to weight gain, poor fitness and lower energy levels at school. A second problem is social. Online contact can be convenient and sometimes very helpful for shy teenagers, but it cannot fully replace conversations in person, and young people who rarely meet friends may find it harder to build confidence and communication skills.
>
> Fortunately, these problems can be addressed without banning technology, which most families would find unrealistic. Firstly, schools could include at least thirty minutes of outdoor exercise in every school day, so that activity becomes a habit rather than an option. Secondly, local councils might fund youth clubs and well-kept parks where teenagers can take part in free group activities, such as football or art. Such clubs also give shy teenagers a low-pressure way to practise talking to others. When young people meet regularly in such places, friendships develop naturally and screens become one hobby among many rather than the only one.
>
> In conclusion, too much screen time harms both physical and social health, but schools and local authorities can reduce the problem by creating attractive opportunities to be active and to meet others in person.

The essay is about 265 words.

## Planning timeline for the model

The model took about five minutes to plan: one minute to read and underline the two question parts, two minutes to list problems and solutions, and two minutes to choose the best pairs. The writing itself needs around 30 minutes, leaving about five minutes for checking articles, plurals and linking words. Keeping to this rhythm is more important than finding the perfect idea.

## What makes the essay work

- **The introduction names both parts of the question.** It states the trend, signals problems and solutions, and mentions who will act.
- **Each problem is explained, not only named.** *Physical inactivity* is followed by a reason and a consequence.
- **Solutions are specific.** *Thirty minutes of outdoor exercise in every school day* is clearer than *schools should do more*.
- **Link words show logic**: *firstly*, *secondly*, *so that*, *when*, *rather than*. See [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/) for more.
- **The conclusion restates, without new ideas.**

## Language for problems and solutions

| Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|
| Introduce a problem | *A major concern is..., One consequence is..., This can lead to...* |
| Explain a cause | *This is largely due to..., The main reason is..., It results from...* |
| Propose a solution | *One effective measure would be..., Governments could..., It would help if...* |
| Show an outcome | *As a result..., This would reduce..., Consequently...* |
| Be tentative | *could, might, would, may help to* |

Using *could* and *might* for suggestions is more natural than claiming that a solution *will* work. Strong certainty can sound unrealistic.

## Common mistakes

1. **Only discussing problems.** The solutions paragraph is half the question.
2. **Solutions unrelated to the problems.** *Teach children to cook* does not solve *screen time*.
3. **Vague solutions.** *The government must take action* says nothing. Say what action, and by whom.
4. **Listing too many ideas.** Two problems and two solutions, each explained, beat five problems named in a list.
5. **Repeated phrases.** Using *problem* ten times shows limited range; vary it with *issue*, *concern*, *challenge* when the meaning fits.
6. **Unsupported claims about statistics.** You do not need numbers, and invented ones can mislead.

You can compare these with the broader list in [IELTS writing mistakes that cost marks](/en/blog/ielts-writing-mistakes/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many problems and solutions should I include?

Two of each is a good balance for about 260 to 290 words. Each one needs an explanation, so depth matters more than number.

### Do I have to give my own opinion in a problem solution essay?

Usually not, unless the question asks for it. The task is to explain problems and propose solutions, so a clear conclusion that summarises your view is enough.

### Can I write about causes instead of problems?

Only if the question asks about causes. Always follow the exact words of the prompt.

### What if I cannot think of a solution?

Use common frameworks: education, regulation, funding and individual responsibility. Pick the one that best matches the problem and explain how it would work.
