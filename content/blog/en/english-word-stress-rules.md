Can you predict where the stress falls in an English word? Often, yes. Word stress rules are not perfect, but a handful of them cover most everyday vocabulary: two-syllable nouns usually stress the first syllable, two-syllable verbs usually stress the second, and many suffixes either pull the stress to a fixed place or leave it alone. Learn those patterns, check exceptions in a dictionary, and you will stop saying *de-VE-lop-MENT* or *COM-mittee*.

This guide explains each rule with examples and IPA, shows the traps that catch learners from many language backgrounds, and ends with a short self-check.

## What word stress is, and why it matters so much

In every English word of two or more syllables, one syllable is **stressed**. It is longer, a little louder, often higher in pitch, and it keeps a full vowel. The other syllables get weaker, and many of their vowels shrink to the neutral sound /ə/ (schwa). Endings matter too: [ed pronunciation rules](/en/blog/ed-pronunciation-rules/) explain why *walked* has one syllable.

Take *banana* /bəˈnɑːnə/. Only the middle syllable is strong; the first and last are both schwa. The mark ˈ in IPA appears **before** the stressed syllable, and ˌ marks a weaker secondary stress, as in *education* /ˌedʒuˈkeɪʃn/.

Why does this matter? Native and fluent listeners recognise words largely by their stress shape. If you say *ho-TEL* correctly but *DE-sert* when you mean *de-SERT* (dessert), the listener hears a different word. A wrong stress often causes more confusion than a slightly wrong vowel.

Learners whose first language is **syllable-timed** feel this most. In Spanish, French, Italian, Tagalog, Yoruba, Hindi and many other languages, syllables tend to get roughly equal length, so speakers give every English syllable a full vowel: *ba-na-na* with three equal beats. French speakers often add stress at the end of the word; speakers of tone languages such as Mandarin, Cantonese or Vietnamese may use pitch but keep every vowel full. The fix is the same for everyone: make the stressed syllable clearly longer and let the others go weak.

A note on varieties: Indian, Nigerian, Filipino and other established Englishes have their own stress habits, and they are legitimate in local use. If your goal is to be understood easily by listeners from anywhere, including exam examiners, it pays to know the patterns shared by most dictionary models, which is what this guide describes.

Here are stress errors that teachers hear again and again, across many first languages:

- *de-VE-lop-MENT* or *DE-ve-lop*: stress on the wrong syllable, or two equal stresses.
- *COM-mit-tee* instead of *com-MIT-tee* /kəˈmɪti/.
- *ca-TE-go-ry* instead of *CA-te-go-ry* /ˈkætəɡəri/.
- *a-na-LY-sis* instead of *a-NA-ly-sis* /əˈnæləsɪs/.
- *in-te-RES-ting* instead of *IN-te-res-ting* /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/, which native speakers often say in three syllables.

## Word stress rules for two-syllable words

### Nouns and adjectives: usually the first syllable

*TA-ble, WIN-dow, DOC-tor, HAP-py, CLE-ver, MO-dern, PI-lot.*

### Verbs: usually the second syllable

*be-GIN, de-CIDE, re-LAX, ex-PLAIN, pre-FER, ad-MIT.*

### Common exceptions

- Nouns stressed on the second syllable: *ho-TEL* /həʊˈtel/, *mis-TAKE*, *ma-CHINE* /məˈʃiːn/, *po-LICE* /pəˈliːs/, *de-SIGN*, *ad-VICE*.
- Verbs stressed on the first syllable, especially those ending in *-en, -er, -ish, -ow, -y*: *HAP-pen, O-pen, LIS-ten, AN-swer, OF-fer, EN-ter, FIN-ish, FOL-low, MAR-ry, VI-sit*.

The exceptions are frequent words, so you probably know many already. The rule helps most with words you meet for the first time.

## Noun–verb pairs that shift stress

A group of two-syllable words is spelled the same as a noun and a verb, but the stress moves: first syllable for the noun, second for the verb. The vowel often changes too, because the unstressed syllable weakens.

| Word | Noun | Verb |
|---|---|---|
| record | /ˈrekɔːd/ a world record | /rɪˈkɔːd/ to record a song |
| present | /ˈpreznt/ a birthday present | /prɪˈzent/ to present results |
| object | /ˈɒbdʒɪkt/ a strange object | /əbˈdʒekt/ to object to a plan |
| increase | /ˈɪŋkriːs/ a price increase | /ɪnˈkriːs/ to increase sales |
| permit | /ˈpɜːmɪt/ a work permit | /pəˈmɪt/ to permit entry |
| conduct | /ˈkɒndʌkt/ good conduct | /kənˈdʌkt/ to conduct a survey |
| produce | /ˈprɒdjuːs/ fresh produce | /prəˈdjuːs/ to produce evidence |

(British IPA shown; American English uses /ɑː/ for the /ɒ/ vowel, so *object* is /ˈɑːbdʒɪkt/.)

Not every pair works this way. *Promise, visit, answer* and *travel* keep first-syllable stress in both uses, and *report, respect, control* keep second-syllable stress in both.

## Suffixes: the key to long words

For words of three or more syllables, look at the ending. Suffixes fall into four useful groups.

### Group 1: stress on the syllable just before the suffix

Suffixes: **-tion, -sion, -ic, -ical, -ity, -ial, -ian, -ious, -ient, -ience**.

*in-for-MA-tion, de-CI-sion, e-co-NO-mic, po-LI-ti-cal, u-ni-VER-si-ty, es-SEN-tial, mu-SI-cian, am-BI-tious, ef-FI-cient.*

This is the most reliable rule in English stress. If you learn only one, learn this.

### Group 2: stress on the third syllable from the end

Suffixes: **-ate** (in words of three or more syllables), **-graphy, -logy, -ify, -ise/-ize** (usually).

*com-MU-ni-cate* /kəˈmjuːnɪkeɪt/, *pho-TO-gra-phy* /fəˈtɒɡrəfi/, *bi-O-lo-gy* /baɪˈɒlədʒi/, *CLAS-si-fy* /ˈklæsɪfaɪ/, *OR-ga-nise* /ˈɔːɡənaɪz/.

### Group 3: suffixes that take the stress themselves

Suffixes: **-ee, -eer, -ese, -ique, -ette, -esque**.

*em-ploy-EE* /ˌɪmplɔɪˈiː/, *en-gi-NEER* /ˌendʒɪˈnɪə/, *Chi-NESE* /ˌtʃaɪˈniːz/, *u-NIQUE* /juˈniːk/, *ci-ga-RETTE*.

(*Committee* /kəˈmɪti/ looks like an *-ee* word but is not; it is a classic exception.)

### Group 4: neutral suffixes that do not move stress

Suffixes: **-ness, -ment, -ful, -less, -er, -or, -ly, -ing, -ed, -able** (mostly).

*HAP-py → HAP-pi-ness, em-PLOY → em-PLOY-ment, CARE → CARE-less, COM-fort → COM-fort-a-ble.*

So *development* is *de-VE-lop-ment*, with the stress where it was in *develop*. The suffix adds a weak syllable and changes nothing else.

**Prefixes behave the same way.** *Un-, dis-, re-, in-, mis-* and *over-* rarely take the main stress: *un-HAP-py, dis-a-GREE, re-WRITE, mis-UN-der-STAND.* If you stress the prefix, the word sounds contrastive, as if you were correcting someone (*I said UNlock, not lock*).

## Word families: watch the stress jump

Because suffixes in Groups 1–3 move the stress, words in the same family can sound surprisingly different.

- *PHO-to-graph* /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/ → *pho-TO-gra-pher* /fəˈtɒɡrəfə/ → *pho-to-GRA-phic* /ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk/
- *e-CO-no-my* /ɪˈkɒnəmi/ → *e-co-NO-mic* /ˌiːkəˈnɒmɪk/
- *PO-li-tics* → *po-LI-ti-cal* → *po-li-TI-cian*
- *a-CA-de-my* /əˈkædəmi/ → *a-ca-DE-mic* /ˌækəˈdemɪk/

Notice that the vowel quality changes along with the stress. The /ɒ/ in *photographer* becomes a schwa in *photograph*. Saying the full vowel in the wrong place is what makes a word hard to recognise.

When you learn a new academic word, learn its family at the same time and mark the stress on each form. Our guide to the [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/) suggests family-based learning for exactly this reason.

## Compounds, phrasal verbs and the -teen/-ty trap

**Compound nouns** usually stress the first part: *BLACK-board, GREEN-house, BOOK-shop, TOOTH-brush.* Compare the phrase *a black BOARD* (a board that is black) or *a green HOUSE* (a house painted green).

**Compound adjectives** do the opposite and usually stress the second part: *old-FASH-ioned, well-KNOWN, bad-TEM-pered.* When one comes before a stressed noun, the stress often moves forward to avoid two strong beats in a row: *a WELL-known WRI-ter*.

**Phrasal verbs** stress the particle: *give UP, turn DOWN, set UP, work OUT.* Their **noun** forms stress the first part: *a SET-up, a WORK-out, a BREAK-down, a TAKE-away.*

**Numbers**: *thir-TEEN* /ˌθɜːˈtiːn/ vs *THIR-ty* /ˈθɜːti/. In listening tests this pair causes many wrong answers. Listen for the long /iːn/ in *-teen* versus a short, weak ending in *-ty*. In American English, the *t* in *thirty* often becomes a quick flap that sounds close to /d/. One more subtlety: when *-teen* numbers come before a stressed noun, the stress can shift forward (*THIR-teen PEO-ple*), so the length of the vowel is a better clue than stress position alone. Our [IELTS listening tips](/en/blog/ielts-listening-tips/) cover more number traps.

## How to check and practise stress

1. **Always check the IPA.** When you look up a word, find the ˈ mark. In Lexkite's [dictionary](/#/dict/photography), every entry shows UK and US transcription with an audio button, so you can see the stress mark and hear it in both accents.

![Lexkite dictionary entry for "resilient" with UK and US IPA, audio, example sentence and collocations](media:dict "Check the stress mark in the IPA line before you add a new word to your list")

2. **Mark stress in your notes.** Write *de-VE-lop-ment* or underline the stressed syllable. Notes without stress train your eyes, not your mouth.
3. **Clap or tap.** Tap the table on the stressed syllable while saying the word. It sounds childish and it works.
4. **Practise in phrases.** Stress in isolation is easier than in a sentence. Say *an e-co-NO-mic po-LI-cy*, not just the single word.
5. **Get feedback.** Record yourself, or use [scored pronunciation practice](/#/practice/speak): you say a word or phrase into the microphone and the app scores it against your voice. If you need to fix individual sounds first, start with the [IPA library](/#/pron).

![Lexkite speaking practice for "cut down on" with a microphone button and a pronunciation score](media:practice-speak "Say multi-syllable words into the mic and check your pronunciation score")

## Self-check

Mark the stressed syllable.

1. comfortable
2. decision (and decide)
3. engineer
4. to record (verb)
5. photographer
6. hotel
7. economic
8. fourteen (said alone)

### Answers and explanations

1. **COM**-fortable: *-able* is neutral, so the stress stays on *comfort*.
2. de-**CI**-sion (stress before *-sion*); de-**CIDE** (two-syllable verb).
3. engi-**NEER**: *-eer* takes the stress.
4. re-**CORD**: verb in a noun–verb pair.
5. pho-**TO**-grapher: same stress as *photography*; *-er* is neutral.
6. ho-**TEL**: a common noun exception.
7. eco-**NO**-mic: stress before *-ic*.
8. four-**TEEN**: *-teen* is stressed when the number stands alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a rule that works for every word?

No. English absorbed words from many languages, so every rule has exceptions. The suffix rules for *-tion, -ic* and *-ity* are the most reliable. For everything else, use rules to guess and the dictionary to confirm.

### Will people misunderstand me if I stress the wrong syllable?

Sometimes. Listeners use stress to identify words, so a wrong stress can make a familiar word unrecognisable, especially when it also changes the vowels. Context often rescues you, but it costs the listener effort.

### Do British and American English stress words differently?

Mostly no, with a few well-known differences: *adVERtisement* (UK) vs *adverTISEment* (US), *GArage* (UK) vs *gaRAGE* (US), and some words borrowed from French, such as *ballet*. Pick one model and be consistent.

### Does word stress affect the IELTS Pronunciation score?

Yes. Word and sentence stress are part of what examiners assess under Pronunciation, alongside individual sounds, rhythm and intonation. Clear stress makes you easier to understand, which is the core of that criterion.
