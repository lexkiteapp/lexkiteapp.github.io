You can read a map, but real directions come as quick spoken English from a stranger. **Asking for directions in English** is easier when you know three things: a polite way to ask, the small set of words people use to answer, and how to check that you understood. This guide covers all three, then moves on to buses, trains, taxis and delay announcements.

Details such as fares, ticket types, schedules and apps vary a lot between cities, so nothing here replaces the local transport website or the staff at the station. Treat prices and rules as "check locally".

## Asking for directions in English: the polite opening

Strangers are usually happy to help, but they are busy. Start with a short attention-getter, ask politely, and say exactly where you want to go.

{{diagram: d2 | Four parts of a polite request: get attention, ask, name the place, then check and thank.}}

Good openers:

- *Excuse me, could you tell me how to get to the bus station?*
- *Excuse me, is there a pharmacy near here?*
- *Sorry to bother you. How do I get to Market Street?*
- *Hi, I'm looking for the museum. Am I going the right way?*

Notice the difference between *Where is...?* and *Could you tell me where... is?* Both are fine, but the second is softer. After *Could you tell me*, the word order goes back to a statement: *Could you tell me where the station **is**?* (not *where is the station*). This is a very common slip for learners, so listen for it in your own speech. Our guide to [how to form questions in English](/en/blog/how-to-form-questions-in-english/) explains the pattern.

Useful follow-ups when the answer is too fast:

- *Sorry, could you say that again, more slowly?*
- *Could you show me on my phone?*
- *How far is it? Is it walkable?* / *How long does it take on foot?*
- *Is it far from here, or can I walk?*

## Understanding the answer: direction words

You will hear a small set of words over and over. Learn these as sounds, not just spellings.

- **Go straight (on / ahead).** *Go straight on for about five minutes.* In the US you often hear *go straight*.
- **Turn left / turn right.** *Take the second left.* *Turn right at the lights.*
- **At the end of the road / street.** *At the end of the street, turn left.*
- **Corner.** *It's on the corner of Oak and Main.*
- **Block (mostly US/Canada).** A *block* is the stretch of street between two crossings. *It's two blocks up, then one block to the right.* The British usually say *the second turning* or *the second street on the left*.
- **Junction / intersection.** *Junction* is British; *intersection* is American. *At the next junction, turn right.*
- **Roundabout (UK, AU) / traffic circle (US).** *Take the second exit at the roundabout.*
- **Traffic lights / stoplight.** *Turn left at the lights.* (US: *stoplight*, *light*.)
- **Opposite / across from.** *It's opposite the bank* (UK), *across from the bank* (US).
- **Next to / beside / behind / in front of / between.** *It's between the café and the post office.*
- **Cross.** *Cross the road at the crossing.* (US: *crosswalk*; UK: *zebra crossing* or *pedestrian crossing*.)
- **Overpass / underpass / bridge / footbridge.** *Take the footbridge over the railway.*

A typical conversation:

> **You:** Excuse me, could you tell me how to get to the central library?
>
> **Passer-by:** Sure. Go straight on down this road until you reach the traffic lights. Turn left, and it's about two blocks along, on the right-hand side, just after the bank.
>
> **You:** So, straight on to the lights, left, then two blocks, and it's on the right?
>
> **Passer-by:** That's it. You can't miss it. It's a big grey building.
>
> **You:** Great, thanks so much for your help.

The best habit here is the check-back: repeat the route in your own words. If you repeat it wrongly, the other person corrects you straight away. Also notice *You can't miss it*, a friendly phrase that means the place is easy to see.

## Public transport: words you will need

{{diagram: d1 | The same transport ideas have different words in the UK, the US and Canada, and Australia. Use the local one in each place.}}

A few more words about the system itself:

- **Subway, underground, metro, tube.** City trains under the ground. *The Tube* is London's. *Metro* is used in many cities. In the UK, *subway* usually means an underpass for pedestrians, so say *underground* or *Tube* when you want trains.
- **Platform, track, line.** *The train leaves from platform 4.* A *line* is a route: *the red line*.
- **Stop and station.** Bus *stops* and train *stations*. *Which stop do I get off at?*
- **Fare, ticket, pass.** The *fare* is the price of a journey. A *single* (UK) or *one-way* ticket goes one direction; a *return* (UK) or *round-trip* (US) ticket covers both. A *day pass* or *travel card* gives unlimited rides for a period. Cities also use *tap-in/tap-out* cards or contactless payment, so check how it works locally.
- **Zone.** Some cities charge by distance bands, called *zones*.
- **Transfer (US) / change (UK).** *Do I need to change at Central?* means switching to another train or bus. In the US, a *transfer* can also be a ticket that lets you change vehicles.
- **Direction and terminus.** Trains are named by their final stop. *Take the train towards Airport Terminal.* *The terminus* or *end of the line* is the last station.
- **Off-peak / rush hour.** *Rush hour* (or *peak time*) is when everyone commutes and tickets or crowds may differ.

Questions at the ticket machine or counter:

- *Which platform for the train to the city centre?*
- *Is this the right train for the airport?*
- *Does this bus stop at the hospital?*
- *How much is a single to the airport?* / *How much is the fare to downtown?*
- *Do I need to buy a ticket before I get on, or can I pay on the bus?*
- *Do I need to validate my ticket?* (Some systems ask you to stamp or scan it.)
- *Could you tell me when we get to the stop? I'm not sure where to get off.*

At a station, you may also hear *Mind the gap* (UK: be careful stepping between the train and platform) or *Stand clear of the doors* (US).

## Delays, cancellations and announcements

Announcements are fast and full of formal words, so it helps to know a few chunks. Exact wording varies by country and operator.

- *The 9:15 service to Leeds is **delayed** by approximately twenty minutes.* (Later than planned.)
- *This train has been **cancelled**.* (UK; Americans may write *canceled*.)
- *We apologise for the inconvenience.*
- *Due to signal problems / a fault on the line / planned engineering works, services are disrupted.*
- *Passengers are advised to use alternative routes.* (Take another way.)
- *This train terminates at ...* (It goes no further.) *All change, please.* (Everyone must get off.)
- *There is a replacement bus service.* (A bus takes the place of the train.)
- *The next train is due in five minutes.* (Expected.)

When it affects you:

- *Excuse me, has the 9:15 been cancelled? What's the next one?*
- *Is there another way to get to the airport?*
- *Can I use my ticket on a different service?* (Ask staff; rules vary by operator.)

Staff at the station can give the current information, and apps and signs update in real time. If you cannot understand the announcement, ask the person next to you: *Sorry, what did they say?*

## Taxis and ride-share

For taxis and apps, the language is short and practical. In many places the destination is given on the app; with a street taxi you say it aloud.

- *Could you take me to this address, please?* (Show it on your phone.)
- *To the airport, please. Terminal 2.*
- *Could you drop me off here?* / *Here is fine, thanks.*
- *Is it okay to put my bag in the boot?* (UK) / *...in the trunk?* (US)
- *About how long will it take?* / *Do you know roughly how much it will be?*
- *Could you stop at an ATM on the way?* (Fares and tipping customs vary; check locally.)

Ride-share tips: check the car, the plate number and the driver's name in the app. The driver may say *I'm out the front* (UK/AU) or *I'm outside* when they arrive. If you cannot find each other: *I'm by the main entrance. Could you tell me where you are?* A pleasant ending is *Thanks, have a good evening*.

## UK vs US vs Canada vs Australia: what changes

You do not need to learn two languages, but a few differences can cause confusion:

- A *flat* in the UK is an *apartment* in the US, and a *lift* in the UK is an *elevator* in the US.
- *First floor* means the floor above ground level in the UK and the ground level in the US. So *It's on the first floor* can mean different floors. Ask: *Which floor is that, counting from the ground?*
- *Pavement* in the UK is the walkway; in the US *pavement* means the road surface. Americans say *sidewalk*.
- Road distances are in *miles* on UK and US signs, and in *kilometres* in Canada and Australia. Walking time is easier to ask about: *How long does it take on foot?*

## Tips for listening to directions

1. **Listen for the pattern, not every word.** Most directions are: *go straight, turn, landmark, how far*. If you caught *left*, *lights*, *bank*, you probably have the route.
2. **Pay attention to numbers.** *Second left* and *third exit* are easy to mishear. Repeat them back.
3. **Ask for landmarks.** *Is there anything I should look for?* The answer, such as *a red building*, helps more than street names.
4. **Check with your phone.** Use a map for the big picture, and use people for the last 200 metres or any shortcut that the map misses.
5. **Do not pretend to understand.** *Sorry, could you slow down?* is always better than turning the wrong way.
6. **Practise with shadowing.** Listen to short direction clips and repeat them in the same rhythm. Our [shadowing guide](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) shows how, and the [listening guide](/en/blog/how-to-improve-english-listening/) has more ideas.

## Mistakes learners make

1. **Wrong word order after "Could you tell me".** *Could you tell me where **the station is**?*, not *where is the station*.
2. **Using "go" without a preposition.** *Go **to** the end of the road*, *turn **at** the lights*, *get **off** at Central*. For prepositions, see our guide to [in, on and at](/en/blog/prepositions-in-on-at/).
3. **"Take" vs "catch".** You *take* a bus to travel on it, and you *catch* it when the point is not missing it: *I need to catch the 8:40 train.*
4. **Missing "the".** *Turn left at the lights*, not *at lights*. More in our guide to [a, an and the](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/).
5. **Too blunt.** *Where is the toilet?* is understood, but *Excuse me, do you know where the restroom is?* (US) or *...the toilets are?* (UK) is smoother.

## Self-check: choose the best answer

1. Someone says "Take the second left." What do you do?
2. In the US you say *sidewalk*. What do you say in the UK?
3. Fix the sentence: *Could you tell me where is the station?*
4. A sign says "This train terminates at Central." What does it mean?
5. You hear "The 9:15 has been cancelled." What question could you ask?
6. Which fits better: *I need to take the 8:40 train* or *I need to catch the 8:40 train*?

**Answers**

1. Go straight and turn left at the second street on the left (the second turning). Repeat it back to check: *So, the second left?*
2. **Pavement** (and Australians say *footpath*).
3. *Could you tell me where the station is?* After *Could you tell me*, use normal statement word order.
4. The train goes no further than Central, so everyone must get off there.
5. *What's the next train?* or *Is there another way to get to the airport?*
6. Both are correct. *Catch* is more natural when the focus is on getting there in time.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I ask for directions politely in English?

Start with *Excuse me*, then use *Could you tell me how to get to...?* or *Is there a ... near here?* Name the place clearly, and finish by repeating the route and saying thank you.

### What is the difference between a subway, a Tube and a metro?

They are all city trains. *Subway* is the usual American word, *the Tube* is London's underground, and *metro* is used in many cities. In the UK, *subway* may mean a pedestrian underpass, so say *underground* if you want trains.

### What does "change" or "transfer" mean on public transport?

It means leaving one train or bus and getting on another to complete your journey. British speakers usually say *change* (*Do I need to change at Central?*), Americans often say *transfer*. Fares and rules differ by city, so check locally.

### What should I do if I do not understand an announcement?

Ask the person next to you, *Sorry, what did they say?*, or ask a staff member. Station screens and the operator's app often show the same information in writing.
