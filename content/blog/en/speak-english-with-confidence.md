Most learners who want to speak English with confidence already know enough words. What they lack is a small set of phrases they trust, practiced out loud in the exact moments that make them nervous. Confidence is not a personality trait you either have or don't. It is the feeling of having said a sentence many times before, so that when the moment arrives, your mouth already knows the way.

This guide walks through 12 everyday situations, grouped from easy to demanding. For each one you get the phrases that carry the conversation, a common "stiff" version to retire, and a quick note on why the better phrase works.

## Why it is hard to speak English with confidence

Nerves in a second language usually come from three places:

- **Search time.** You know the idea but need two or three seconds to find the words. That silence feels huge, so you panic.
- **Fear of sounding rude.** Many textbook phrases are grammatically correct but socially odd. *Give me a coffee* is understandable, yet it can sound blunt.
- **No exit plan.** You worry about what happens if you do not understand the reply.

All three shrink with the same fix, and you can see more model answers on the [speaking samples](/speaking/) page: a short, rehearsed toolkit for each situation, plus a few rescue phrases for when things go off-script. You do not need a big vocabulary for that. You need the right 6 to 8 phrases per situation, said aloud until they feel boring.

## Level 1: four situations for your first week

These are short, predictable exchanges. The other person usually follows a script too, which makes them ideal for building early wins.

### 1. Greetings and small talk

- *Nice to meet you.* / *How's it going?*
- *So, what do you do?*
- Retire: *How are you? I am fine, thank you, and you?* It is correct but sounds like a classroom drill. Try *Pretty good, thanks. You?*

### 2. At a café

- *Can I get a latte, please?*
- *For here or to go?* (learn to understand this one)
- Retire: *I want a latte.* "I want" is fine with friends but feels demanding at a counter. *Can I get…* or *I'd like…* is the everyday standard.

### 3. Shopping

- *I'm just looking, thanks.*
- *Do you have this in a smaller size?*
- *Where are the fitting rooms?*
- Retire: *No, I don't need help.* It can sound cold. *I'm just looking, thanks* says the same thing warmly.

### 4. Asking for directions

- *Excuse me, sorry to bother you.*
- *Is there a pharmacy near here?*
- *So it's left at the lights, then straight on?* (repeat the route back)
- Repeating the directions back is a powerful habit. It checks your understanding and gives the other person a chance to correct you.

## Level 2: four situations where plans can change

Here the conversation can branch. Someone asks a question you did not expect, or a problem needs solving.

### 5. Hotels and airports

- *I have a reservation under the name Lee.*
- *Could I get a late checkout?*
- *My flight was canceled. Can you rebook me?*
- Tip: give your name and the key fact in the first sentence. Staff are busy; front-loading helps them help you.

### 6. At the doctor's

- *I've had a sore throat since Monday.*
- *It hurts when I swallow.*
- *Should I take this with food?*
- The present perfect (*I've had…*) is the natural tense for symptoms that started in the past and are still happening. If that tense still feels shaky, the guide on [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) covers it.

### 7. Phone calls

- *Hi, this is Sam calling about my appointment.*
- *Sorry, the line is bad. Could you say that again?*
- *Let me just check I've got that right.*
- Phone calls are hard because you lose lip movement and gestures. Slow down and confirm details out loud.

### 8. Making friends

- *Is this seat free?*
- *I'm Lina, by the way.*
- *We should grab a coffee sometime.*
- Retire: *Can we be friends?* It is sweet but unusual between adults. Suggesting a shared activity does the same job naturally.

## Level 3: four situations with higher stakes

These involve work, money or disagreement. The phrases are longer, and tone matters as much as grammar.

### 9. Work meetings

- *Could I add something here?*
- *Just to clarify, are we moving the deadline?*
- *We're on track for Friday.*

### 10. Job interviews

- *I've worked in customer support for three years.*
- *What drew me to this role was the chance to train new staff.*
- Retire: *I am a hard worker.* Everyone says it. Replace it with one specific example.

### 11. Making a complaint

- *I'm calling about an order I received yesterday.*
- *Unfortunately, the screen is cracked.*
- *What can you do to fix this?*
- A good complaint has three parts: what happened, the evidence, and what you want. Stay calm; the facts do the work.

### 12. Disagreeing politely

- *That's a fair point. However, the timeline worries me.*
- *I see it a bit differently.*
- Retire: *You are wrong.* Even when true, it closes the discussion. Acknowledge first, then disagree.

## Rescue phrases that work in every situation

When you freeze, these buy time without sounding lost:

| Problem | Rescue phrase |
|---|---|
| You did not hear | *Sorry, could you say that again?* |
| You did not understand a word | *Sorry, what does "deposit" mean here?* |
| You need thinking time | *Hmm, let me think for a second.* |
| You forgot a word | *It's the thing you use to… / It's a kind of…* |
| You want to check | *So you mean…?* |

Describing a word you have forgotten ("the thing you use to open wine") is a real skill, not a failure. Native speakers do it all the time.

## How to practice so the phrases stick

Reading this list will not make you confident. Saying it will. A simple routine:

1. **Listen first.** Hear a short model dialogue, slowly and then at normal speed.
2. **Shadow.** Repeat each line right after the speaker, matching rhythm and stress. The [shadowing technique](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) guide explains how.
3. **Fill the gap aloud.** Cover part of a phrase and say the full sentence from memory.
4. **Role-play.** Have a short conversation where the other side can surprise you.
5. **Review later.** Bring the phrases back over the next days with [spaced repetition](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/), so they stay ready.

Keep each session short, around five to eight minutes. Short and frequent beats one long, stressful hour. If you also want the sounds to flow, read about [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/), which explains why *Can I get a…* often sounds like "c'nai get a".

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to speak English with confidence?

It depends on your level, but most learners feel a real change in specific situations within a couple of weeks of short daily practice. Confidence grows situation by situation, so pick the one you face most often and start there.

### Should I learn grammar first or phrases first?

Do both, but let phrases lead for speaking. A ready phrase like *Could I get…?* is useful today, and the grammar inside it becomes clear as you use it.

### What if I make mistakes while speaking?

You will, and that is fine. Listeners care about meaning and tone far more than perfect grammar. A polite phrase with a small error beats a perfect sentence you were too scared to say.

### Is it OK to use the same phrases again and again?

Yes. Fluent speakers rely on fixed phrases constantly. Reusing a phrase is how it becomes automatic, which frees your mind to listen.
