Read Aloud is the first scored speaking task in PTE Academic (the unscored Personal Introduction comes before it): a text of up to 60 words appears, you get 30 to 40 seconds to prepare, and then you read it aloud. According to Pearson's task description, the answer also counts toward your Reading score. This page turns Pearson's published criteria into practical **PTE read aloud pronunciation tips**: how to chunk the text, which syllables to stress, and how to keep going when you slip.

Some strong English speakers treat this task as a reading test rather than a speaking one. You are scored on what you say and on how it sounds, and the second part is mostly about clear stress and steady rhythm.

## What Pearson says Read Aloud is scored on

On its speaking and writing test-format page, Pearson describes three things being scored in Read Aloud:

{{diagram: d1 | The three Read Aloud criteria as Pearson describes them, with one practical step for each.}}

### Content
Every word of the text counts. Replacing a word, adding one or leaving one out lowers the content score, so read what is printed, even if a word is unfamiliar.

### Pronunciation
Pearson looks at whether your speech is easy to understand, with clear vowels, consonants and stress. Its published descriptions do not require a particular accent.

### Oral fluency
Pearson describes fluency as smooth rhythm, phrasing and stress, and says hesitations, repetitions and false starts lower it. You can see why chunking and a steady pace matter: they are how you avoid those.

If you are comparing tests, the guide to [PTE vs IELTS for Australia](/en/blog/pte-vs-ielts-australia/) explains how the two differ.

## 5 PTE read aloud pronunciation tips for test day

These techniques follow Pearson's published criteria for pronunciation and fluency:

### 1. Chunk the text into thought groups
Never read word by word. During the 30 to 40 seconds of preparation time, mark in your head where the meaning groups end:

> *[In the contemporary global economy,] [sustainable technological innovation] [is reshaping] [traditional manufacturing.]*

Pause briefly between chunks, but speak continuously within each chunk. This gives you natural phrasing and fewer hesitations inside a phrase.

### 2. Stress the content words
Give a little more length and emphasis to nouns, main verbs and adjectives, and say words such as *in the*, *of a* and *to the* quickly and lightly. English rhythm is built around its stressed syllables, and this contrast is what makes speech sound natural rather than flat.

### 3. Keep an even pace
Speak at a natural, steady pace: rushing blurs words together, and slowing down with long gaps breaks the rhythm. If the text is long, a calm, even pace is safer than a sprint to finish. Pearson notes that the recording time depends on the length of the text and that a progress bar shows what is left, so aim to finish before it ends. Pearson also suggests using the punctuation to plan your pauses: commas and full stops mark natural places to breathe.

### 4. Say the word endings
Final consonants carry meaning in English, so say them: *aspects*, *impacts*, *predicts*. Dropping the last sound of a word makes it harder to understand even when the stress is right.

### 5. If you slip, keep going
If you stumble on a word, carry on: repetitions and false starts lower your oral fluency, and stopping to repeat costs more than the one word you missed.

## Syllable stress in academic words

Long academic words are where stress mistakes appear, and the stress sometimes moves when the word changes form:

- *AN-a-lyse* (verb) and *a-NAL-y-sis* (noun)
- *PHO-to-graph* (noun), *pho-TOG-ra-phy* (noun) and *pho-to-GRAPH-ic* (adjective)
- *de-CIDE* (verb) and *de-CI-sion* (noun)
- *e-CON-o-my* (noun) and *ec-o-NOM-ic* (adjective)

Not every word shifts: *significant* and *significance* both keep the stress on the second syllable. The safest habit is to check the stress in a dictionary whenever you meet a long word, and to say it aloud once. The [English word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) article lists the common patterns, and the [IPA pronunciation guide](/en/blog/ipa-pronunciation-guide-for-english-learners/) helps with the sounds themselves. You can also build vocabulary with the [PTE academic vocabulary list](/en/blog/pte-academic-vocabulary-list/), then practise saying each word.

## What to do with a word you do not know

The text is short, but it often contains one or two long or rare words. Because every word counts for content, skipping the word is the worst option. Use the preparation time to say the word quietly in your head, split it into syllables, and decide which syllable is stressed. If you are still unsure when the recording starts, say your best attempt at normal volume and move on without going back. A confident attempt with a small stress error is better for both content and fluency than a long pause or a repeat.

## Intonation at commas and full stops

Pearson does not publish rules for pitch, but natural reading still follows ordinary English patterns, and they help your phrasing:

1. **Lists:** in a series such as *investors, researchers and policymakers*, let your voice stay up slightly on the first items and fall on the last one.
2. **Full stops:** let your voice fall at the end of a statement, so the listener hears that the sentence is finished.
3. **Commas:** a small lift or a level tone at a comma shows that more is coming. A flat, monotone reading sounds like reciting.

## Daily drills

1. **Shadowing:** play a short news clip and speak along with it a moment behind. The [shadowing technique](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) article shows how to do this step by step.
2. **Backward build-up:** if a long sentence feels like a tongue-twister, practise the last clause first, then add the clause before it, until you can read the whole sentence smoothly.
3. **Record and listen:** record yourself reading a short academic paragraph and listen for the stress in each long word.
4. **Breathing:** take a quick breath at commas and full stops so you do not run out of air in the middle of a long clause.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I use a British or American accent in Read Aloud?

Pearson does not ask for a particular accent; aim to be easy to understand. Copying an accent you do not use naturally often makes your vowels inconsistent, so keep your own voice and focus on clear stress and rhythm.

### What if I am still speaking when the progress bar ends?

Pearson advises finishing before the bar ends, and the recording time depends on how long the text is. Keep an even pace from the first word so you do not have to rush the last sentence.

### Does Read Aloud also count for Reading?

Yes. Pearson's task description says Read Aloud contributes to both Speaking and Reading, so a missed word costs you in more than one place.
