Reading a short paragraph aloud sounds simple, and it is the task where small habits show most clearly. **PTE read aloud pronunciation tips** are mainly about three things: stress the right syllables, group words into natural phrases, and keep a steady pace without restarts. You do not need a perfect accent. Pearson says PTE Academic recognises regional and national varieties of English pronunciation as long as they are understandable to most regular speakers of the language, so clear, consistent speech is the aim.

We checked Pearson's description of the task on 15 November 2026: a text of up to 60 words appears on screen, you read it aloud, and the scoring traits are content, pronunciation and oral fluency. Time to prepare and record varies with the item and with test updates, so confirm the current figures on Pearson's pages. This guide shows what to practise, and every exercise works with any text.

## What the read aloud task rewards

Three ideas to keep in mind:

1. **Content:** read the words in the text, all of them and only those. Skipping or adding words costs you, so do not improvise.
2. **Pronunciation:** sounds and stress that a listener can follow easily. It is not about sounding American or British.
3. **Oral fluency:** smooth rhythm and speed, with natural pauses, and without long hesitations, repeats or restarts.

Pronunciation and fluency go together: a word said with the wrong stress breaks the rhythm of the whole phrase. Scoring details, such as how heavily each trait counts, are set by Pearson and can change, so we do not quote weights here. Our guide is general speaking skill, not a promise about a score.

## Step 1: Read the line before you speak

You usually get a short moment to look at the text first. Use it for three quick scans, not to memorise.

{{diagram: d1 | A four-step routine for the preparation time: scan, mark, chunk, speak.}}

- **Scan for hard words.** Look for long words (*infrastructure*, *environmental*), numbers and unusual names. Decide how you will say them before the microphone starts.
- **Mark the commas and full stops.** They show where the natural pauses go.
- **Chunk the sentence.** Group words that belong together: *the effects of climate change / on coastal communities / are becoming clearer*. Phrases of three to six words sound more natural than word by word reading.
- **Choose a pace.** A calm, steady speed beats a fast one. Rushing is the most common cause of mistakes.

If you have no time, even the first two steps are worth it.

## Step 2: Stress the right syllables

English is a stress-timed language: some syllables are longer, louder and clearer, and others are short and weak. Learners whose first language gives each syllable equal weight often sound flat, which can hurt both clarity and fluency.

Most long words have one main stress. Learn a few patterns:

- Many two-syllable nouns and adjectives are stressed on the **first** syllable: *TAble, PREsent, HAPpy*.
- Many two-syllable verbs are stressed on the **second**: *preSENT, reLAX, deCIDE*.
- Words ending in *-tion, -sion, -ic, -ical, -ity* are stressed on the syllable **before** the ending: *eduCAtion, ecoNOMic, ciVILity*.
- Compound nouns usually stress the first part: *BLACKboard, GREENhouse*.

{{diagram: d2 | The same spelling can change stress and meaning between noun and verb. Say each pair aloud and tap on the stressed syllable.}}

Look up any word you are unsure about in a dictionary that shows the pronunciation and listen to it. Our guide to [English word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) explains the patterns in more detail, and the [IPA pronunciation guide](/en/blog/ipa-pronunciation-guide-for-english-learners/) helps you read the symbols in dictionary entries.

### Content words and function words

Within a sentence, stress the **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs) and say **function words** (*the, of, to, and, a, is*) quickly and weakly. Compare *The EFfects of CLImate CHANGE* with a robotic *The Ef-fects Of Cli-mate Change*. Weak forms, like /ðə/ for *the* and /əv/ for *of*, are part of natural rhythm. See [sentence stress](/en/blog/sentence-stress-in-english-speaking-guide/) for practice.

## Step 3: Pause and link like a native speaker

Pausing at the right place makes you sound fluent even when you are not fast.

- **Pause briefly at commas and full stops.** A short pause (a beat) is enough. Long silences count as hesitation.
- **Do not pause in the middle of a phrase.** *The new library / opens next week* is better than *The new / library opens / next week*.
- **Link words.** A final consonant often joins the next vowel: *pick it up* sounds like *pi-ki-tup*, *an apple* like *a-na-pple*. Our guide to [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/) explains the patterns.
- **Keep your final sounds.** Many learners drop the last consonant (*-s, -ed, -t*), which can change meaning. Our guide to [-ed endings](/en/blog/ed-pronunciation-rules/) shows the three sounds.

## Step 4: Handle hard words and mistakes calmly

Everyone stumbles in a test. What matters is what happens next.

- **Do not restart.** If you say a word wrongly, keep going at the same pace. A restart creates a repetition that damages fluency, and you may lose content words.
- **Keep your volume steady.** Speak clearly at a normal level and keep the microphone distance constant.
- **Do not whisper or mumble the end.** Finish the last phrase clearly, because the final words are easy to rush.
- **For unfamiliar words,** use the most likely stress and say it with confidence. A confident, smooth guess is better than a long stop.

## PTE read aloud pronunciation tips: a 15-minute daily routine

Consistency matters more than long sessions.

1. **Minute 0 to 3: warm up.** Read a short paragraph aloud slowly, exaggerating stress, to wake up your mouth.
2. **Minute 3 to 8: shadow.** Play a clear recording of a speaker and repeat after them, a phrase at a time. Our [shadowing technique guide](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) explains how.
3. **Minute 8 to 13: record yourself.** Read a text of about 60 words with your phone microphone, then listen. Note one thing to fix: a stress pattern, a dropped ending, or a long pause.
4. **Minute 13 to 15: fix one word.** Look up the word, listen to it, say it five times, then use it in a sentence.

Choose any texts of about 60 words: a news paragraph, a product description or an article summary that you write yourself. Using material that you write yourself avoids copyright issues and lets you control difficulty.

## Common pronunciation problems and how to fix them

| Problem | What it sounds like | Quick fix |
|---|---|---|
| Flat stress | Each syllable equal | Tap the stressed syllable and make it longer |
| Dropped endings | *wor* for *worked* | Say the final consonant, softly but clearly |
| Merged vowels | *ship* and *sheep* alike | Practise minimal pairs aloud |
| Word-by-word reading | Robotic rhythm | Chunk into phrases |
| Rushing | Fast, unclear | Slow down by 10 to 15 percent |

If you do not know which sound is hardest for you, record ten words with common problem sounds (for example *think*, *this*, *very*, *world*, *beach*) and compare them with a model. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a native accent for PTE read aloud?

No. Pearson says it recognises regional and national varieties of English pronunciation as long as they are understandable to most regular speakers of the language. Clear stress, steady rhythm and complete words matter more than accent.

### How long should I pause at a comma?

A short natural pause, about a beat. Long silences look like hesitation, and rushing past commas makes the meaning harder to follow.

### What if I do not know how to say a word?

Use the most likely stress pattern and say it smoothly. Practise by looking up unknown words and listening to them in a dictionary regularly.

### Can I practise without a teacher?

Yes. Record yourself, listen back, compare with a model, and fix one thing at a time. Shadowing recordings of clear speakers helps rhythm and stress.
