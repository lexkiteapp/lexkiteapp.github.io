Should you say *I enjoy to read* or *I enjoy reading*? The second one. With gerunds and infinitives, the short answer is this: some verbs are followed by the *-ing* form (the gerund), some by *to* + base verb (the infinitive), and a few take both, sometimes with a change in meaning. You do need to learn the common verbs, but there is a logic underneath that makes most of them predictable.

The infinitive tends to look **forward**, towards something not yet done. The gerund tends to look at an action as a **real experience or activity**, something already happening, already done, or treated as a general idea. Once you see this, lists stop feeling random.

## What gerunds and infinitives are

A **gerund** is the *-ing* form of a verb used like a noun: *Swimming is good exercise.* *She's thinking about changing jobs.*

A **to-infinitive** is *to* + the base form: *I want to leave early.* *It's hard to explain.* There is also the **bare infinitive** without *to*, used after modal verbs (*can go*), after *make* and *let* (*They made me wait*), and after *would rather* and *had better*.

The confusion comes when a verb is followed by another verb. English needs to decide the shape of that second verb, and the choice is fixed by the first verb, not by your intuition. Learners who speak languages with no verb inflection, such as Chinese, Vietnamese or Thai, often leave the second verb bare (*I enjoy read*). Speakers of Spanish, Portuguese, French or Arabic often pick the infinitive by default, because their own languages use an infinitive-type form after most verbs (*I enjoy to read*, modelled on Spanish *me gusta leer* or French *j'aime lire*). Both patterns are worth watching for.

## The core logic: future vs experience

Look at these pairs and notice the time direction.

| Infinitive (forward-looking) | Gerund (experience / activity) |
|---|---|
| I **want to** study abroad. | I **enjoy** studying with friends. |
| She **decided to** quit. | She **admitted** taking the money. |
| We **plan to** launch in May. | We **finished** testing on Friday. |
| He **promised to** call. | He **denied** calling her. |

*Want, decide, plan, hope, promise, agree, refuse, expect, intend* all point at an action that has not happened yet. That matches the infinitive. *Enjoy, finish, admit, deny, avoid, mind, practise, keep, miss, imagine* relate to an action you are doing, have done, or are picturing as an activity. That matches the gerund.

The logic is a tendency, not a law. *Avoid* refers to something that has not happened, yet it takes a gerund. That is why you still need a short list. But the logic will help you guess correctly far more often than chance.

## Verbs that change meaning: stop, remember, try and friends

This is where the future-vs-experience idea earns its keep.

**Remember / forget**
- *Remember to lock the door.* (the locking comes **after** the remembering: future)
- *I remember locking the door.* (the locking happened **before**; you have a memory of it)
- *Don't forget to email Priya.* vs *I'll never forget meeting her.*

**Stop**
- *He stopped smoking.* (the smoking ended)
- *He stopped to smoke.* (he stopped walking or working **in order to** smoke)

**Try**
- *Try to lift the box.* (make an effort; it may be difficult)
- *Try restarting the router.* (do it as an experiment and see if it solves the problem)

**Regret**
- *I regret telling him.* (I told him, and I'm sorry)
- *We regret to inform you that…* (formal: I'm sorry about what I'm about to say)

**Go on**
- *She went on talking for an hour.* (continued the same activity)
- *After the intro, she went on to talk about costs.* (moved to the next thing)

**Mean**
- *I meant to call you.* (I intended to: the call was in the future)
- *Taking the job means moving to Leeds.* (it involves this; the moving is part of the situation)

**Need** (British and American English both use this)
- *I need to clean the car.* (I have to do it)
- *The car needs cleaning.* (passive meaning: the car needs to be cleaned)

Notice how every infinitive version points forward from the main verb, and every gerund version treats the action as something real or ongoing.

## Where you must use the gerund

### After prepositions

Any verb that follows a preposition takes the *-ing* form. No exceptions.

- *She's good **at** solving puzzles.*
- *Thanks **for** helping.*
- *I'm tired **of** waiting.*
- *How **about** meeting on Thursday?*

The famous trap is that *to* is sometimes a preposition, not part of an infinitive. In these phrases *to* is a preposition, so a gerund follows:

- *I look forward **to hearing** from you.* (not *to hear*)
- *I'm used **to working** late.* (= accustomed to)
- *She's committed **to improving** the service.*
- *He objected **to paying** twice.*
- *When it comes **to cooking**, I'm hopeless.*

A quick test: if you can put a noun after *to* (*I look forward to the weekend*), then *to* is a preposition, and a verb there must be a gerund.

### After a few fixed expressions

Learn these as chunks, because no general rule produces them: *It's no use complaining. It's worth checking. I can't help laughing. There's no point waiting. I have difficulty concentrating. She spent the weekend revising.*

### As the subject of a sentence

When an action is the subject, the gerund is the natural choice: *Learning a language takes time.* An infinitive subject (*To learn a language takes time*) is grammatical but sounds formal or literary. In everyday and exam writing, prefer the gerund or the *it* pattern: *It takes time to learn a language.*

## Where you must use the infinitive

- **Purpose**: *I came here **to study** engineering.* Never *for study* or *for studying* when you mean someone's purpose. This is one of the most common errors among learners from South Asia and the Philippines (*I went to the bank for withdrawing money*).
- **After adjectives**: *happy to help, easy to use, difficult to understand, ready to start.*
- **After *too* and *enough***: *too tired to drive, old enough to vote.*
- **After question words**: *I don't know **how to fix** it. Tell me **what to do**.*
- **Verb + object + infinitive**: *ask, tell, want, allow, encourage, remind, advise someone **to do** something.*

### Perception verbs: a third option

After *see, hear, watch, notice* and *feel* + object, English uses either the **bare infinitive** or the **-ing** form, and the choice changes what you saw:

- *I saw her **cross** the road.* (the whole action, start to finish)
- *I saw her **crossing** the road.* (part of the action, in progress)

The same split explains *I heard the phone **ring*** (one complete ring) versus *I heard the phone **ringing*** (it went on for a while). In the passive, *to* returns: *She was seen **to leave** at midnight.*

### The suggest and recommend trap

*Suggest* and *recommend* never take an object + infinitive.

- Wrong: *She suggested me to apply.*
- Right: *She suggested applying.* / *She suggested (that) I apply.* / *She suggested that I should apply.*

The same goes for *recommend*: *I recommend booking early* or *I recommend that you book early*. This error appears constantly in emails and IELTS essays, partly because many languages build this sentence with an infinitive.

## Verbs that take both with little change in meaning

*Like, love, hate, prefer, begin, start, continue* can take either form.

- *It started raining / to rain.* (no real difference)
- *I like cooking* (I enjoy it as an activity) vs *I like to cook at home on weekdays* (it's my habit or choice, often because it's sensible). The difference is small, and both are correct in most contexts.

Two details: after *would like, would love, would prefer, would hate*, use the infinitive (*I'd like to book a table*). And avoid two *-ing* forms in a row: *It's starting to rain* sounds better than *It's starting raining*.

## Verb groups to learn, not lists to cram

Memorising long alphabetical lists rarely works. Learn verbs in meaning groups and attach one example sentence to each group.

| Group | Verbs | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Plans and decisions | want, plan, decide, hope, expect, intend, aim | to-infinitive |
| Promises and refusals | promise, agree, refuse, offer, threaten | to-infinitive |
| Enjoying and disliking | enjoy, mind, can't stand, don't mind, fancy (UK) | gerund |
| Finishing and avoiding | finish, quit, avoid, postpone, put off, delay | gerund |
| Saying what happened | admit, deny, mention, report, recall | gerund |
| Continuing | keep, carry on, practise, risk | gerund |
| Changing meaning | remember, forget, stop, try, regret, go on, need | both |

## Self-check

Choose the correct form.

1. I'm looking forward to (see / seeing) you next week.
2. She refused (to sign / signing) the contract.
3. Please remember (to send / sending) the invoice before Friday.
4. My manager suggested (me to take / taking) a short break.
5. We stopped (to buy / buying) coffee on the way, so we were late.
6. He went to the library (for borrowing / to borrow) a book.
7. Have you tried (to switch / switching) it off and on again?
8. I don't mind (to wait / waiting) a few minutes.

### Answers and explanations

1. **seeing**: *to* is a preposition in *look forward to*.
2. **to sign**: *refuse* points at a future action you won't do.
3. **to send**: the sending comes after the remembering.
4. **taking**: *suggest* never takes object + infinitive.
5. **to buy**: they stopped in order to buy coffee (purpose).
6. **to borrow**: purpose uses the infinitive, not *for* + *-ing*.
7. **switching**: an experiment to solve a problem.
8. **waiting**: *mind* is in the gerund group.

If you missed 4 or 6, look at your own writing for the same pattern. These two errors are typical of fluent intermediate learners and they are easy to fix once you see them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a rule that works without memorising?

Not completely. The future-vs-experience logic predicts most common verbs, but some, like *avoid* or *postpone*, break it. Use the logic to guess, then learn the 30 or so most frequent verbs as fixed chunks.

### What is the difference between "like doing" and "like to do"?

Very little in most contexts. *Like doing* often stresses enjoyment of the activity; *like to do* can suggest a habit or a choice you think is sensible. In American English *like to do* is slightly more common. Both are correct.

### Why does "look forward to" take -ing?

Because *to* in this phrase is a preposition, not part of an infinitive. You can say *I look forward to the holidays*, with a noun after *to*. Any verb after a preposition must be a gerund, so *I look forward to seeing you* is correct.

### Do gerund and infinitive mistakes affect IELTS scores?

They can. Errors in verb patterns count under Grammatical Range and Accuracy in both Writing and Speaking. One slip will not change a band, but repeated errors such as *suggest me to* or *for doing* to express purpose make your accuracy look weaker.
