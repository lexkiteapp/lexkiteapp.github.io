A strong **tell me about yourself interview answer** has three parts: what you do now (present), how you got here (past), and why you want this job (future). Aim for about 60 to 90 seconds, keep it professional, and finish by linking your story to the role. You are not reading your whole CV aloud; you are choosing the three or four facts that make a hiring manager think "this person fits".

This guide gives you the formula, three complete sample answers for different backgrounds, a list of what to avoid, and the wording for variations such as *introduce yourself* and *walk me through your resume*. If you want the wider list of interview questions and the STAR method for behavioural ones, see our guides to [job interview questions and answers](/en/blog/job-interview-questions-and-answers-in-english/) and the [STAR method in English interviews](/en/blog/star-method-in-english-interviews-guide/). Here we go deeper on this one opening question, because it sets the tone for everything after it.

## What interviewers really want when they say "Tell me about yourself"

It sounds personal, but it is a work question. The interviewer usually has three silent questions:

1. Can this person communicate clearly and calmly in English?
2. Does their background make sense for this role?
3. Did they prepare, or will they ramble?

So the best answer is not your life story, your family, or your hobbies (a single short personal line at the end is fine). It is a **tailored career summary**. Two candidates can say true things about themselves, but the one who selects the details that match the job description wins.

Before you write anything, open the job post and underline three keywords: skills, tools, or responsibilities. Your answer should echo at least two of them.

## The Present–Past–Future formula

{{diagram: d1 | A simple structure with timings. Practise until it lasts about 75 seconds when spoken at a normal pace.}}

**Present (about 20 seconds).** Say your current role or situation, and one thing you are good at.

- *Currently, I'm a customer support specialist at a software company, where I handle about…* (avoid exact numbers unless you can prove them)
- *At the moment, I'm finishing my degree in…*
- *I work as a project coordinator, mainly supporting…*

**Past (about 30 seconds).** Give two or three steps that explain how you reached the present, with one achievement. Keep to the relevant ones.

- *Before that, I spent three years at… where I…*
- *I started out in… and later moved into…*
- *One thing I'm proud of is…*

**Future (about 20 seconds).** Say what you want next and why this company and role fit.

- *I'm now looking for a role where I can…*
- *What attracted me to this position is…*
- *I'd love to bring my experience in… to your team.*

Tenses matter here. Use the present simple or continuous for now (*I work*, *I'm studying*), the past simple for finished experiences (*I managed*, *I moved*), the present perfect for experience that connects to now (*I've worked on…*), and *would like to* / *I'm looking to* for goals. If those tenses are shaky, review [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/).

## Three sample tell me about yourself interview answer models

These are models, not scripts. Change the details so they are true for you.

### 1. Recent graduate (no full-time experience yet)

> *Thanks for having me. I've just graduated from university with a degree in business and marketing, and I'm now looking for my first full-time role in digital marketing. During my degree I ran the social media account for a student society, which grew our event attendance, and I completed a three-month internship at a small design agency, where I helped schedule posts and prepare monthly reports. I enjoyed the analytical side most, so I taught myself the basics of spreadsheets and a free analytics tool. I'm excited about this position because your team works on campaigns for local brands, and I'd like to learn from experienced colleagues while contributing fresh ideas.*

Why it works: it names the degree, shows two real activities, shows initiative (*taught myself*), and ends with the role. A graduate does not need to apologise for lack of experience (*I only have…* is weak). Say what you did, not what you lack.

### 2. Career changer (new field)

> *Currently, I'm a secondary school teacher, and I've spent six years explaining complex ideas clearly to different audiences, planning lessons with tight deadlines, and using data to track progress. Over the past year I've been moving towards a career in training and development: I designed an online course for new teachers at my school and completed a certificate in instructional design. I'm now looking for a learning designer role. What interests me about your company is that it builds training for healthcare staff, and I'd like to use my classroom experience to make learning materials clear and practical.*

Why it works: it turns the old job into **transferable skills** (explaining, planning, data), gives proof of the switch (a course, a certificate), and states a clear reason. For career changers, the "past" part is a bridge, not an apology.

### 3. Experienced professional

> *I'm a senior accountant with about nine years of experience, currently leading a small team in a manufacturing company. My focus is month-end reporting and process improvement; last year I helped our team shorten the closing process by introducing a clearer checklist and automating some manual steps. Before that, I worked in audit, which gave me a strong foundation in controls and compliance. I'm now looking for a role with more responsibility in finance business partnering, and your position stood out because it works directly with department heads to guide decisions.*

Why it works: it opens with the headline (title, years, scope), proves value with a result, and links the past to the future. Notice that it says *about nine years* and uses facts the speaker can confirm. If you give a number, be ready to explain it.

Each model is roughly 100 to 130 words, which takes about 45 to 60 seconds at a calm pace. Add one more detail if you want to reach 75 seconds. If yours runs past 90 seconds, cut detail from the middle, not from the opening and closing.

## How long should it be, and how to sound natural

**Timing.** Aim for 60 to 90 seconds. Under 30 seconds sounds unprepared; over two minutes loses the listener. Practise with a timer on your phone.

**Natural, not memorised.** A memorised paragraph often sounds flat and breaks if you forget one word. Instead, memorise the **structure** and about five key phrases, then say it differently each time.

1. Write your answer as **bullet points**, not full sentences: *current role, two achievements, why this job*.
2. Say it aloud five or six times without reading. Your wording will change, and that is a good sign.
3. Use **short sentences** and a pause at each full stop. Long sentences make you run out of breath and sound nervous.
4. Use *connectors* that sound spoken: *So…*, *Before that…*, *What I enjoy most is…*, *That's why…*.
5. Smile, speak a little slower than normal, and finish with a clear falling tone, so the interviewer knows you have finished.

Try recording yourself and listen for fillers (*um*, *like*, *you know*). A short pause is better than a filler. If you freeze, a safe recovery phrase is *Let me put it another way.*

## What to avoid

1. **Reading your CV line by line.** The interviewer already has it. Add the story behind the lines.
2. **Starting too far back.** *I was born in a small town, then I went to primary school…* wastes your time. Start with the present.
3. **Too personal.** Family, relationship status, religion, health and similar topics are not needed. One line about a hobby is enough if it adds something (*I run on weekends, which keeps me focused*).
4. **Negative comments about a past employer.** Even if true, they make you sound difficult. Say what you want *next*, not what you ran away from.
5. **Vague claims.** *I'm a hard worker and a team player* proves nothing. Give a short example: *I coordinated a team of four to deliver the project a week early.*
6. **Over-claiming.** Never invent numbers or titles; interviewers may check, and you must be able to talk about every detail.
7. **Apologising for your English.** A short *Please let me know if you'd like me to repeat anything* is fine; repeated apologies are not.

Rules differ by country. In the US, a confident, achievement-focused tone is common. In the UK, a slightly more modest tone with *I'm proud of…* works better than *I'm the best*. In Germany, Japan and Korea, interviews can be more formal and may expect precise facts about education and roles. When unsure, stay factual, polite, and specific.

## Variations of the question

The question comes in different shapes, but the answer structure is the same.

{{diagram: d2 | Four common wordings and the opening that fits each. The Present–Past–Future structure works for all of them.}}

- **"Tell me about yourself."** Use the full Present–Past–Future answer.
- **"Introduce yourself."** Often used at the start or in a group or panel interview. Begin with a friendly line: *Thanks for having me. I'm Linh, and I'm a…* Keep it shorter (40 to 60 seconds).
- **"Walk me through your resume."** The interviewer wants the **order and the reasons**. Go from earliest to latest and explain each move: *I started out in…, then I moved to… because…*
- **"What brings you here today?" / "Why are you interested in this role?"** Lead with the future part: *I'm looking for a role where I can…* then add one proof from the past.
- **"Tell me something that's not on your resume."** Share a relevant strength or project, such as a volunteer project, a skill you taught yourself, or a language you speak.

A short dialogue shows how a follow-up might sound:

> **Interviewer:** Thanks for coming in. Could you start by telling me a little about yourself?
>
> **Candidate:** Of course. I'm currently a logistics coordinator at a retail company, where I manage deliveries for about twenty stores. Before that, I worked in a warehouse, so I understand the operation from the ground up. I'm now looking to move into planning, and your role stood out because it combines data and day-to-day coordination.
>
> **Interviewer:** Interesting. What did you enjoy most about the warehouse work?
>
> **Candidate:** Seeing how a small delay at one point affects everything else. That's what made me want to work on planning.

Notice how the candidate's answer leaves a natural hook (*warehouse*, *planning*) that invites the next question. A good opener steers the interview toward your strengths.

## Self-check: improve these answers

1. Which opening is better: *I was born in 1998 in…* or *Currently, I work as…*?
2. Correct the sentence: *I have worked in marketing since three years.*
3. Which is better to say about a former boss: *He was terrible* or *I'm looking for more room to grow*?
4. The interviewer says *Walk me through your resume.* Should you read it word for word?
5. Your answer is 3 minutes long. What should you cut first?
6. Complete the last sentence of an answer: *That's why I'm excited about this ____ at your company.*

**Answers**

1. *Currently, I work as…* Start with the present and keep to the work story.
2. *I have worked in marketing for three years.* (Use *for* with a period of time, *since* with a starting point such as *since 2021*.)
3. *I'm looking for more room to grow.* Focus on your goals and avoid blaming people.
4. No. Give the order of your career and the reason behind each change; the details are on the page.
5. Cut minor details, early jobs not related to the role, and any repeated information. Keep the opening line and the closing link to the job.
6. *role* (or *position* or *opportunity*). End with a clear link to the job.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should my answer to "Tell me about yourself" be?

Aim for 60 to 90 seconds, about 130 to 200 words at a calm speaking pace. If the interviewer wants more detail they will ask follow-up questions.

### Should I include personal information like hobbies or family?

Keep it mostly professional. One short personal line can make you memorable if it relates to the job or shows a useful quality, but avoid family, relationship, religion and health topics.

### I have no work experience. What do I say?

Use the same formula with study, projects, volunteering, internships or part-time work. Say what you did, what you learned, and why the role fits. Showing real initiative is more convincing than apologising for what you lack.

### Should I memorise my answer?

Memorise the structure and a few key phrases rather than every word. A memorised script often sounds flat and can fall apart if you forget a line. Practise it aloud until you can say it in your own words each time.
