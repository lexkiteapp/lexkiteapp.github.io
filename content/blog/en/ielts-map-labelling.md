**IELTS map labelling** is a Listening task in which you hear someone describe a place, such as a campus, a museum or a leisure centre, and you label spots on a map or plan. It usually appears in Section 2 and sometimes in Section 1 or 3. The task does not test whether you know the place. It tests whether you can follow directions in real time while looking at a picture. Candidates lose marks on small words: *opposite*, *beside*, *past*, *left*, *right*. This guide gives a method, a worked example and a plan for practice.

## How IELTS map labelling works

You see a map or plan with some places already named and others marked with letters. The questions ask you to match place names (a gym, a café, a library) to the letters. You hear a speaker walking you through the map, and the questions follow the route. As in all Listening tasks, the recording is played once. For the other task types, see [IELTS Listening question types](/en/blog/ielts-listening-question-types/).

## Use the preview time to orient yourself

{{diagram: d2 | Orient first; then follow the route like a walk}}

You get about 30 seconds to read the questions before the recording. Use this time to:

1. **Find the starting point**, usually an entrance, a gate or a reception desk.
2. **Check the compass** and the roads or paths. Note any one-way arrows.
3. **Read the labelled places**, because the speaker will use them as landmarks.
4. **Notice the letters** you must label and their rough positions.
5. **Look at the list of places** you must match, and read them quickly. If a place is on your list, you will hear its name.

The speaker usually starts at the entrance and walks to the places in order. If you are at the starting point on the map, you will not get lost.

## Direction language

{{diagram: d1 | Small words decide the answer, so learn them as pairs}}

Beyond the phrases in the table, you will hear:

- **Movement:** *go straight on, turn left, turn right, keep going, cross the road, follow the path.*
- **Position:** *on your left, on your right, in front of you, behind, in the corner, at the far end.*
- **Distance:** *a short way along, about halfway, just before, just after.*
- **Order:** *first, then, after that, finally.*

Directions are almost always given from the walker's point of view. *On your left* means on the left of the person walking, not the left side of the page. Turn the map in your head: imagine you are walking along the route.

## A worked example

Read the script as if you were listening. A small leisure centre has the entrance at the bottom of the plan. The speaker says:

*"When you come in through the main entrance, you'll find reception straight ahead. If you turn left and walk along the corridor, the first room on your right is the changing area. Continue past it, and the café is at the end of the corridor, next to the windows. Now, if you go back to reception and turn right instead, you will see the swimming pool straight ahead. The gym is on the left of the pool, and the shop is directly opposite it."*

Questions: *Where is the changing area? Where is the café? Where is the gym?*

- **Changing area:** after the left turn, the first room on the right.
- **Café:** at the end of the corridor, beside the windows, not in the middle.
- **Gym:** to the left of the pool as you face it from reception, with the shop directly across from it.

Notice three traps in this short extract. *Past* tells you to go beyond the changing area. *At the end* means the far point of the corridor. *Opposite* says that the gym and the shop face each other across a space, not that they touch.

## Traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | What happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Left and right | You choose the side as it appears on the page | Imagine you are walking the route |
| Opposite and next to | You treat them as the same | Draw a small arrow when you hear opposite |
| Self-correction | *The cafe is on the left... sorry, the right* | Wait for the final statement |
| Several places mentioned | The speaker names three buildings in one sentence | Keep your pencil on the map |
| Lost position | You miss one direction | Jump to the next landmark you can find |
| Distractors | The speaker says where a place used to be | Listen for *now* and *used to* |

If you miss one landmark, do not stop. Find the next one the speaker mentions; it may help you place the lost location afterwards.

## What examiners are really testing

Map labelling is a test of attention, not of geography. The recording uses everyday language, but the speaker never says *the café is at letter C*. Instead, you must connect a description to a picture. That is why candidates with good general English still lose marks here: they listen to words but forget to look at the plan. A good habit is to keep your eyes on the map for the whole recording, using the pencil as a pointer. Treat the speaker as a guide on a short walk. If you can follow a friend's directions in a new city, you can follow this task, and the practice below builds exactly that skill.

## Skills to practise outside the exam

1. **Describe your route.** Explain how to walk from your front door to a nearby shop in 60 seconds. Use *past*, *opposite*, *between*.
2. **Draw and check.** Ask a friend to describe a simple map while you draw it.
3. **Listen for prepositions.** In English-language directions and videos, listen only for location words.
4. **Learn the vocabulary of buildings.** Words such as *reception*, *car park*, *lecture theatre*, *gallery* and *entrance hall* appear again and again. You can find definitions in the dictionary, for example [reception](/app/#/dict/reception).

To improve your listening speed more generally, read [how to catch fast speech](/en/blog/how-to-catch-fast-speech-in-ielts-listening/) and [IELTS Listening tips](/en/blog/ielts-listening-tips/). For prepositions in writing as well as speaking, the blog guide to [in, on, at](/en/blog/prepositions-in-on-at/) is useful.

## A one-week practice plan

| Day | Practice |
|---|---|
| 1 | Learn direction phrases; describe your own route aloud |
| 2 | Listen to one map task; trace the route with a pencil |
| 3 | Review errors and note the small words you missed |
| 4 | Do a second map task; add the vocabulary to your list |
| 5 | Practise a full Section 2 with map and multiple choice |
| 6 | Repeat your weakest direction words with a friend |
| 7 | Do a timed section; review every error |

## Frequently asked questions

### In which section does map labelling appear?

It is most common in Section 2, but plans and maps can also appear in other sections. The question types vary from test to test.

### How do I avoid left and right mistakes?

Imagine you are walking through the map from the entrance. Directions are normally given from the walker's point of view, not from the page.

### What if I miss a direction?

Move on and find the next landmark. Staying on one missed answer can cost you the next as well.

### Do I need to know the places mentioned?

No. All the information you need is on the map or in the recording. Prepare vocabulary for common buildings and facilities, though.
