The **TOEFL Academic Discussion** task asks you to write a short post for an online class forum: a professor asks a question, two classmates reply, and you add your own view in about 10 minutes. Most test takers can write 100 words; far fewer write a post that actually *moves the discussion forward*. That is the skill this guide trains, with a structure you can memorise, a model answer and a plan for each minute.

## What the TOEFL Academic Discussion task looks like

Since the new format arrived on 21 January 2026, Writing contains three task types: Build a Sentence, Write an Email, and Write for an Academic Discussion. Our overview of the [new TOEFL format 2026](/en/blog/new-toefl-ibt-format-2026/) covers the whole test; here we focus on the discussion post.

On screen you see three things:

1. **The professor's post.** A short paragraph that introduces a topic from a university course (education, technology, business, environment, sociology) and ends with a direct question.
2. **Two student replies.** Each classmate gives a view with a reason. Usually they disagree, or approach the question from different angles.
3. **Your box.** You have about 10 minutes, and the instructions ask for at least 100 words.

![](media:@s1 "The practice task shows the professor's question and two classmates' posts, then gives you a timed box for your reply")

The test is not asking for an essay. There is no title, no formal introduction and no conclusion paragraph. Think of a smart, polite student writing on a course forum: clear, direct and specific.

## What raters reward (and what they ignore)

A good post does three jobs at once.

- **It answers the question.** If the professor asks "Should cities ban cars from the centre?", your first sentence should say yes, no, or "only under certain conditions". Do not make the reader wait.
- **It contributes something new.** This is the heart of the task. If a classmate has already said "cars cause pollution", repeating that idea in different words does not count as a contribution. You need a reason, angle or example that is not on the screen yet.
- **It is written in clear, accurate English.** Variety helps, but accuracy matters more than rare vocabulary. A 120-word post with clean grammar beats a 180-word post full of errors.

What matters much less: length beyond the minimum, a dramatic introduction, and "impressive" words that you are not sure how to use. Our article on [the sophistication trap in IELTS writing](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) explains why forced vocabulary hurts more than it helps, and the same logic applies here.

## The four-move structure for a strong TOEFL Academic Discussion post

Use the same skeleton every time, so all your attention goes to *ideas* rather than organisation.

{{diagram: d1 | Five short moves: say where you stand, react to a classmate, add the new reason, prove it with one example, close in one line}}

### Move 1: Position (one sentence)

State your answer plainly. *I believe universities should require every student to complete an internship before graduation.* Avoid "There are many advantages and disadvantages", which says nothing.

### Move 2: Link to a classmate (one or two sentences)

Show that you have read the discussion. Name one student and react:

- *Mei makes a fair point that internships can delay graduation, but…*
- *I agree with Tom that employers value experience; however, I would go further…*
- *Unlike Mei, I do not think cost is the main obstacle.*

This move is short. Its purpose is to set up your contribution, not to summarise.

### Move 3: Your new reason (two or three sentences)

Give one reason that neither classmate has mentioned, and explain *why* it is true. One well-developed reason is stronger than three that are only listed.

### Move 4: A concrete example (two or three sentences)

Make the reason real. A specific situation (a student, a company, a city, a common experience) turns a general claim into evidence. Invented personal examples are fine; nobody checks whether your cousin really did an internship.

### Optional close (one sentence)

*For these reasons, a required internship would benefit students far more than it would cost them.* Keep it to one line, or skip it if you are short of time.

## A model TOEFL Academic Discussion answer

**Professor (Dr. Rivera):** *Many universities now offer internships, but few make them compulsory. Should universities require all students to complete an internship before they graduate? Why or why not?*

**Mei:** *I don't think internships should be required. Some students have to work part-time to pay their fees, and an unpaid internship would make graduation even harder for them.*

**Tom:** *I support the idea. Employers often say graduates lack experience, so an internship would make students more competitive in the job market.*

**Model post (about 140 words):**

> I believe universities should require an internship, but only if they make it paid or offer credit-bearing options. Mei raises an important concern about cost, and I think it can be solved rather than used as a reason to reject the idea. What neither post mentions is that internships help students choose the right career *before* it is too late to change. Many students spend four years preparing for a job they have never actually seen. For example, a friend of mine studied accounting for three years, completed a short internship, and realised she disliked working alone with spreadsheets all day. Because the internship came in her third year, she still had time to add courses in marketing. Without it, she might have discovered this only after accepting her first full-time job. A required internship would therefore save students time, not just improve their CVs.

Notice what the post does. It takes a position in the first sentence, links to Mei with fair language, adds a new reason (career choice, which is neither cost nor competitiveness), proves it with one clear example and closes in one line. The language is plain and accurate, with a few useful structures: *what neither post mentions is that…*, *only if…*, *without it, she might have…*.

## A 10-minute plan

| Time | What you do |
|---|---|
| 0:00–1:00 | Read the professor's question twice. Skim both classmates and note their two reasons in two words each. |
| 1:00–2:00 | Choose your position and find the *third* reason, one that is different from both. Pick one example. |
| 2:00–8:30 | Write the four moves. Do not stop to perfect a sentence; keep moving. |
| 8:30–10:00 | Check verb forms, articles, plural -s and spelling. Delete any sentence that repeats a classmate. |

If you cannot think of a new reason in one minute, run through four "idea lenses": **money**, **time**, **health or well-being**, and **long-term effects on society**. One of them almost always gives you an angle the classmates missed.

## Five mistakes that cost points

1. **Echoing a classmate.** "I agree with Tom because experience is important for jobs" adds nothing. Agree with Tom, then add something Tom did not say.
2. **Listing without developing.** Three reasons with one sentence each read like a shopping list. Develop one.
3. **Essay templates.** Openings such as "In this modern era, technology has changed every aspect of our lives" waste words and signal memorised text.
4. **Over-hedging.** Some caution is academic (*this may suggest*, *in many cases*), as our guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) explains, but a post where every claim is "maybe possibly" has no position.
5. **Running out of time.** If you are at minute 8 without an example, write a one-sentence example and finish. A complete short post beats an unfinished long one.

## Useful language for discussion posts

Keep a small, reliable set of phrases rather than a long list you never use:

- **Taking a position:** *I believe…*, *In my view…*, *I would argue that…*
- **Reacting to a classmate:** *Mei raises a fair point about…*, *I see why Tom thinks…, but…*
- **Adding something new:** *What has not been mentioned yet is…*, *Another factor worth considering is…*
- **Giving an example:** *For instance…*, *A good example is…*, *This is exactly what happened when…*
- **Showing a result:** *As a result…*, *This means that…*, *Therefore…*

For more ways to connect ideas without sounding mechanical, see [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/); the advice transfers directly to TOEFL. If you tend to copy the professor's wording, [how to paraphrase](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) gives you a quick system for restating a question.

## Self-check

Decide whether each opening is **strong** (S) or **weak** (W) for a post on the question "Should high schools teach personal finance?"

1. *Personal finance is a topic that many people have different opinions about.*
2. *Yes, and I would make it a required course in the final year of high school.*
3. *I agree with both of my classmates that this is an important question.*
4. *Unlike Sara, I think the biggest benefit is not saving money but avoiding debt.*
5. *Nowadays, in the modern world, money is very important for everyone.*

### Answers

1. **W** – it has no position and could open any post.
2. **S** – it gives a clear answer and adds a detail (required, final year).
3. **W** – agreeing with "both" while adding nothing is filler.
4. **S** – it links to a classmate and introduces a new angle in one sentence.
5. **W** – a generic template opening with no answer.

If you marked 4 or 5 correctly, your instinct for openings is sound. Next, practise Move 3, finding the new reason, since that is where most marks are won or lost.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many words should a TOEFL Academic Discussion post be?

The instructions ask for at least 100 words. Most strong posts land between 120 and 160 words. Going far beyond that rarely helps, because time spent adding words is time not spent checking accuracy or sharpening your new idea.

### Can I disagree with both classmates?

Yes. You can disagree with both, agree with one, or take a middle position. What matters is that your post answers the professor's question and adds a reason that is not already on the screen.

### Is it acceptable to use personal examples?

Yes. Personal or invented examples are normal in this task, as long as they are specific and clearly support your reason. A short story about one student or one company is more convincing than a vague claim about "many people".

### Should I write a conclusion?

A one-line close is helpful when you have time, but it is optional. Never write a separate concluding paragraph that repeats everything; with only 10 minutes, that space is better used for your example.
