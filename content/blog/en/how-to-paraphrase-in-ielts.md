Imagine the Task 2 prompt reads: *Some people say that working from home is better for employees than working in an office.* A candidate opens with exactly the same sentence, word for word. The examiner learns nothing about that candidate's vocabulary from it. Knowing how to paraphrase in IELTS means saying the same idea in different words without changing its meaning, and it is one of the few skills that every part of the test relies on. Below you will find five practical techniques, original examples for each, the mistakes that quietly change your meaning, and a short exercise with answers.

## Why paraphrasing matters in every IELTS paper

Paraphrasing is usually taught as a Writing trick, but it runs through the whole exam.

- **Writing.** The introduction to Academic Task 1 and to Task 2 normally restates the question in your own words. In the body, you need varied wording so that you do not repeat the same noun ten times. Both habits feed into Lexical Resource, one of the four criteria.
- **Reading.** Questions rarely copy the passage. A text might say *fewer people are choosing to drive to work*, while the question asks about *a fall in car commuting*. True/False/Not Given and Matching Headings are, in large part, tests of whether you can spot this. Our guide to [True/False/Not Given questions](/en/blog/ielts-reading-true-false-not-given/) shows how often the trap is a paraphrase that looks similar but means something different.
- **Listening.** The speaker seldom says the exact words printed on your question paper. If you wait for those words, the answer may pass you by.
- **Speaking.** When a word escapes you mid-sentence, describing it another way keeps you talking, which protects your Fluency and Coherence score.

So paraphrasing has two directions. You **produce** it in Writing and Speaking, and you **recognise** it in Reading and Listening. Practising one direction strengthens the other, because both depend on the same mental store of synonyms, word families and sentence patterns.

## How to paraphrase in IELTS: five core techniques

Each technique below comes with an original example. Notice that none of them changes the meaning; they only change the form.

### Technique 1: Use a synonym or near-synonym

Replace a word or phrase with one that means the same thing in that context.

- *employees* → *staff, workers*
- *better for* → *more beneficial to, of greater benefit to*
- *a lot of* → *a considerable amount of, a large number of*

Original: *Some people say that working from home is better for employees than working in an office.*
Rewritten: *It is sometimes claimed that remote work is more beneficial to staff than office-based work.*

A warning: true synonyms are rare. *Employees* and *colleagues* are not interchangeable, and *home* is not the same as *house* in this sentence. Check the meaning, the register and the grammar that follows each new word before you swap it in.

### Technique 2: Change the word form

Turn a verb into a noun, an adjective into an adverb, or the other way round. This often forces the sentence to reorganise itself, which makes the paraphrase look natural rather than mechanical.

- *Fuel prices rose sharply last year.* → *Last year saw a sharp rise in fuel prices.*
- *Cities should develop cycle lanes.* → *The development of cycle lanes should be a priority for cities.*
- *Most visitors were satisfied with the service.* → *Visitor satisfaction with the service was high.*

This technique is especially useful in Task 1, where you describe the same kind of change several times. If you are working on charts, the guide to [IELTS Writing Task 1 line graphs](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) has more verb and noun pairs for trends. To use word forms accurately, learn the whole family: *develop, development, developer, developing*.

### Technique 3: Change the sentence structure

Keep the idea but rebuild the frame: move a clause, swap a linking word, or use a relative clause.

- *Although public transport is cheap, many people still prefer to drive.* → *Many people still prefer to drive, even though public transport is inexpensive.*
- *Students who work part-time often have less time to study.* → *Part-time jobs often reduce the time students can spend studying.*
- *Because the bridge was closed, traffic increased in the town centre.* → *The closure of the bridge led to heavier traffic in the town centre.*

The third example also uses Technique 2 (*was closed* → *closure*). Changing structure well depends on a good range of linking words, which you can build with our list of [linking words for IELTS Writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/).

### Technique 4: Switch between active and passive

- *Local councils should recycle more household waste.* → *More household waste should be recycled by local councils.*
- *Engineers have designed a quieter engine.* → *A quieter engine has been designed.*

The passive moves the important object to the front of the sentence and suits a more formal tone. Use it with care, though. An essay full of passives sounds heavy, and it can hide who is responsible, which matters when you are arguing that a particular group should act.

### Technique 5: Generalise or make specific

Replace a list with a general term, or expand a general term into specific examples.

- *trains, trams and buses* → *public transport*
- *smartphones, tablets and laptops* → *digital devices*
- *unhealthy habits* → *smoking, poor sleep and lack of exercise* (useful later in a body paragraph)

Generalising makes your introduction shorter and shows that you have understood the idea behind the details.

### Combining techniques

Strong paraphrases usually combine two techniques, occasionally three.

Original: *Many people think that the government should spend more money on parks and green spaces.*
Rewritten: *There is a widespread view that greater public investment in urban green areas is needed.*

Here *many people think* becomes *a widespread view* (synonym plus word form), *spend more money* becomes *greater public investment* (word form), and *parks and green spaces* becomes *urban green areas* (generalising).

### Paraphrasing a Task 1 introduction

In Academic Task 1, your first sentence restates the description of the visual. Some reliable swaps:

| In the question | Possible paraphrase |
|---|---|
| *The graph shows* | *The line graph illustrates / compares / gives information about* |
| *the number of* | *how many* / *the figures for* |
| *between 2005 and 2025* | *over a twenty-year period* / *from 2005 to 2025* |
| *in four countries* | *in four different nations* |
| *the percentage of households* | *the proportion of homes* |

Original (invented): *The chart shows the percentage of households that owned a car in four countries between 2005 and 2025.*
Rewritten: *The bar chart compares the proportion of homes with a car in four different nations over a twenty-year period.*

In General Training, Task 1 is a letter, so you do not restate the prompt in this way. Instead, cover each bullet point in your own words, as explained in our guide to [writing a formal email or letter](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/).

## Recognising paraphrase in Reading and Listening

When you read a question, predict how it might be expressed in the text. Ask yourself three things: what are the likely synonyms, what other word forms exist, and could the idea appear as a general term or a specific example?

For instance, if a question mentions *the cost of building the stadium*, the passage might say *construction expenses*, *what the project cost to complete*, or simply *the bill*. Your eyes should be searching for the idea, not the string of letters. Skimming and scanning still matter; the guide to [skimming and scanning in IELTS Reading](/en/blog/ielts-reading-skimming-and-scanning/) explains how to find the right paragraph fast, and paraphrase recognition helps you confirm you have found it.

## Paraphrasing mistakes that cost marks

Most paraphrasing errors are not grammar errors. They are meaning errors, and they are easy to miss because the sentence still looks impressive.

- **Shifting the meaning.** *Some people* → *most people* is a different claim. *Can reduce* → *will eliminate* is far stronger than the original. Keep quantities, certainty and time the same.
- **Choosing a synonym that does not fit.** *A big problem* → *a huge problem* is fine, but *an enormous problem* may exaggerate, and *a large problem* sounds unnatural. *A serious problem* is usually the safest choice.
- **Breaking a collocation.** Learners often reach for a formal verb and pair it with the wrong noun: *perform a decision* instead of *make a decision*. Our article on [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) lists natural pairs for common topics.
- **Translating from your first language.** If your language has one word that covers both *borrow* and *lend*, or both *make* and *do*, a "synonym" that feels right may be the wrong half of the pair. The list of [commonly confused words in English](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/) covers many of these.
- **Trying to change every word.** Technical or topic words such as *climate change*, *social media* or *university* often have no good alternative. Keeping them is correct.
- **Making it too long.** An introduction needs one clear restated sentence, not three.

A simple test: put the original and your version side by side, wait a minute, and ask whether a reader would draw exactly the same conclusion from both. If you hesitate, revise.

## Self-check: is the paraphrase accurate?

Each rewritten sentence below has one problem. Decide what it is and fix it.

1. Original: *Some teenagers spend too much time online.* Rewritten: *Most adolescents spend an excessive amount of time on the internet.*
2. Original: *Exercise can reduce stress.* Rewritten: *Physical activity eliminates stress.*
3. Original: *The number of tourists increased in 2024.* Rewritten: *There was a raise in tourist numbers in 2024.*
4. Original: *Governments should make decisions quickly in a crisis.* Rewritten: *Governments should commit decisions rapidly in an emergency.*
5. Original: *Many people use social media every day.* Rewritten: *Many people use online communal media on a daily basis.*
6. Original: *The table shows how much water three cities used in 2020.* Rewritten: *The table illustrates the water used by three urban areas in 2020, and it can be seen clearly that there are many different figures for each one.*

**Answers:**

1. *Some* has become *most*, which changes the claim. Use *Some adolescents spend an excessive amount of time on the internet.*
2. *Can reduce* has become *eliminates*, which is far too strong. Use *Physical activity can lower stress levels.*
3. *Raise* is mainly a verb (as a noun it means a pay increase in American English); the noun for numbers going up is *rise* or *increase*. Use *There was a rise in tourist numbers in 2024.*
4. *Commit decisions* is not a natural collocation. Use *Governments should make decisions rapidly in an emergency* or *act decisively in an emergency.*
5. *Social media* is a fixed term and should be kept. Use *Many people use social media on a daily basis.*
6. The rewrite is far too long and adds nothing. Use *The table compares water consumption in three cities in 2020.*

## Building a daily paraphrasing habit

1. **Rewrite one Task 2 prompt a day** in at least two different ways, then compare your versions with the introductions in our [band 8 Task 2 sample essays](/en/samples/) (an English-language page of annotated answers).
2. **Learn words in families and with synonyms.** An entry such as *significant (adj), significance (n), significantly (adv)*, plus *considerable* and *notable*, gives you ready material for Techniques 1 and 2. Our guide on [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/) explains how to record words this way.
3. **Mark paraphrase pairs after every Reading practice.** Once you have checked your answers, find the sentence in the passage that matches each question and note the pair of expressions. After a few weeks, you will have your own bank of exam-style synonyms.
4. **Practise talking around words.** Pick a random object and describe it for 20 seconds without naming it. This trains exactly what you need when a word disappears during the [Speaking Part 2 long turn](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-tips/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I keep some words from the question when I paraphrase?

Yes. Keeping core topic words such as *technology*, *environment* or *university* is normal and often necessary. What matters is that you change most of the surrounding wording and keep the meaning exactly the same.

### How many techniques should I use in one sentence?

Two is usually enough to make a sentence clearly different. Using four or five at once tends to produce long, awkward sentences and increases the risk of changing the meaning.

### Is it a good idea to use an online paraphrasing tool?

They can be interesting for comparison during practice, but they often choose synonyms that do not suit the context or break collocations. In the exam you have to do it yourself, so write your own version first and only then compare it with a tool.

### Is paraphrasing in Speaking different from Writing?

Yes. In Speaking you mainly paraphrase to avoid repeating the examiner's question word for word and to explain a word you cannot remember. Your language can be simpler and more conversational than in an essay, as long as the meaning stays clear.
