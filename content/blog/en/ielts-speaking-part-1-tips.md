*"Do you work or are you a student?"* It sounds so easy that many candidates never practise it. Then, in the exam room, they say *"I'm a student"* and stop. The examiner moves on, gets another four-word reply, and after a few minutes has heard almost nothing worth assessing.

**IELTS Speaking Part 1** is the warm-up: everyday questions about you, your studies or job, your habits and your likes. The content is never difficult, but this is where the examiner forms a first impression of your English. Knowing [how to think in English without translating](/en/blog/how-to-think-in-english-without-translating/) helps you respond immediately without long pauses. A strong answer here is not long or clever. It is *just enough*: a direct reply followed by a little extra. This guide shows you exactly how much "a little extra" means, gives you a three-step formula, original model answers on common topics, and the errors that learners from different language backgrounds tend to make.

## How IELTS Speaking Part 1 works

The full Speaking test takes 11–14 minutes and has three parts. Part 1 usually fills the first four to five minutes:

- The examiner introduces themselves, asks your name and checks your identity.
- You then answer questions on **two or three familiar topics**.
- Each topic normally has three or four short questions.

The first topic is almost always about you: work or study, your home town, or where you live now. Later topics can be almost anything ordinary: weather, music, gifts, mobile phones, walking, birthdays, public transport.

The examiner follows a script, so they will not chat back or react much to what you say. That is normal and has nothing to do with how well you are doing. The test is assessed on four criteria across the whole interview: fluency and coherence, lexical resource (your vocabulary), grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Part 1 has no separate score, but every sentence you say is evidence the examiner can use.

## The answer formula: answer, reason, detail

A good Part 1 answer is usually **two or three sentences**, roughly 15–25 seconds. A reliable way to get there:

1. **Answer** the question directly in the first few words.
2. **Add a reason** – why is that true for you?
3. **Add one detail or example** – when, where, with whom, or a recent instance.

An original example:

> **Q:** *Do you enjoy walking?*
>
> **Weak:** *Yes, I do.*
>
> **Better:** *Yes, I really do, mainly because it clears my head after sitting at a desk all day. Most evenings I walk along the river near my flat, and last Sunday I actually managed about fifteen kilometres with a friend.*

The better answer uses no rare words. What makes it work is the structure: a clear reply, a reason (*because it clears my head*), a habit (*most evenings*), a past event (*last Sunday*) and a natural phrase (*actually managed*). That small range of tenses and ideas is exactly what Part 1 rewards. If you want an idiom, one light expression is plenty; our guide to [idioms for IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/idioms-for-ielts-speaking/) shows how to place one without sounding rehearsed.

### Five ways to extend when you run out of ideas

| Technique | Original example |
|---|---|
| Then and now | *I couldn't stand coffee as a teenager, but these days I can't start work without it.* |
| An exception | *I normally cycle to college, unless there's ice on the roads.* |
| Someone else | *My sister is the complete opposite – she hasn't walked anywhere in years.* |
| The future | *I'm hoping to try a proper hiking trip next summer.* |
| A feeling | *It's one of the few times in the day when I don't look at my phone.* |

You do not need all five for every question. One is enough to stop an answer from sounding cut off. For an advanced look at how to connect these steps into a continuous flow, read our guide on [how to extend answers in IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/the-domino-effect-in-ielts-speaking/).

## Common topics and model answers

All the answers below are original and pitched at a natural, conversational level. Read them for structure, not for memorising.

### Work or study

> **Q:** *What do you do?*
>
> *I work as a junior accountant at a logistics company, so most of my day is spent checking invoices and spreadsheets. It's not the most exciting job in the world, but I like the fact that there's always a right answer.*

### Home town

> **Q:** *What is your home town like?*
>
> *It's a fairly small coastal town in the south, known mainly for fishing. It's lovely in summer, but it gets very quiet in winter, which is partly why I moved to the city for university.*

### Free time

> **Q:** *What do you usually do in the evenings?*
>
> *That depends on how tiring the day has been. If I'm exhausted, I'll just cook something simple and watch a series. If I've got some energy left, I go to a climbing gym near my office twice a week.*

### Technology

> **Q:** *Do you use your phone a lot?*
>
> *Far more than I'd like to admit. It's the first thing I check when I wake up, which is a habit I'm trying to break. Recently I've switched off most notifications, and it's made a noticeable difference to my concentration.*

### Weather

> **Q:** *What kind of weather do you prefer?*
>
> *I'd say crisp, sunny autumn days. Where I live, summer is unbearably humid, so when the temperature finally drops in October, I feel much more energetic.*

Notice the vocabulary: *fairly small*, *partly why*, *unbearably humid*, *a noticeable difference*, *a habit I'm trying to break*. These are everyday words used precisely, and that is what moves a lexical resource score, not long academic words. Natural word partnerships are covered in more depth in our guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/). For more topics and fuller model answers, browse the [IELTS Speaking question bank](/en/speaking/).

## Mistakes that hold Part 1 answers back

Some problems are universal; others depend on your first language. Check which ones sound like you.

### Problems almost everyone has

- **One-word answers.** *"Yes." "No." "Madrid."* The examiner has nothing to assess.
- **Answers that are far too long.** Speaking for a minute about whether you like rain eats into the time for other questions. Save extended turns for Part 2. Developed answers belong in Part 3; see our [IELTS Speaking Part 3 guide](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-tips/).
- **Memorised scripts.** A flat, recited tone and bookish language for a simple question are easy to spot, and examiners are trained to notice them.
- **Repeating the question.** *"Do I like walking? Yes, I like walking."* It wastes time and adds nothing. Just answer, or use a natural opener such as *"Yes, quite a lot."*
- **The same opener every time.** Starting every reply with *"Well, to be honest…"* turns a natural phrase into a verbal tic.

### Problems linked to your first language

- **Dropping final sounds.** Speakers of Vietnamese, Thai or Cantonese often lose word endings: *"I like walk"*, *"two year"*. To the examiner this sounds like a grammar mistake as well as a pronunciation one. For past tense verbs, [ed pronunciation rules](/en/blog/ed-pronunciation-rules/) show which ending to use.
- **Adding extra vowels.** Spanish and Portuguese speakers may say *"estudent"* or *"espeak"*; Japanese speakers sometimes add a vowel after consonants (*"worku"*). Practising consonant clusters slowly helps.
- **Present tense for duration.** German, French and Spanish speakers often say *"I live here since 2019"*. In English it is *"I've lived here since 2019"*; our [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) guide explains why.
- **Translated phrases.** *"I have 22 years"* (French, Spanish, Italian), *"make a party"* or *"open the light"* come straight from another language. The natural forms are *"I'm 22"*, *"have a party"* and *"turn on the light"*.
- **Missing past tenses.** Speakers of languages without verb endings for time, such as Mandarin or Indonesian, may say *"Yesterday I go to the gym"*. Listen back to recordings specifically for this.
- **Robotic rhythm.** Giving every word equal weight makes even correct English hard to follow. Read about [English word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) and [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/) to sound smoother.

## How to prepare without memorising
The aim is to make the *ideas* and *vocabulary* familiar while leaving the sentences free. Understand [why memorizing IELTS speaking sample answers](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/) destroys your fluency score, and apply this simple method for each common topic instead:

1. **Write three facts about yourself** for the topic: where, how often, why. Keep them true where possible – real details are easier to extend.
2. **Collect five or six useful phrases** that fit those facts, such as *a fairly long commute* or *I'm not much of a morning person*.
3. **Speak, do not write.** Answer three practice questions aloud, recording yourself each time, without a script.
4. **Listen and fix one thing.** Was any answer one sentence? Did you miss an *-s* or *-ed*? Repeat only the weakest answer.

Repeating a short model answer just after hearing it, known as [shadowing](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/), is a quick way to absorb natural rhythm and phrasing before you produce your own version.

## Quick self-check

Decide which answer is better and why.

1. **Q:** *Do you like reading?* (a) *"Do I like reading? Yes, I like reading very much."* (b) *"Yes, especially crime novels. I usually read for half an hour before bed because it helps me switch off."*
2. **Q:** *How long have you lived in your current home?* (a) *"I live here since three years."* (b) *"I've lived there for about three years now."*
3. **Q:** *Do you prefer mornings or evenings?* (a) A 70-second answer covering your whole daily routine. (b) *"Evenings, definitely. I'm not much of a morning person, and I find I concentrate better after dinner."*
4. **Q:** *Do you often buy gifts?* (a) *"No."* (b) *"Not very often, to be honest. I'd rather give people an experience, like a meal out, than another object."*
5. **Q:** *What did you do last weekend?* (a) *"Last weekend I visit my grandmother and we cook together."* (b) *"I visited my grandmother, and we cooked a huge lunch together."*

**Answers:**

1. (b) – it answers directly, then adds a habit and a reason; (a) only repeats the question.
2. (b) – duration up to now needs the present perfect with *for*; *since* goes with a starting point.
3. (b) – a direct answer plus one reason is the right length; (a) is closer to a Part 2 turn.
4. (b) – a short negative answer is fine as long as you extend it with a reason or preference.
5. (b) – a finished past event needs the past simple: *visited*, *cooked*.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should each IELTS Speaking Part 1 answer be?
Usually two or three sentences, about 15–25 seconds. That is enough to answer and extend a little without drifting into Part 2 length. If the examiner interrupts to move on, do not worry; they have a fixed number of questions to cover in a short time.

### Should I prepare answers for common topics?
Prepare ideas and vocabulary for the frequent topics, but do not memorise whole answers. Learned sentences tend to sound flat and may not match the exact wording of the question. Practise speaking about each topic several times until the ideas feel familiar, then let the sentences come naturally.

### Can I ask the examiner to repeat a question?
Yes. Asking politely, for example *"Sorry, could you repeat the question, please?"*, is perfectly acceptable. In Part 1 the examiner can repeat the question but will not usually explain what a word means. Asking once does not count against you.

### Does Part 1 affect my Speaking score much?
Your band is based on the whole test, not on each part separately. Still, Part 1 is the examiner's first impression and your chance to settle your nerves. Relaxed, well-developed short answers here make it easier to perform confidently in Parts 2 and 3.
