Almost every IELTS candidate meets a form, table or note to complete in the Listening test. In **IELTS Listening form completion**, you fill in missing details such as a name, a date, a phone number or an address while you listen to a short conversation or monologue. It looks easy, and in Section 1 it often is, but small mistakes with spelling, numbers and word limits cost marks. This guide shows you how to predict, listen, write and check, with the traps examiners and the recording use.

## What is IELTS listening form completion?

Form completion is a question type where you see a form, table, note or short summary with gaps. You listen to the audio and write the missing words and/or numbers. It appears most often in **Section 1**, an everyday conversation between two people (booking a room, joining a gym, asking about a course), but similar tasks can appear in other sections.

Important facts to check on the official IELTS website for your test format:

- The audio is played **once**.
- The instructions tell you the **word limit** (for example, *NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER*). Going over it makes the answer wrong.
- The questions follow the **order** of the recording.
- **Spelling counts.** A misspelled answer is marked wrong, so learn to write names and common words correctly.

For the whole test see our [IELTS listening tips](/en/blog/ielts-listening-tips/).

## Step 1: Prepare during the reading time

Before each section, you get time to read the questions. Use it well.

1. **Read the title and labels.** *Booking form, name, phone, date…* They tell you who is speaking and what kind of information comes.
2. **Predict the answer type.** Is the gap a name, a number, a date, a place, a price, a noun or an adjective?
3. **Check the word limit.**
4. **Notice the words around the gap.** *"Arrive on ____"* needs a date or day. *"____ per night"* needs a price.
5. **Underline key words.** Skip small words; mark what you need to hear.

## Step 2: Listen for answers and corrections

The recording often **corrects** information: *"Is that 14 Park Road?" — "No, 40."* The final version is your answer. Listen to the whole sentence before you write.

Common patterns:

- **Spelling:** *"Smith, S-M-I-T-H."* Write what is spelt out, not what you think the name is.
- **Numbers:** phone numbers are read in groups, and *"double five"* means 55. Zero can be *oh* or *zero*.
- **Dates:** *"the second of March"* or *"March the second"*; write what the form asks for (usually a number or a short form).
- **Time:** *"half past three"* means 3:30.
- **Addresses:** the street number, name and postcode may all be given quickly.
- **Distractors:** the speaker mentions two options and then chooses one.

Keep your eyes one question ahead. If you miss one answer, leave it and move on; do not lose the next.

## A worked example

Imagine a form for joining a language club. You see these labels: *Name: ____ / Phone: ____ / Preferred day: ____ / Monthly fee: ____*. Before the audio starts, you predict: a name (maybe spelt out), a number, a day of the week, and a price with a currency sign.

The conversation might go: the receptionist asks for the name, and the caller spells it letter by letter. Then comes the phone number, read in groups. Next, the caller says, "I could come on Thursdays… actually, Fridays are better for me." The answer is **Friday**, not Thursday. Last, the fee is mentioned twice: a full price, then a discount for students. The question asks for the monthly fee the caller will pay, so you must decide which one applies.

This is the usual rhythm of Section 1: a simple fact, a spelling or number check, a correction and a small distractor. If you predict the type of answer in advance, the sounds are much easier to catch, because your brain is already looking for them.

## Spelling, numbers and format

{{diagram: d2 | Small sound differences cause the most lost marks.}}

- **Spelling:** learn British and American variants (*colour/color*, *centre/center*); both are normally accepted if consistent, but check the official guidance. Practise common words: *accommodation, Wednesday, February, receipt, necessary.*
- **Word limit:** hyphenated words are usually counted as one word, but always follow your instruction line.
- **Numbers:** you can write numerals (*15*) or words (*fifteen*) as long as you follow the limit; numerals are quicker and safer.
- **Capital letters:** use them for names, places and days. Writing in capitals throughout is allowed and avoids confusion.
- **Plural forms:** *"two rooms"* needs the *s* if the answer is *rooms*.

For paper-based tests you have extra time to transfer answers; for the computer-delivered test you type them as you go. Check the official guidance for the format you will take.

## Common traps

| Trap | Example | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Teens vs tens | fifteen / fifty | Listen for the stress |
| Self-correction | "Tuesday, no, Wednesday" | Write the final version |
| Similar letters | B/P, M/N, G/J | Practise the alphabet aloud |
| Too many words | "in the library" instead of "library" | Count words |
| Wrong word class | writing a verb for a noun gap | Check the sentence meaning |
| Losing place | missing one answer, then another | Skip and jump to the next gap |
| Different spelling | *Cheryl* vs *Sheryl* | Write exactly what is spelt |

## How to practise

- **Dictation:** write short audio exactly as you hear it. This trains accuracy in names, numbers and spelling. See our [English dictation practice guide](/en/blog/english-dictation-practice-guide/).
- **Fast speech:** learn to catch details even when the speaker is quick; see [how to catch fast speech in IELTS Listening](/en/blog/how-to-catch-fast-speech-in-ielts-listening/) and [how to improve English listening](/en/blog/how-to-improve-english-listening/).
- **Phone numbers and addresses:** say them aloud, then listen and write them from audio.
- **Mock sections:** do a full Section 1 in timed conditions and note every mistake: spelling, number, word limit or missed answer.

## Self-check

1. You hear "double-one-seven". What do you write?
2. The word limit is TWO WORDS. Is *"the post office"* correct?
3. You hear "Johnson, spelt J-O-H-N-S-O-N." What do you write?
4. The speaker says "half past two". How do you write the time?
5. What should you do if you miss an answer?

### Answers

1. **117.**
2. **No.** It has three words. Write *post office*.
3. **Johnson.**
4. **2:30** or **2.30** (follow the form).
5. Leave it, move on, and keep your place in the recording.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is spelling really that important?

Yes. A wrongly spelled word is marked wrong, even when you heard the right word.

### Do I lose marks for going over the word limit?

Yes. Check the instruction line for every set of questions.

### Can I use American spelling?

Usually either variety is accepted, but be consistent. Check the official guidance for details.

### How can I get faster at writing while listening?

Write only the answer, not full sentences, and use short forms for repeated labels while you practise.
