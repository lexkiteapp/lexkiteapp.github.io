Most trouble in **English for video calls** is not grammar; it is the small practical moments: "Can you hear me?", "I think you are on mute", a frozen screen, and the awkward silence when two people start speaking at once. These moments have standard phrases, and once you know them you stop freezing and start sounding like a confident colleague.

This guide covers what to say when you open a call, check sound and camera, share your screen, interrupt politely, handle a bad connection, ask people to repeat, work across time zones, write in chat, and close with clear action items. For the content of meetings (opinions, agreeing and disagreeing), see [business English meeting phrases](/en/blog/business-english-meeting-phrases-guide/); for audio-only calls, see [business phone calls in English](/en/blog/business-phone-calls-in-english-guide/). Here we focus on what is different about video and remote work.

## English for video calls: opening the call, joining and checking

The first minute sets the tone. People often join a minute or two early, so a little small talk is normal; see our guide to [English small talk phrases at work](/en/blog/english-small-talk-phrases-workplace-guide/) for ideas.

- *Hi everyone, can you hear me okay?*
- *Can you see my screen? / Can you see me?* (If you are not sure, a quick check is polite, not weak.)
- *I'm just waiting for a few more people to join.*
- *Let's give it a minute for others to join.*
- *Shall we get started?* / *Let's make a start.* (UK) / *Let's get started.* (US)
- *Thanks for joining, everyone. We have about thirty minutes, so let's go through the agenda.*

If you are the host, say the plan out loud: *First we will review last week's actions, then talk about the launch, and finish with questions.* Hearing the plan helps people who join late or follow in a second language.

A short dialogue to read aloud:

> **Host:** Morning, Kenji. Can you hear me?
>
> **Kenji:** Yes, loud and clear. Can you see my camera? It's a bit dark here.
>
> **Host:** I can see you fine. Let's give it a minute for the others to join.
>
> **Kenji:** Sure. Sorry, I have a slow connection today, so I might turn off my video if it lags.
>
> **Host:** No problem at all. That's fine.

## Audio, camera and screen sharing

The most common call phrases belong to a small family. Learn them as chunks, not word by word.

{{diagram: d1 | Common video call words and phrases with a plain meaning and a ready sentence.}}

More phrases for tools:

- *Could you please turn your camera on? / I'll keep my camera off, because my connection is weak.*
- *I'm going to share my screen. Can you see the slides?*
- *Could you zoom in a bit? The text is small.* (Note: *zoom in* is the general verb, not only the app.)
- *Could you move to the next slide, please?*
- *I'll stop sharing now.*
- *I'll drop the link in the chat.* / *I'll put the link in the chat.*
- *Please raise your hand if you have a question.* (Many tools have a *hand* button.)
- *Let me put myself on mute while the dog settles down.* (Light humour is normal.)

A small cultural note: it is common in many workplaces for people to stay muted when they are not speaking, especially on large calls. In small calls, people may leave the microphone on. If you are unsure, follow the host.

## "You are on mute": polite ways to say it

This is the most famous remote work sentence. How you say it matters.

- Friendly: *I think you're on mute.* / *We can't hear you, Anna.* / *You might be on mute.*
- Gentle: *Sorry, we can't hear you. Could you check your microphone?*
- If it is you: *Sorry, I was on mute. Let me say that again.* / *Can you hear me now?*

You will often hear *I think you're on mute* rather than *You are on mute*, because the first is softer and it admits you may be wrong. This kind of softening is also used when you disagree; see the blunt-versus-polite section in our meeting guide.

## Interrupting politely and managing turns

Video makes interruptions harder: there is a small delay, no body language, and two people start talking at the same time. Cultures also differ; some people expect to wait for a pause, others jump in quickly. A few safe options:

| Situation | Blunt | Better |
|---|---|---|
| You want to speak | *Wait, I have something.* | *Sorry to jump in, may I add something?* |
| You need to correct a fact | *That is wrong.* | *Could I just check one thing? I thought the date was the 12th.* |
| Two people spoke at once | *No, me first.* | *Sorry, you go first. / After you.* |
| Someone speaks too long | *Please stop.* | *Sorry to interrupt. Could we hear from a few others as well?* |
| You were interrupted | *Let me finish.* | *Sorry, if I could just finish this point...* |

Other useful phrases: *Sorry, go ahead.* / *No, please, after you.* / *Can I come in here?* / *Could I add one more thing?* If you want to speak but do not want to cut in, type *+1* or *question* in the chat, or use the *raise hand* button, and let the host call on you.

If you dislike speaking up in groups, practise with a short roleplay. Our [client call roleplay](/app/#/roleplay/client-call) is a phone call with a new client, a safe place to practise answering aloud.

## When the connection fails

Bad internet is not your fault, and it is nothing to be embarrassed about. Name the problem, then offer a fix.

- *Sorry, you're breaking up.* / *You are cutting out.* / *The audio is a bit choppy.*
- *Your video is frozen on my side.* / *I think my connection has dropped.*
- *There's a bit of a delay, so let's try not to talk over each other.*
- *Let me turn my camera off to save bandwidth.*
- *I'm going to dial in by phone, so I'll be back in a minute.*
- *Sorry, I lost you for a moment. Could you repeat the last part?*
- *I'll rejoin the call.* / *I'll drop off and rejoin.*
- *If I disappear, I'll send my comments in the chat afterwards.*

British speakers often add *I'm afraid* (*I'm afraid you're breaking up*), and Americans may say *Looks like you've frozen*. Both are polite.

## Asking people to repeat or clarify

On video, you cannot read lips and sound is compressed, so asking again is normal, including for native speakers. Do not nod to hide that you did not understand.

- Sound problem: *Sorry, could you say that again? The audio cut out.*
- Speed: *Could you speak a little more slowly, please?*
- Meaning: *Just to check, do you mean the deadline is next Friday?*
- Spelling: *Could you spell that for me?* / *Could you type that in the chat?*
- Summarise to confirm: *So if I understand correctly, you would like the draft by Tuesday. Is that right?*

If sound problems keep happening, rephrase rather than say *What?*. *Sorry, I did not catch that* is a friendly standard; *Pardon?* is polite in British English; *Come again?* is casual.

## Working across time zones

Remote teams often span several countries, so scheduling language matters.

- *What time works for everyone? I'm in Hanoi, which is seven hours ahead of London in winter.* (Time differences change with daylight saving time, so state the city or use *check your local time*.)
- *Would 9:00 a.m. Pacific work for everyone?* (US: *Pacific / Eastern*; UK: *GMT* in winter and *BST* in summer; always include time zone in invites.)
- *Is that your morning or your evening?*
- *I'll be offline after 6, but I'll check messages first thing tomorrow.*
- *Let's find a slot that is not too late for you.*
- *I'll send the notes so people in other time zones can catch up.*
- *To avoid confusion, I'll write the time in UTC and your local time.*

Practical habits: write the date in words (*Tuesday 14 March*), because dates like 03/04 mean different things in the US and the UK; and say *a.m./p.m.* or use the 24-hour clock consistently. Be generous when you are the one with the late-night call, and rotate inconvenient times when you can.

## Chat and Slack: short and clear

Written chat has its own style. It is shorter than email and a little informal, but still professional.

- Be direct: *Quick question: do we have the final figures?* Start with the question.
- Use threads for replies so the main channel stays readable.
- Add context for a link: *Here's the draft for Friday's call: [link]. Comments by Thursday, please.*
- Acknowledge: *Got it, thanks.* / *On it.* / *Will do.* / *Noted.*
- Tag only the people you need: *@Sara, could you check this?*
- Avoid walls of text; one idea per message.
- Use reactions (a thumbs-up) for simple agreement, but write a message if you need a decision.
- Do not use all capital letters; they read as shouting.
- Tone: a short *Thanks!* and an emoji are often welcome, but check your workplace norm; some teams are formal. If a message might sound abrupt, add *please* or *when you have a moment*.

If a message needs a formal tone, use the structure in our [formal email guide](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/), and for chasing someone politely see [follow up emails in English](/en/blog/follow-up-email-in-english-examples-and-timing/).

## Closing the call: action items

The meeting is not finished until everyone knows who will do what. End with a short summary.

{{diagram: d2 | A clean ending: summarise, name owners, set dates and confirm.}}

> **Host:** Let me quickly recap. Sara will send the revised timeline by Thursday. Kenji will check the delivery dates with the supplier. I'll update the plan and share it on Friday. Does that sound right to everyone?
>
> **Sara:** Yes, that's right. Thursday works.
>
> **Kenji:** Yes, I'll get back to you on Friday morning. Could you send a summary in the chat?
>
> **Host:** Of course. Thanks, everyone, and thanks for staying on a bit longer. Have a good rest of the day.

Other closing phrases: *Any final questions before we wrap up?* / *I'll send the notes after the call.* / *Let's take the rest offline.* / *Let's pick this up next week.* / *Thanks for your time, everyone.* In the US you may hear *Talk soon* or *Have a good one*; in the UK, *Cheers, bye for now*; in Australia, *Thanks, everyone, cheers.* To turn the notes into a follow-up message, use the templates in our email guide above.

## Mistakes learners make on video calls

1. **Saying nothing when you did not understand.** Ask. Native speakers ask too, and everyone is relieved when somebody does.
2. **Staying silent for the whole call.** Prepare one comment or question and say it early. *I have a quick question about the timeline.*
3. **Speaking too fast when nervous.** Slow down and pause between ideas.
4. **Using "Can you repeat?" with a flat tone.** Add *please* and a reason: *Could you repeat that, please? The sound dropped.*
5. **Saying "do a call".** Use *have a call*, *join a call* or *get on a call*.
6. **Wrong prepositions.** Say *on a call* (not *in a call*), *on mute*, *in the chat*, *on the screen*. See our guide to [prepositions in, on and at](/en/blog/prepositions-in-on-at/).
7. **Reading the slides word for word.** Say the main point in your own words.

## Self-check: choose the best phrase

1. A colleague starts talking but nobody can hear. What do you say?
2. You lose the connection for a moment and come back. What is a natural thing to say?
3. Make this politer: *Stop talking, I have a point.*
4. You are in Tokyo and your colleague is in London. What should you write in the invite?
5. Which preposition: *I'm ____ a call right now, so I'll reply later.*
6. How do you check you understood a deadline?

**Answers**

1. *I think you're on mute.* or *Sorry, we can't hear you. Could you check your microphone?* Both are polite and clear.
2. *Sorry, I lost you for a moment. Could you repeat the last part?* It names the problem and asks for what you need.
3. *Sorry to interrupt, may I add something here?* Soften, apologise briefly and ask permission.
4. The city and time zone, ideally both local times (for example *9:00 a.m. London / 6:00 p.m. Tokyo*, remembering that daylight saving can change it) plus the date in words.
5. *on*: *I'm on a call right now.* We say *on a call*, *on mute* and *on the phone*, not *in a call*.
6. *Just to check, do you mean the deadline is next Friday?* Repeating the key detail confirms it politely.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I say when I cannot hear someone on a video call?

Say *Sorry, I can't hear you. I think you may be on mute*, or *Could you check your microphone?* If the audio is choppy, try *You're breaking up a little. Could you say that again?* Staying polite and specific helps the other person fix the problem quickly.

### How do I interrupt politely in an online meeting?

Use a softening phrase such as *Sorry to jump in, may I add something?* or *Could I just check one thing?* On a large call, you can use the raise-hand button or type *question* in the chat and wait for the host to call on you.

### Is it rude to turn my camera off?

It depends on the team. In many workplaces it is fine, especially with a poor connection, and you can say *I'll keep my camera off because of my connection*. If you are unsure, ask the host or follow what others do. For interviews and client calls, cameras are usually expected unless the other side says otherwise.

### How do I show time zones clearly in an invitation?

Write the day and date in words, the time with a.m. or p.m. (or the 24-hour clock), and the time zone or city, for example *Tuesday 14 March, 9:00 a.m. London time*. Ideally add the local time for each key participant. Daylight saving changes can shift the difference, so check your calendar.
