To describe an **IELTS line graph**, write four paragraphs: a one-sentence introduction that paraphrases the title, an overview that names the two or three biggest trends, and two body paragraphs that support those trends with selected figures. Do not explain causes or give opinions; your job is to report what the lines show, accurately and selectively. The rest of this guide walks through a three-minute reading routine, an original example graph with a full model answer, the trend language examiners expect and the mistakes that keep candidates below their target band.

## What Task 1 asks of you

In the Academic module, Task 1 asks for at least 150 words, and the suggested time is about 20 minutes of the 60-minute Writing test. It is assessed on four criteria: Task Achievement, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy.

For a line graph, Task Achievement depends on two things above all: a clear **overview** of the main trends, and accurate **data selection**. The public band descriptors make this explicit: at band 7 a report "presents a clear overview of main trends, differences or stages", while at band 5 there may be no clear overview and details may be mechanically recounted. Reports that list every number without a summary usually score lower than shorter reports that identify what matters.

Line graphs, like all charts, belong to Academic Task 1 only. General Training candidates write a letter instead.

## Read the line graph in three minutes

Before writing a word, study the chart with these questions:

1. **What is measured, and in what unit?** Thousands, millions, percentages, tonnes? Copying the unit wrongly is an expensive slip.
2. **What is the time span?** Past years mean past tenses. Future projections need forms such as *is expected to* or *is predicted to*.
3. **What is the general direction of each line?** Up, down, stable, or fluctuating.
4. **What stands out?** The highest and lowest lines, any crossing point, the steepest change, a peak or a dip.
5. **How can the lines be grouped?** Lines that behave similarly can share a body paragraph.

Those five answers give you almost the entire report.

## The four-paragraph IELTS line graph structure

| Paragraph | Content | Approx. length |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Paraphrase the title: what, where, when | 1 sentence |
| Overview | 2–3 main features, no specific numbers | 2 sentences |
| Body 1 | Details for the first group of lines, with figures | 3–4 sentences |
| Body 2 | Details for the second group, with comparisons | 3–4 sentences |

Some teachers put the overview at the end; both positions are acceptable. Placing it second makes sure it is never forgotten when time runs short.

### An example graph (invented for practice)

*The graph shows the average number of monthly visitors (in thousands) to three museums in one city – a science museum, an art gallery and a history museum – between 2010 and 2025.*

The imaginary data:

- **Science museum:** 20,000 in 2010, rising steadily to 45,000 in 2025.
- **Art gallery:** 35,000 in 2010, fluctuating between 30,000 and 38,000, ending at 32,000.
- **History museum:** 30,000 in 2010, falling to 12,000 in 2020, then recovering slightly to 15,000.
- The science museum overtook the art gallery in 2016.

### Model answer

**Introduction.** *The line graph compares the average monthly attendance at a science museum, an art gallery and a history museum in a particular city from 2010 to 2025.*

**Overview.** *Overall, the science museum saw a steady rise and became the most visited of the three, while attendance at the history museum declined considerably. The art gallery's visitor numbers remained relatively stable throughout the period.*

**Body 1.** *In 2010, the science museum attracted around 20,000 visitors a month, the lowest figure of the three. This number grew consistently, overtaking the art gallery in 2016, and by 2025 it had more than doubled to approximately 45,000.*

**Body 2.** *The art gallery began the period with about 35,000 monthly visitors and fluctuated between 30,000 and 38,000 before finishing at roughly 32,000. By contrast, the history museum experienced a sharp fall, from 30,000 in 2010 to a low of 12,000 in 2020, followed by a slight recovery to 15,000 in the final year.*

That is just over 150 words, which meets the minimum; in the exam, aim for roughly 160–190 so that a miscount never leaves you short. Notice what the overview does *not* contain: no figures, no years except implicitly, just the big picture.

### A two-sentence overview formula

If overviews are your weak point, use this pattern until it becomes automatic. Sentence one names the general direction of the lines: *Overall, X rose while Y fell.* Sentence two names the most striking comparison or change: *X ended the period as the highest, whereas Y remained the lowest throughout.* Begin with *Overall* or *In general*, keep numbers out, and check that someone who reads only these two sentences would understand the main story of the graph. If they would not, you have described details rather than trends.

## Trend vocabulary for line graphs

Variety matters for Lexical Resource, but accuracy matters more. A sharp change described as *slight* is a factual error, not a vocabulary choice.

### Verbs and nouns of change

| Direction | Verbs | Nouns |
|---|---|---|
| Up | rise, increase, grow, climb | a rise, an increase, growth |
| Down | fall, decrease, decline, drop | a fall, a decrease, a decline, a drop |
| No change | remain stable, level off, stay constant | a plateau |
| Up and down | fluctuate | a fluctuation |
| Highest/lowest point | peak at, hit a low of | a peak, a low |

*Rise* has no object (*visitor numbers rose*); *raise* needs one (*the museum raised its prices*). This pair is one of several covered in our list of [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

### Adverbs and adjectives of degree

- Large or fast: *sharply/sharp, dramatically/dramatic, significantly/significant*
- Moderate: *steadily/steady, gradually/gradual, considerably/considerable*
- Small: *slightly/slight, marginally/marginal*

### Two sentence patterns to alternate

- **Verb + adverb:** *The number of visitors rose sharply.*
- **There was + adjective + noun:** *There was a sharp rise in the number of visitors.*

Switching between these two patterns is the simplest way to show grammatical range without risky structures.

### Prepositions with figures

- **to** for the end point: *rose to 45,000*
- **by** for the size of the change: *fell by 18,000*
- **from … to** for both points: *from 30,000 to 12,000*
- **at** for a level: *peaked at 38,000; stood at 20,000*
- **of** after a noun: *a fall of 18,000; a low of 12,000*

### Comparing lines

*Higher than / lower than; twice as many as; by contrast; whereas; similarly; overtook; the gap between X and Y widened/narrowed.* For smooth connections between sentences, see [linking words for IELTS Writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/).

## Common mistakes in line graph reports

- **No overview.** This is the most damaging omission for Task Achievement.
- **Describing every year.** Choose the start, the end, peaks, lows and crossing points.
- **Explaining causes.** "Because people became more interested in technology…" is speculation. The graph does not say why.
- **Wrong tense.** Past years need past simple; *by 2025 it had more than doubled* uses the past perfect correctly for a change completed by a point. Our guide to the [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) explains why the present perfect does not fit finished years.
- **Prepositions.** *Increased with 10%* should be *increased by 10%*.
- **Repeating the title word for word.** Paraphrase: *shows* becomes *compares* or *illustrates*; *number of visitors* becomes *attendance*.
- **Missing articles.** *The number of visitors* (not *number of visitors*) and *a sharp rise* need their articles; the [a, an, the rules](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/) guide shows why.

## Quick self-check

Fill each gap with one word.

1. Sales rose ___ 200 units ___ 600 units.
2. The figure peaked ___ 80% in 2015.
3. There was a slight decline ___ the number of cars.
4. Attendance fell ___ a quarter over the decade.
5. Prices are predicted ___ rise after 2030.

**Answers:** 1. from, to – start and end points. 2. at – a level. 3. in – *a decline in* something. 4. by – the size of the change. 5. to – *be predicted to + verb* for future data.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should the overview go at the start or the end?
Either works. Many teachers recommend placing it right after the introduction so that it is never forgotten, but an overview at the end is equally acceptable as long as it clearly summarises the main trends.

### Do I need to mention every number on the graph?
No. Select the most significant data: starting and finishing points, peaks, lows and any point where lines cross. Mentioning everything makes the report long, repetitive and harder to follow.

### How do I describe a graph with future years?
Use prediction language: *is expected to rise*, *is projected to reach*, *will probably level off*. If the graph combines past and future, switch from past tenses to prediction forms at the point where actual data ends.

### How should I group lines when there are four or more?
Group lines that behave similarly, for example all rising lines in Body 1 and all falling or stable lines in Body 2. Grouping by trend is clearer than describing each line separately.
