Most airport conversations are short, scripted and polite. Learn a small set of **airport English phrases** for each stage, check-in, security, immigration, delays and lost bags, and you can handle almost every situation without panic. Keep your answers brief and true: officers and desk staff want clear facts, not long stories.

This guide follows your journey in order. For each stage you get the questions you will hear, short natural answers, and the mistakes learners make most often, including common ones for speakers of Vietnamese, German, Japanese and Korean. For general trip language beyond airports, see our guide to [travel English phrases for abroad trips](/en/blog/travel-english-phrases-for-abroad-trips/).

Two general tips before the details. First, speak slowly and finish your sentence; staff hear thousands of accents a day and are used to learners. Second, if you do not understand, say so directly: *Sorry, could you say that again, please?* or *Could you speak a little more slowly?* This is far better than guessing.

## Check-in and bag drop

At the desk or the self-service kiosk, you show your passport and booking, then deal with bags and seats. Typical questions and answers:

- **Agent:** *Where are you flying to today?* **You:** *To Toronto, with a connection in Frankfurt.*
- **Agent:** *Are you checking any bags?* **You:** *Yes, one bag, and I have one carry-on.*
- **Agent:** *Did you pack your bags yourself?* **You:** *Yes.* (Answer truthfully. Security questions are routine.)
- **Agent:** *Would you like a window or an aisle seat?* **You:** *An aisle seat, please, if possible.*

> **You:** Hi, I'd like to check in for the flight to Chicago.
>
> **Agent:** Of course. Passport, please. Are you checking any bags?
>
> **You:** Just this one. Is it okay for the weight? I'm not sure about the limit.
>
> **Agent:** Let me put it on the scale. That's fine. Here's your boarding pass. Boarding starts at gate 24 at ten past six.
>
> **You:** Thank you. Could you tell me whether my bag will go all the way to my final destination?

That last question matters on connecting flights. Allowances differ by airline and ticket, so check your booking rather than assuming.

**Mistake to avoid:** saying *I want a window seat* sounds abrupt in English. Use *I'd like…* or *Could I have…?* For the grammar behind these polite forms, see our guide to [modal verbs such as can, could and should](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/).

## Security: trays, liquids and laptops

Security is mostly instructions in the imperative. You will hear them fast, often repeated in a flat, bored voice. This is normal and not rude.

| What you hear | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| *Place your bag on the belt.* | Put your carry-on on the conveyor. |
| *Any liquids or laptops in your bag?* | Take them out and put them in a tray, if the airport requires it. |
| *Empty your pockets.* | Put keys, phone, coins in the tray. |
| *Step forward / Step to the side, please.* | Move where the officer points. |
| *Arms up, please.* | Raise your arms for the scanner. |
| *Is this your bag?* | Confirm ownership. |

Rules for liquids, electronics and shoes vary by airport and change over time. Look at the signs, and ask staff if unsure: *Do I need to take my laptop out?* or *Does this water bottle need to go in the tray?* Do not joke about weapons or bombs in an airport, in any language; it can be treated as a serious matter.

## Immigration and customs: typical questions and honest short answers

At passport control, an officer asks a handful of questions to understand why you are visiting and for how long. Answer the question you were asked, nothing more. If you are nervous, speaking slowly helps, and a short true answer is better than a long uncertain one.

Typical questions, with answers that sound natural:

1. **What is the purpose of your visit?** *Tourism.* / *I'm visiting family.* / *I'm here for a conference.* / *I'm studying at the University of Leeds.*
2. **How long are you staying?** *Ten days.* / *Until the fifteenth of March.* / *For the whole academic year.*
3. **Where are you staying?** *At the Riverside Hotel in the city centre.* / *With my brother. I have his address here.*
4. **Do you have a return ticket?** *Yes, I fly back on the twentieth.* (Show it if asked.)
5. **What do you do for a living?** *I'm a nurse.* / *I work in IT.* / *I'm a student.*
6. **Is this your first visit?** *Yes, it's my first time.* / *No, I came here two years ago for work.*
7. **Do you have anything to declare?** *No, nothing to declare.* / *Yes, I have some food items and gifts.* (Rules differ widely, so check the official customs page of your destination before you pack.)

> **Officer:** Good afternoon. Passport, please. What brings you to the UK?
>
> **You:** I'm visiting a friend and doing some sightseeing.
>
> **Officer:** How long will you be staying?
>
> **You:** Twelve days. I fly home on the twelfth.
>
> **Officer:** And where will you be staying?
>
> **You:** With my friend in Manchester. I have her address on my phone. Would you like to see it?
>
> **Officer:** That's fine. Enjoy your stay.

**Common learner mistakes at immigration:**

- Giving too much detail. *I'm going to see my girlfriend and also maybe I look for a job* creates unnecessary confusion. Say only what is true and relevant.
- Using *I come for tourism* instead of the natural *I'm here for tourism* or *Tourism.*
- Mixing tenses: *I stay ten days* should be *I'm staying ten days* or *I'll be staying ten days.*
- Using *business* when you mean *conference*; the officer may ask follow-up questions, so be accurate.
- For German speakers, translating *Ich bleibe zehn Tage* too literally. In English, *I'll be here for ten days* is more natural than *I stay since ten days*.
- For Japanese and Korean speakers, long indirect answers. Lead with the answer: *Ten days.* Then stop.

This article is language help only. It is not legal or immigration advice. Entry rules, visas and what you must declare depend on your nationality and destination, so check the official government page for your destination before you travel.

## Flight delayed, cancelled or a missed connection

This is where airport English matters most, because plans change and you need a clear solution quickly. The key skill is asking calmly and specifically. Staff can often help more when you are polite and concrete.

Vocabulary: *delayed* (later than scheduled), *cancelled*, *rebooked* (given another flight), *a connection* (the next flight), *to miss a connection*, *standby*, *a voucher* (for food or a hotel, if the airline offers one), *the departures board*.

What you may hear:

- *Your flight has been delayed by about two hours. We will update you at gate 12.*
- *We apologise for the inconvenience.*
- *Your flight has been cancelled. Please go to the customer service desk.*
- *We can rebook you on the next available flight.*

What you can say:

- *Excuse me, do you know why the flight is delayed?*
- *Do you know when we're likely to board?*
- *I have a connecting flight in Doha. Will I still make it?*
- *I've missed my connection. What are my options?*
- *Could you rebook me on the next flight to Melbourne?*
- *Is the airline able to provide a meal or accommodation?* (Policies vary by airline and country. Ask, and keep your documents.)
- *Could you put the new flight details in writing, please?*

> **You:** Excuse me, my flight to Vienna was cancelled and I've been told to come here.
>
> **Agent:** Yes, I'm sorry about that. Let me look at your booking. I can offer you a seat on the first flight tomorrow morning.
>
> **You:** Is there anything earlier this evening, even with a stopover?
>
> **Agent:** Let me check. There's a flight via Zurich at nine, but it's nearly full.
>
> **You:** I'd be happy to take it. Could you also confirm that my bag will be transferred?
>
> **Agent:** Yes, it will be checked through. Here is your new boarding pass.

**Tone tip:** being upset is understandable, but *This is unacceptable!* usually helps less than *I understand it's not your fault, but I need to be in Vienna tomorrow. What can we do?* A calm, specific request gets better help. Rights to compensation depend on the country and the reason for the delay, so look at your airline's website and the official page for the region you fly in. This article does not tell you what you are owed.

## Lost or damaged luggage: baggage claim

If your bag does not appear, do not leave the airport. Go to the *baggage service desk* or the *lost luggage* counter, usually near the carousel, before you leave the arrivals hall.

- *My suitcase didn't arrive. Could you help me find it?*
- *It's a medium black hard-shell case with a red ribbon on the handle.*
- *Here's my baggage tag and boarding pass.*
- *Could you give me a reference number, please?*
- *Could you send it to my hotel when it arrives? The address is…*
- *The handle is broken. The suitcase was fine when I checked it in.*
- *How will I be updated, by phone, text or email?*

> **You:** Hello, my bag didn't come out on the carousel, and everyone else has left.
>
> **Agent:** I'm sorry to hear that. Do you have your baggage tag?
>
> **You:** Yes, here. It's a navy suitcase, quite large, with a yellow strap.
>
> **Agent:** Thank you. I'll create a report. Where can we deliver it when we find it?
>
> **You:** To the Hotel Lindenhof, in the old town. Could I have a reference number to follow up?

Ask for the *report number* in writing. If you need essentials such as toiletries or clothes while you wait, ask what the airline can provide, since rules depend on the airline and country.

## Gate changes, boarding and announcements

Announcements are fast and distorted, so learn the patterns.

- *Flight BA 217 to Madrid is now boarding at gate 18.*
- *Gate change: your flight will now depart from gate 31.*
- *Final call for passenger Smith, travelling to Boston.*
- *Boarding will begin in ten minutes. Passengers with young children may board first.*
- *Group 3, you may now board.*

**Gate and boarding mistakes:** *Where is my gate?* is fine, but *Where is the gate of Lufthansa to Munich?* is awkward. Try *Excuse me, where's the gate for the Munich flight?* In everyday speech people say *boarding*, not *embarking*.

## Mistakes learners make at the airport

1. **Long answers to short questions.** Officers ask what they need to know. Answer in one short sentence.
2. **Translating word by word.** *I have a station in Frankfurt* is a common mix-up. Use *a connection* or *a layover* (US) / *a stopover*.
3. **Mixing up *take off* and *board*.** A plane *takes off*; you *board* it or *get on* it.
4. **Skipping politeness words.** *Please*, *could you…?* and *thank you* make a large difference to how you are treated. See our guide to [how to form questions in English](/en/blog/how-to-form-questions-in-english/).
5. **Not asking when you do not understand.** Say *Sorry, I didn't catch that.* Nobody minds.
6. **Forgetting prepositions.** *At* the gate, *on* the plane, *in* the queue, *to* the airport, *for* the flight. More in our [guide to in, on and at](/en/blog/prepositions-in-on-at/).

## Self-check: complete the sentence

1. At the check-in desk, which is more polite: *Give me an aisle seat* or *Could I have an aisle seat, please?*
2. The officer asks, *What is the purpose of your visit?* Which is better: *I come to see my sister* or *I'm visiting my sister*?
3. Your suitcase has not arrived. Where do you go first?
4. Complete: *My flight was cancelled, so could you ______ me on the next one?*
5. The word for the next flight on the same ticket is a ______.
6. In British English, a *line of people waiting* is called a ______.

**Answers**

1. *Could I have an aisle seat, please?* It is a polite question, not an order.
2. *I'm visiting my sister.* The present continuous works naturally for a current trip.
3. The baggage service desk or lost luggage counter, before you leave the arrivals hall.
4. **rebook.** *Rebook me* means to arrange another flight.
5. **connection.** (A *stopover* or *layover* describes the wait between flights.)
6. **queue.** In North America, people say *line*.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I say when an immigration officer asks why I am visiting?

Give a short, true answer: *Tourism*, *I'm visiting family*, *I'm here for a conference* or *I'm studying here*. Add how long you are staying if asked. Do not guess or exaggerate. Entry rules depend on your nationality and destination, so check the official government site for the country you are visiting.

### What should I say if my flight is cancelled?

Stay calm and be specific: *My flight was cancelled. Could you rebook me on the next available flight?* Then ask about meals, accommodation and baggage. What the airline provides depends on the airline, the route and local rules, so ask politely and keep written confirmation.

### What if I do not understand a question at the airport?

Say it directly: *Sorry, could you repeat that, please?* or *Could you say that more slowly?* Staff hear this all the time. If it is still unclear, ask them to point to the line or show it on screen. Guessing is worse than asking.

### What is the difference between a connection, a layover and a stopover?

A *connection* is the next flight you need to catch. *Layover* is common in US English for the wait between flights. *Stopover* is used in British English too and can mean a longer planned stay. Airlines sometimes use them a little differently, but the meaning is clear from context.
