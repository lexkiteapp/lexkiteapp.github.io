Opening an account is one of the first jobs after you arrive in a new country, and it is also one of the most nervous conversations to have in a second language. The good news: **English for opening a bank account** is a small, repeatable set of words and questions. Once you know the vocabulary, the documents banks usually ask about, and a few polite phrases, the appointment becomes routine.

This guide is about language only. Banks, fees, rules and required documents differ by country and even by branch, so nothing here is financial advice. Always **check with your bank** about its own terms.

## English for opening a bank account: the words you will hear

Start with the vocabulary, because the same ideas have different names in different countries.

{{diagram: d1 | The same banking ideas use different words in different countries. Learn the local word for your destination.}}

More notes the table cannot show:

- **Checking account, current account, chequing account.** All mean the everyday account that your pay goes into and your card spends from. Americans say *checking*, Canadians say *chequing*, and the British say *current account*; Australians often say *transaction account* or *everyday account*.
- **Savings account.** A separate account for money you plan to keep. Banks often describe how it earns *interest* (the money the bank pays you). Ask what the rules are for taking money out.
- **Statement.** A list of money in and out over a period. It may be on paper or in the app. A *bank statement* is also something a landlord may ask for later.
- **Direct debit and standing order.** A *direct debit* lets a company take a payment from your account, such as a phone bill, and the amount can change. A *standing order* is a fixed payment you set up to go out on a regular date. These two words are common in the UK and parts of Europe; other countries use *automatic payment* or *pre-authorized debit*.
- **Overdraft.** Spending more than you have. Some accounts allow it, often with a fee or interest. Ask: *Does this account have an overdraft, and what does it cost?*
- **Sort code and routing number.** These are the bank-branch identifiers used when money is sent. The UK uses a *sort code* together with an *account number*; the US uses a *routing number*; Australia uses a *BSB*. For international transfers people also mention an *IBAN* and a *SWIFT* or *BIC* code. You do not need to memorise any of this: the bank will tell you which details to give people.
- **PIN, contactless, chip.** Your *PIN* is the secret number for the card. *Contactless* means you tap the card. The *chip* is the small gold square you insert.
- **Balance.** The amount in the account. *Available balance* may be less than *current balance* if some payments are still *pending*.

## What banks often ask for (in general terms)

Every bank has its own rules, so treat this as a map, not a checklist. Many banks ask to see some mix of:

- **Proof of identity**, usually a passport or another official photo ID.
- **Proof of address**, such as a rental agreement or a recent letter or bill with your name and address. Newcomers often find this hard because the first account needs an address and the address needs paperwork, so say honestly: *I've just arrived, so I don't have a utility bill yet. What else can I show you?*
- **Immigration or residence status**, such as a visa or permit, and sometimes a letter from an employer or school.
- **Tax-related information.** Some countries ask for a tax number.

Useful questions at the start:

- *What do I need to bring to open an account?*
- *Can I open an account before I have a permanent address?*
- *Is there a monthly fee, and can it be avoided?*
- *Do I need to make a minimum deposit?*

Whatever the answer, you can always ask: *Could you write that down for me?*

## A dialogue at the bank

Here is a typical conversation at a branch. Read it aloud, then cover the right-hand lines and answer from memory.

> **Clerk:** Good morning. How can I help you today?
>
> **You:** Hi, I'd like to open a current account, please. I've recently moved here for work.
>
> **Clerk:** Of course. Do you have some ID with you?
>
> **You:** Yes, I have my passport and my residence permit. I also have a letter from my employer. Is that enough, or do you need proof of address?
>
> **Clerk:** The letter helps. We'll also need an address. Where are you staying at the moment?
>
> **You:** I'm in temporary accommodation. I can give you that address, and I'll update it when I move. Is that all right?
>
> **Clerk:** That's fine. Would you like a debit card with the account?
>
> **You:** Yes, please. Does the account have any monthly fees? And can I use the mobile app?
>
> **Clerk:** There's no monthly fee if your salary is paid in. You'll get an email to set up the app.
>
> **You:** Great. Could you tell me how long the card will take to arrive?

Notice the pattern: you state what you want (*I'd like to open…*), give the facts, then ask about fees and next steps. Staff expect questions, so asking them is not rude.

## Polite and clear: what to say and what to avoid

Banking staff hear the same worries all day. These small changes make you sound natural.

| Too blunt | More natural |
|---|---|
| *I want open account.* | *I'd like to open an account, please.* |
| *Give me my money.* | *Could I withdraw some cash, please?* |
| *Your fee is too much.* | *Could you explain this fee? I wasn't expecting it.* |
| *My card does not work.* | *My card was declined. Could you check why?* |
| *I don't understand.* | *Sorry, could you say that more slowly?* |

Also, *to deposit* means to put money in, *to withdraw* means to take it out. Learners often mix these up.

## When your card is declined

A declined card in a shop is embarrassing, but the language is simple and the reasons are usually ordinary. A *declined* payment can happen because the card is new and not activated, the balance is too low, the card is blocked for security, or the shop's machine has a problem. Do not guess in front of the cashier; try another payment method and sort it out afterwards.

At the till:

> **Cashier:** I'm sorry, the card's been declined.
>
> **You:** Oh, could you try it once more? ... Still no? Let me use another card. I'll sort it out with my bank.

By phone or at the branch:

- *My card was declined at a shop this morning. Could you tell me why?*
- *I think I need to activate my new card. How do I do that?*
- *Has my card been blocked? I haven't received any message.*
- *Could you unblock it, or send me a new one? How long would that take?*

Banks may ask you to answer a few *security questions* or confirm your identity before they discuss your account. That is a good thing: *I understand. Thank you for checking.* Never share your full PIN or a one-time code with anyone who contacts you; a real bank will not ask for it. If a call or message worries you, hang up and phone the number on the back of your own card.

## A charge you do not recognise

Seeing a payment you cannot explain is stressful. Stay factual. The structure below works for a call, a chat message or a visit.

{{diagram: d2 | Four parts of a clear message to your bank: who you are, the item, what is wrong, and a request for next steps.}}

On the phone:

> **You:** Hello, I'd like to ask about a payment on my account. There's a charge of this amount on the 12th that I don't recognise.
>
> **Agent:** Let me look at it. Can you read me the name that shows on your statement?
>
> **You:** It says the name of a subscription service. I don't think I signed up for it.
>
> **Agent:** It may be a recurring payment. Would you like me to look up when it started?
>
> **You:** Yes, please. If it's not mine, what are the next steps? Can the payment be disputed?

Handy vocabulary: *a charge*, *a transaction*, *a recurring payment*, *a subscription*, *to dispute a payment*, *to block a card*, *a refund*, *a pending payment* (not yet final). A phrase like *I don't recognise this transaction* is the safest opener. Whether you get money back depends on the bank and the situation, so ask what the process is.

## Sending money and understanding transfers

Learners often meet these words when they send rent, pay a deposit or send money home.

- **To transfer money / make a transfer.** *I'd like to send money to an account in another country. What details do you need from me?*
- **Recipient or beneficiary.** The person receiving the money. You usually give their name, account number and the bank's identifier, plus an *IBAN* and *SWIFT/BIC code* for many international transfers.
- **Exchange rate.** The rate at which one currency is changed into another. *Is that the rate you apply, or is there a margin on top?*
- **Transfer fee.** *Is there a fee for sending money abroad, and does the recipient's bank charge one too?*
- **Reference.** The short note on the payment. *What should I put as the reference?*
- **How long it takes.** *When should the money arrive?* Banks often describe this in *working days* or *business days*, which means weekdays that are not public holidays.

Money transfers are a place where mistakes are costly. Before you confirm, check the name, numbers and amount twice, and ask the bank what happens if you enter a detail wrongly: *If I make a mistake, can the payment be recalled?*

## Fees: questions worth asking

You do not need to be an expert to ask good questions. Banks may charge for things such as running the account, using a cash machine abroad, converting currency, or sending an international payment. Every bank differs, and it is reasonable to ask for everything in writing.

- *Is there a monthly account fee, and is it ever waived?*
- *What does it cost to use a cash machine abroad?*
- *Is there a fee for foreign-currency purchases with my card?*
- *Are there any charges if my balance goes below zero?*
- *Where can I see the full list of fees?* (Often called the *fee schedule* or *tariff*.)

Then say: *Thank you. I'll read this and decide. Could I take it home?* There is no need to decide on the spot.

For general advice on a particular product, such as which account suits you, speak to your bank or a qualified adviser; this article only helps you ask clearly.

## Mistakes learners make at the bank

1. **Mixing up deposit and withdraw.** You *deposit* money into the account and *withdraw* it out.
2. **Saying "I want" all the time.** It is understood but abrupt. Use *I'd like…* or *Could I…?*
3. **Missing articles.** *I'd like to open **an** account* and *I have **a** passport*, not *I'd like to open account*. See our guide to [a, an and the](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/).
4. **Guessing a word instead of asking.** If you do not know a term, ask: *What does "overdraft" mean?* Staff are used to it.
5. **Agreeing to everything.** *Could you send me that in writing?* is a normal request.

## Self-check: choose the best word or phrase

1. In the UK, the everyday account for your pay is usually called a ____ account.
2. You put money into an account. Do you *deposit* it or *withdraw* it?
3. A company takes a variable payment from your account every month, with your permission. What is it called?
4. You see a payment you do not recognise. Choose the better opening: *This is wrong!* or *I don't recognise this transaction.*
5. Fix the sentence: *I want open a account.*
6. What does *pending* mean next to a payment?

**Answers**

1. **current.** Americans say *checking* and Canadians say *chequing*.
2. **Deposit.** *Withdraw* means to take money out.
3. A **direct debit** (in some places also called a pre-authorized debit or automatic payment).
4. *I don't recognise this transaction.* It is calm and factual, so the agent can help.
5. *I'd like to open an account.* Use *I'd like to* plus the base verb, and *an* before a vowel sound.
6. It has not been completed yet. The money may still be reserved but the payment is not final.

## Frequently asked questions

### What English do I need to open a bank account?

You need a small set of words (account, statement, deposit, withdraw, fee, balance, transfer), a polite request such as *I'd like to open an account, please*, and a few questions about fees and documents. Practise them aloud before you go.

### What is the difference between a checking account and a current account?

They are the same kind of everyday account. Americans say *checking account*, Canadians say *chequing account*, and the UK and many other countries say *current account*. Features differ by bank, so check with your bank.

### What should I say if my card is declined?

Stay calm and say *Could you try it again?* or *I'll use another card.* Afterwards contact your bank: *My card was declined this morning. Could you tell me why?* Never give your PIN or one-time codes to anyone who contacts you.

### Is this advice about which account to choose?

No. This guide only helps with the language. Fees, rules and documents vary by bank and country, so always check with your bank or a qualified adviser.
