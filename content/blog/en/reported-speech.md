**Reported speech** (also called indirect speech) is how we tell someone what another person said without quoting their exact words. *"I'm running late,"* becomes *She said she was running late.* Three things usually change: the **pronouns**, the **tense**, and words for **time and place**. Questions and commands follow their own patterns, and the reporting verb you choose (*say, tell, ask, advise, promise*) affects the grammar after it.

This guide explains each rule, shows when you do **not** need to change the tense, and ends with a self-check so you can test yourself.

## Direct vs reported speech

Direct speech repeats the exact words inside quotation marks:

- *Ravi said, "I have booked the tickets."*

Reported speech gives the meaning in your own sentence, with no quotation marks:

- *Ravi said (that) he **had booked** the tickets.*

The word *that* is optional after *say* and *tell*. Leaving it out is more common in conversation; keeping it can make long written sentences clearer.

## Tense changes: the backshift rule

When the reporting verb is in the past (*said, told, asked*), the verb in the reported clause usually moves one step back in time. Grammar books call this **backshift**.

{{diagram: d1 | Each tense moves one step into the past when the reporting verb is past}}

A few examples:

- *"I **live** in Chennai."* → *He said he **lived** in Chennai.*
- *"We **are moving** house."* → *They said they **were moving** house.*
- *"I **have lost** my keys."* → *She said she **had lost** her keys.*
- *"I **will send** it tomorrow."* → *He said he **would send** it the next day.*

Two notes. First, the past simple often stays the same in speech: *"I saw him"* → *She said she saw him* is normal and acceptable; *had seen* is more formal and makes the sequence clearer. Second, *could, would, should, might* and *ought to* do not change: *"You should rest"* → *He said I should rest.* The modal **must** can stay as *must* or become *had to* when it describes an obligation.

## When you don't need to backshift

Backshift is the default, not an iron law. You can keep the original tense when:

1. **The reporting verb is in the present.** *She **says** she **is** busy.* This is common when you pass on a message straight away: *Mum says dinner is ready.*
2. **The statement is still true.** *"The Earth goes round the Sun"* → *Our teacher said the Earth **goes** round the Sun.* Both *goes* and *went* are grammatical; *goes* stresses that it is a fact.
3. **The situation hasn't changed.** If your friend told you this morning *"I'm vegetarian,"* you can report *She said she's vegetarian* because it is still true now.

Choosing the original tense signals *this is still true*; backshifting signals *this is what was said then*. Both are correct; the meaning is slightly different.

## Changing pronouns, time and place words

Reported speech shifts the point of view, so words that depend on *who* and *when* change with it.

| Direct | Reported (later, elsewhere) |
|---|---|
| I / me / my | he, she / him, her / his, her |
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the day before / the previous day |
| next week | the following week |
| ago | before / earlier |
| here | there |
| this / these | that / those |

Do not change these mechanically. If you report something on the same day and in the same place, *tomorrow* and *here* can stay: *(at 10 a.m.) Priya said she'll call **tomorrow**.* Use your head: ask what the words mean from **your** point of view now.

## Reporting questions

{{diagram: d2 | A reported question is no longer a question: statement order, no do/does/did, no question mark}}

**Yes/no questions** use *if* or *whether*:

- *"Are you coming?"* → *She asked **if I was** coming.*
- *"Did you finish the report?"* → *My manager asked **whether I had finished** the report.*

**Wh- questions** keep the question word:

- *"Where **do you work**?"* → *He asked where **I worked**.*
- *"Why **is she** upset?"* → *They wanted to know why **she was** upset.*

The most common mistake is keeping question word order: ✗ *He asked where did I work.* Once the question is reported, it behaves like a statement: subject first, then verb, with no auxiliary *do/does/did* and no question mark. The same order is used in polite indirect questions (*Could you tell me where the station is?*), so practising one helps the other.

## Reporting commands, requests and advice

For orders and requests, use a reporting verb + object + **to-infinitive**:

- *"Close the door."* → *She **told me to close** the door.*
- *"Please don't park here."* → *The guard **asked us not to park** there.*

Note the position of *not*: before *to*. ✗ *asked us to not park* is heard in speech but is better avoided in exams.

Many other reporting verbs carry the speaker's intention, and each has its own pattern:

| Pattern | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| verb + to-infinitive | agree, offer, promise, refuse, threaten | *He **promised to call** me.* |
| verb + person + to-infinitive | advise, ask, encourage, invite, remind, warn | *She **reminded me to bring** my ID.* |
| verb + -ing | admit, deny, recommend, suggest | *He **denied taking** the money.* |
| verb + that-clause | explain, claim, insist, suggest | *She **suggested that we leave** early.* |
| verb + preposition + -ing | apologise for, insist on, accuse sb of | *They **accused him of lying**.* |

*Suggest* is the trap: you can say *suggested going* or *suggested that we go*, but never ✗ *suggested me to go*. The [gerunds and infinitives guide](/en/blog/gerunds-and-infinitives/) explains why some verbs take *-ing* and others *to*.

## Say vs tell (and other common mistakes)

*Say* and *tell* mean almost the same, but the grammar differs:

- *say* + (that) clause: *She **said** (that) she was busy.* With a person, use *to*: *She said **to me**…* (less common).
- *tell* + person + (that) clause: *She **told me** (that) she was busy.*

So ✗ *She said me she was busy* and ✗ *She told that she was busy* are both wrong. *Tell* also appears in fixed phrases without a listener: *tell the truth, tell a lie, tell a story*.

Other frequent errors:

- **Forgetting the pronoun change:** ✗ *He said I am hungry* (when he meant himself) → ✓ *He said he was hungry.*
- **Keeping quotation punctuation:** reported speech has no question marks or quotation marks.
- **Double past markers:** ✗ *She asked did I went* → ✓ *She asked if I had gone* / *if I went.*
- **Overusing *said*:** in writing, *explained*, *admitted*, *warned* and *pointed out* show *how* something was said and make your text more precise.

Reported speech also connects with other grammar. *"If I had time, I would help"* stays almost unchanged when reported (*He said that if he had time, he would help*), which our guide to [conditional sentences](/en/blog/conditional-sentences-in-english/) explains, and the impersonal passive (*It is said that…*, *He is reported to be…*) is covered in [passive voice](/en/blog/passive-voice-in-english/).

## Reported speech in IELTS and everyday English

In **IELTS Speaking**, reported speech appears naturally when you tell a story in Part 2: *My friend told me the restaurant had just opened, so we decided to try it.* Using it accurately (with the tense shift) shows grammatical range without sounding memorised. See our [Speaking Part 2 tips](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-tips/) for story structures.

In **IELTS Writing Task 2**, reporting verbs help you present other people's views: *Some argue that…*, *Critics claim that…*, *Supporters point out that…*. Combine them with cautious language from [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/). At work, the same verbs help you summarise a meeting by email: *The client confirmed that the deadline was Friday and asked us to send a draft first.*

## Self-check: report these sentences

Report each sentence, starting with the words given. Assume you are reporting it a week later.

1. "I'm working from home today," Anil said. → Anil said…
2. "Have you seen my charger?" my sister asked me. → My sister asked me…
3. "Don't touch the screen," the technician told us. → The technician told us…
4. "I'll finish the slides tomorrow," Meera said. → Meera said…
5. "Why did you leave early?" the coach asked him. → The coach asked him…
6. "You should apply for the job," my friend said to me. → My friend advised me…
7. "I didn't break the window," the boy said. → The boy denied…
8. "Water boils at 100 °C at sea level," the teacher said. → The teacher said…

### Answers

1. *Anil said (that) he **was working** from home **that day**.*
2. *My sister asked me **if/whether I had seen her** charger.*
3. *The technician told us **not to touch** the screen.*
4. *Meera said she **would finish** the slides **the next day**.*
5. *The coach asked him **why he had left** early.* (*why he left* is also accepted in speech.)
6. *My friend advised me **to apply** for the job.*
7. *The boy denied **breaking** the window.* (or *denied that he had broken*)
8. *The teacher said water **boils** at 100 °C at sea level.* (A general fact, so no backshift is needed; *boiled* is also correct.)

Score 7–8: you are ready to use reporting verbs in writing. Score below 5: go back to the backshift table and the question section, then try again tomorrow.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I always have to change the tense in reported speech?

No. Backshift is normal when the reporting verb is past, but you can keep the original tense if the information is still true or if the reporting verb is present (*She says she is busy*). Both versions are grammatical; backshift simply places the statement in the past.

### What is the difference between said and told?

*Tell* needs a listener directly after it (*told me, told the class*), while *say* does not (*said that…*, or *said to me*). Use *tell* for instructions too: *She told me to wait.*

### How do I report a question without "do" or "did"?

Use *if/whether* or the question word, then normal statement order: *He asked where I lived*, not *where did I live*. The auxiliary *do/does/did* disappears and the main verb carries the tense.

### Is reported speech the same as indirect speech?

Yes. *Reported speech* and *indirect speech* are two names for the same structure. Some textbooks also use *indirect speech* for polite indirect questions such as *Do you know where she is?*, which use the same word order.
