**Modal verbs** are small words with a big job: they show *how* the speaker sees an action, whether it is possible, allowed, necessary, advisable or almost certainly true. *You go* is a fact; *you should go*, *you must go* and *you might go* are three different attitudes towards the same action. Master the meanings below and you will choose the right modal by logic rather than by guessing.

## What modal verbs are and the grammar they share

The core modal verbs are **can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will** and **would**. Several "semi-modals" behave similarly in meaning: **have to, ought to, need to** and **be able to**.

All core modals follow four rules that never change:

1. **No -s for he/she/it.** *She can swim*, never *she cans swim*.
2. **Base form after the modal, without *to*.** *You should rest*, never *you should to rest*.
3. **No *do* in questions and negatives.** *Can you help?* and *I can't come*, never *Do you can help?*
4. **No infinitive or -ing form.** There is no *to can* or *canning*; use *be able to* instead: *I'd like to be able to drive.*

Semi-modals are different: *have to* and *need to* behave like ordinary verbs (*She has to leave. Does she have to leave?*).

## Ability: can, could, be able to

**Can** describes present ability: *Ana can read music.* **Could** describes general ability in the past: *When I was five, I could swim.*

There is one trap. For **a single achievement on one occasion in the past**, use *was able to* or *managed to*, not *could*:

- ✗ *The fire spread fast, but everyone could escape.*
- ✓ *The fire spread fast, but everyone **was able to** escape.*
- ✓ *The fire spread fast, but everyone **managed to** escape.*

In the negative, *couldn't* is fine for both: *I couldn't open the file yesterday.*

For future ability or other tenses, use *be able to*: *After this course you **will be able to** give a short presentation. I **have been able to** relax more since I moved.*

## Permission and requests: can, could, may

To **ask for permission**, *can* is everyday, *could* is more polite, and *may* is formal:

- *Can I sit here?* (friendly)
- *Could I ask you a question?* (polite)
- *May I leave early today?* (formal or very polite)

For **requests**, *could you* is softer than *can you*: *Could you send me the file?* *Would you mind* + -ing is softer still: *Would you mind closing the window?*

To **give or refuse permission**, use *can* / *can't* or, in formal notices, *may* / *may not*: *Visitors may not take photographs.*

## Advice: should and ought to

**Should** gives advice or says what is right: *You should back up your files. People shouldn't drive when they are tired.* **Ought to** has the same meaning but is less common in everyday speech and rare in questions.

For stronger, more urgent advice, *had better* (often *'d better*) adds a warning of consequences: *You'd better leave now, or you'll miss the train.* Note that *had better* refers to the present or future, not the past, and is followed by the base form.

**Should have + past participle** looks back with regret or criticism: *I should have saved my work. You shouldn't have told him.* The action did (or did not) happen, and it was a mistake.

## Obligation and necessity: must vs have to

Both express necessity, but the source is different:

- **Must** often shows the speaker's own opinion or a rule the speaker supports: *I must call my grandmother this weekend.* It is also common in written rules: *Passengers must wear seat belts.*
- **Have to** often presents an external obligation, a rule or circumstance: *I have to wear a uniform at work. We have to leave at six because the road closes.*

In many everyday sentences either is acceptable, so do not worry about tiny differences. Two points, however, are not optional.

**1. Past and future use *have to*.** *Must* has no past form. The past of both is *had to*: *Yesterday I **had to** work late.* For the future, use *will have to*: *You'll have to renew your passport.*

**2. The negatives mean completely different things.**

{{diagram: d2 | Mustn't removes the choice; don't have to leaves the choice with you}}

- *You **mustn't** use your phone during the exam.* (It is forbidden.)
- *You **don't have to** arrive early; doors open at nine.* (It is not necessary, but you may.)

This is one of the most common modal errors at every level, including advanced learners who otherwise write accurately. *Needn't* and *don't need to* share the "not necessary" meaning of *don't have to*.

## Possibility and deduction: may, might, could, must, can't

Modals also express **how sure we are** that something is true.

{{diagram: d1 | From near-certain yes to near-certain no: choose the modal that matches your evidence}}

- **Must be** = I am almost sure it is true (based on evidence): *You've been travelling all day. You must be exhausted.*
- **May / might / could be** = it is possible: *The meeting might be cancelled. Take an umbrella; it could rain.* (*Might* sounds slightly less certain than *may* to many speakers, but the difference is small.)
- **Can't be** = I am almost sure it is false: *That can't be Tom; he's in Canada this week.*

Two notes matter. First, the opposite of *must be* in deduction is **can't be**, not *mustn't be*. Second, *can* is used for general possibility (*Winters here can be very cold*) but not for a deduction about one specific situation: say *She may be at home*, not *She can be at home*.

### Deduction about the past

Add **have + past participle**:

- *The road is wet. It **must have rained** overnight.*
- *She **might have missed** the bus.*
- *He **can't have seen** the email; he would have replied.*

## Modal verbs in writing and exams

In **IELTS Writing Task 2** and academic writing, modals of possibility are a key tool for measured claims: *This policy **could** reduce traffic in city centres* sounds more academic than *This policy will reduce traffic*. Our guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) shows how to use *may*, *might* and *could* without weakening every sentence. In the **TOEFL Academic Discussion** task, a clear position plus one carefully hedged claim works well; see our [TOEFL Academic Discussion guide](/en/blog/toefl-academic-discussion-writing/).

In **conditionals**, *would*, *could* and *might* carry the imagined result: *If I had more time, I **would** learn Japanese.* That structure gets its own article on [conditional sentences](/en/blog/conditional-sentences-in-english/). ## Common mistakes with modal verbs

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *He can drives.* | *He can drive.* | base form after modals |
| *You must to finish.* | *You must finish.* | no *to* after core modals |
| *Do you can come?* | *Can you come?* | no *do* with modals |
| *I must work yesterday.* | *I had to work yesterday.* | *must* has no past form |
| *You mustn't pay; it's free.* | *You don't have to pay; it's free.* | not necessary ≠ forbidden |
| *He mustn't be home; the car is gone.* | *He can't be home; the car is gone.* | negative deduction = *can't* |
| *I will can help.* | *I will be able to help.* | two modals cannot combine |

## Self-check

Choose the best option.

1. *Last year I ____ run 10 km without stopping.* (could / can)
2. *The concert was sold out, but we ____ get two tickets.* (could / managed to)
3. *You ____ smoke here; it's a hospital.* (mustn't / don't have to)
4. *She's been studying all night. She ____ be tired.* (must / can)
5. *I ____ have checked the address. Now we're lost.* (should / must)
6. *He ____ have stolen the money; he was with me all evening.* (can't / mustn't)
7. *Tomorrow we ____ get up early for the flight.* (will have to / will must)
8. *____ I borrow your pen?* (Could / Should)

### Answers

1. **could** – general past ability over a period.
2. **managed to** – a single achievement on one occasion.
3. **mustn't** – smoking is forbidden.
4. **must** – confident deduction from evidence.
5. **should** – regret about a past mistake: *should have checked*.
6. **can't** – negative deduction about the past.
7. **will have to** – *must* has no future form.
8. **Could** – a polite request for permission.

Seven or eight correct means modals are under control; review whichever section caused a miss. Revisit the set in two or three days, because spaced retesting, as explained in [spaced repetition for language learning](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/), is what moves a rule into automatic use.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the nine core modal verbs in English?

They are can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would. Have to, ought to, need to and be able to are often called semi-modals because they express similar meanings but follow different grammar.

### Is "shall" still used?

Yes, mostly in offers and suggestions with I and we: *Shall I open the window? Shall we start?* It also appears in legal and formal documents. In other uses, modern English normally prefers *will*.

### What is the difference between "could" and "was able to"?

For general ability in the past, both work: *I could / was able to swim as a child.* For one specific achievement, use *was able to* or *managed to*: *I was able to finish the report on time.* In the negative, *couldn't* is fine in both cases.

### Can I use two modal verbs together?

Not in standard English. *I will can* and *you might should* are incorrect in standard usage. Replace the second modal with a semi-modal: *I will be able to*, *you might have to*.
