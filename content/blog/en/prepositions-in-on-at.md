The **prepositions in, on, at** cause more small errors than almost any other part of English, because every language divides time and space differently. The good news: most everyday uses follow two simple pictures. For time, think **big to small** (*in* a year, *on* a day, *at* a time). For place, think **container, surface, point** (*in* a box, *on* a table, *at* a door). Learn those pictures first, then the short list of fixed exceptions.

## Prepositions in, on, at for time: the big-to-small rule

{{diagram: d1 | In covers the largest periods, on covers single days, at pinpoints exact times}}

### IN: longer periods

Use *in* for years, months, seasons, decades, centuries and long periods:

- *in 2019*, *in March*, *in (the) summer*, *in the 1990s*, *in the 21st century*
- Parts of the day: *in the morning*, *in the afternoon*, *in the evening*

### ON: days and dates

Use *on* for named days, dates and special days:

- *on Monday*, *on 3 June* / *on June 3*, *on New Year's Day*, *on my birthday*
- A day plus a part of the day still takes *on*: *on Sunday evening*, *on the morning of the exam*.

That last point surprises many learners. *In the morning* is correct alone, but once a specific day is attached, the day wins: *on Tuesday morning*.

### AT: exact moments

Use *at* for clock times and short, precise points:

- *at 7:30*, *at noon*, *at midnight*, *at sunrise*, *at the end of the lesson*
- Some fixed expressions: *at night*, *at the moment*, *at the same time*, *at Christmas* / *at Easter* (the holiday period)

**Weekend:** British English says *at the weekend*; American English says *on the weekend*. Both are correct in their own variety. Pick one and be consistent in a piece of writing.

### No preposition at all

Do not add a preposition before *next, last, this, every, today, tomorrow, yesterday*:

- ✗ *I'll call you on next Monday.* → ✓ *I'll call you next Monday.*
- ✗ *We met in last year.* → ✓ *We met last year.*

### "In" for future time from now

*In* + a period can mean "after that amount of time from now": *The train leaves in ten minutes. I'll be back in a week.* Compare *within a week*, which means "before the week is over".

## Prepositions in, on, at for place: container, surface, point

{{diagram: d2 | Picture the space: a point you go to, a surface or line you are on, or a container you are inside}}

### IN: inside an enclosed space or area

*in the box*, *in the kitchen*, *in the car*, *in a taxi*, *in the garden*, *in the photo*, *in the newspaper*, and larger areas: *in Paris*, *in Brazil*, *in the city centre*.

### ON: on a surface or along a line

*on the table*, *on the wall*, *on the ceiling*, *on the screen*, *on page 12*, *on the third floor*, *on the left*, *on the coast*, *on the river* (a town beside the river), *on the motorway*.

### AT: at a point or a place used for its purpose

*at the bus stop*, *at the door*, *at the traffic lights*, *at the top of the page*, and places seen as a function rather than a building: *at work*, *at school*, *at university*, *at home*, *at the doctor's*, *at a party*, *at the airport*.

### At or in for buildings?

Both can be right, with a change in meaning. *She's **at** the cinema* says where she is in terms of activity (watching a film). *It's cold **in** the cinema* refers to the inside of the building. If you mean "inside the walls", use *in*; if you mean "at that place, doing the usual thing there", use *at*.

## Transport: in a car, on a bus

The rule is about whether you can stand up and walk around:

- **on** large public transport: *on the bus*, *on a train*, *on a plane*, *on a ship*, and also *on a bike*, *on a horse* (you sit on top)
- **in** small private vehicles: *in a car*, *in a taxi*, *in a small boat*

The method of travel uses *by* with no article: *by car*, *by train*, but *on foot*.

## Addresses and arrive

- **Exact address:** *at 25 Hill Road*
- **Street name only:** *on Hill Road* (American and increasingly common everywhere), *in Hill Road* (traditional British)
- **City or country:** *in Lisbon*, *in Canada*

**Arrive** never takes *to*. Use *arrive in* for cities and countries, *arrive at* for buildings, stations and events: *We arrived in Rome on Friday and arrived at the hotel at six.* Compare *go to* and *get to*, which do take *to*.

## Fixed pairs that change meaning

| Pair | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **on time** / **in time** | *on time* = at the planned time (*The train left on time*); *in time* = early enough (*We got there in time to see the start*) |
| **at the end** / **in the end** | *at the end of the film* = the final part; *in the end* = finally, after everything (*In the end, we stayed home*) |
| **in the corner** / **at (on) the corner** | *in the corner of the room* = inside; *at / on the corner of the street* = the outside point where two streets meet |
| **in the beginning** / **at the beginning** | *at the beginning of the year* = the first part; *in the beginning* = at first (often contrasting with later) |

Pairs like these are the same kind of trap as the ones in our [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/) guide: the words look similar, but the meanings are fixed.

## Prepositions in exams

In **IELTS Writing Task 1**, time prepositions appear in nearly every sentence: *in 2010*, *between 2010 and 2020*, *from 2010 to 2020*, *by 2030* (no later than). Our [IELTS line graph guide](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) shows these in full model sentences. In **TOEIC Part 5**, preposition items are common and often hinge on a fixed pairing such as *on time* or *in advance*; see our [TOEIC Part 5 tips](/en/blog/toeic-part-5-tips/) for a fast method.

Many preposition errors are not about *in/on/at* at all but about which preposition follows a particular word (*interested in*, *depend on*, *good at*). These "dependent prepositions" are best learned as chunks, a habit described in [learn vocabulary from reading](/en/blog/learn-vocabulary-from-reading/). ## Self-check

Fill each gap with *in*, *on*, *at* or **–** (no preposition).

1. My course starts ___ September.
2. The shop closes ___ 6 p.m. ___ Saturdays.
3. I'll see you ___ next Thursday.
4. There's a spider ___ the ceiling.
5. She's not here; she's ___ work.
6. We met ___ the plane to Tokyo.
7. They live ___ 14 Park Avenue.
8. Our flight arrives ___ Madrid ___ the evening.
9. The meeting started exactly ___ time.
10. ___ the end of the article there's a quiz.

### Answers

1. **in** – months.
2. **at** – clock time; **on** – days.
3. **–** – no preposition before *next*.
4. **on** – a surface (the ceiling is a surface, even upside down).
5. **at** – *at work* (the place as a function).
6. **on** – large public transport.
7. **at** – exact address.
8. **in** – a city; **in** – part of the day without a named day.
9. **on** – *on time* = at the planned time.
10. **At** – the final part of something.

Nine or ten correct is a strong result. For anything you missed, write two new sentences of your own using the same rule and test yourself again in a few days.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it "in the weekend", "on the weekend" or "at the weekend"?

*At the weekend* is British English and *on the weekend* is American English. *In the weekend* is not standard in either. Both accepted forms are fine in exams; just stay consistent.

### Why is it "in the morning" but "at night"?

*In the morning*, *in the afternoon* and *in the evening* follow the rule for periods of time. *At night* is a fixed expression that developed differently, so it must be memorised. Note that *in the night* also exists, meaning during one particular night: *I woke up twice in the night.*

### Do I say "in the bus" or "on the bus"?

*On the bus*. Large public transport, where you can stand and walk, takes *on*. Small vehicles such as cars and taxis take *in*.

### Is "arrive to" ever correct?

No. Use *arrive in* for cities and countries and *arrive at* for buildings, stations and events. *Go to* and *get to* are the verbs that take *to*.
