The **IELTS Reading question types** fall into four families: tasks that ask you to locate facts, to complete a text, to match ideas, and to choose between options. The test gives you 60 minutes for 40 questions over three passages, with no extra time to copy answers, so knowing each family in advance saves minutes. This guide describes every task, gives a method that fits it, and shows the traps that cost marks.

## The four families of IELTS Reading question types

{{diagram: d1 | Group the tasks by skill; the method for one often works for its neighbours}}

Academic and General Training use the same kinds of tasks, though the passages differ. In both, the order of the questions usually follows the order of the text within one task, except for matching headings and a few matching tasks, where the order can differ.

## Read the instructions before the passage

Many candidates start with the passage and meet the questions later. A better habit is to spend thirty seconds on the question sets first. Note which types appear, because they tell you how to read. A passage followed by matching headings needs attention to the main idea of each paragraph; a passage followed by True/False/Not Given and completion needs close reading of details. Look at any title, subheadings and numbers in the questions, then skim the passage once for its structure. This short survey stops you from reading everything word by word, which is a common reason candidates run short of time on the later passages.

## Time: a plan for 60 minutes

In the Academic test the passages tend to get harder. General Training is organised differently, with shorter everyday texts in the early sections and a longer text at the end. A simple rule:

{{diagram: d2 | About twenty minutes a passage, with the last minutes of the hour kept for checking}}

If a question takes more than a minute and a half, mark it, guess, and return later. Marks are the same for every question, so fast questions deserve your time first.

## Multiple choice

You choose one or more answers from a list. Wrong options are usually partly right: they use words from the passage but change a detail, give the wrong cause, or say more than the text says.

- Read the question stem, find the part of the passage, then read the sentences before and after.
- Eliminate options that contradict the text or that go beyond it.
- When you must choose more than one answer, keep a clear count; the order of the answers need not match the text.

## True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given

These tasks test whether you can compare meaning exactly. The key distinction is False (the text contradicts the statement) versus Not Given (the text does not address it). A full method is in [True False Not Given: how to tell False from Not Given](/en/blog/ielts-reading-true-false-not-given/). Yes/No/Not Given works the same way but asks about the writer's views and claims.

## Matching headings

For matching headings you choose the best title for each paragraph. Read the first and last sentences of the paragraph for the main idea, then compare with the list; some headings are too narrow or too broad, and several can share a word with the text. The dedicated guide to [matching headings](/en/blog/ielts-reading-matching-headings/) shows how to avoid detail traps.

## Matching information, features and sentence endings

- **Matching information**: you find which paragraph contains a given detail. The answer is a paragraph letter and the same paragraph can be used more than once unless the instruction says otherwise.
- **Matching features**: you link statements to people, places or categories named in a list. Scan for the names first.
- **Matching sentence endings**: you finish half a sentence with an ending from a list. Check grammar fit first, then meaning.

These tasks usually need a quick scan and then careful reading of two or three sentences, so make notes of keywords and names as you go. Practise the scanning technique in [skimming and scanning in IELTS Reading](/en/blog/ielts-reading-skimming-and-scanning/).

## Completion tasks: sentence, summary, note, table, flow chart

Completion tasks ask for words taken from the passage. Always check the word limit (for example, no more than two words). Then:

1. Decide what type of word fits: noun, adjective, number, verb form.
2. Find the matching part of the text; the information is paraphrased, not copied.
3. Copy the word exactly as it appears in the passage, including spelling.

If the summary has a word bank instead of a word limit, match by grammar first: choose an adjective for an adjective gap, and so on.

Take an original example. The passage says, "Rainfall in the valley has become less predictable, and farmers have responded by planting a wider range of crops." The item reads: *Farmers have responded to the valley's ____ rainfall by growing more kinds of crops.* The gap needs an adjective, and the passage offers *predictable* only inside *less predictable*, so you cannot copy a ready-made answer from it. In a real completion task the passage would contain the exact word, for example *unpredictable rainfall*, and you would copy it with the same spelling. The lesson is to let the word limit, the grammar of the sentence and the passage all agree before you write.

## Short-answer questions

You answer in a few words. Read the question words (who, what, when, how many) and look for the matching information using the wording of the question. Do not exceed the word limit, and do not add information that is not in the text.

## Diagram label completion

You label parts of a diagram or process with words from the passage. First look at the diagram and read the labels you already have; follow the process in the text step by step.

## Common traps in all Reading tasks

| Trap | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Spotting a keyword and answering at once | The same word appears near several ideas | Read the whole sentence and the next one |
| Using general knowledge | You know the topic | Use only the passage |
| Ignoring qualifiers | Words such as *mainly* or *only* change meaning | Underline limiting words |
| Exceeding the word limit | Rushing | Read the instruction each time |
| Spending too long on one question | Pride, or worry | Mark it and move on |

Long sentences are another trap; [how to read long sentences in English](/en/blog/sentence-parsing-for-ielts-reading/) shows how to cut them down quickly. For managing the clock, see [IELTS Reading time management](/en/blog/ielts-reading-time-management-strategies/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many question types are there in IELTS Reading?

There are around a dozen formats, grouped into tasks that locate facts, complete a text, match ideas or choose between options. Each test uses a selection of them over three passages.

### Do I lose marks for wrong answers?

No. Every correct answer earns a mark and wrong answers lose nothing, so always give an answer even when you are unsure.

### Which Reading question type is hardest?

Many candidates find True/False/Not Given and matching headings hardest, because they need precise reasoning about meaning. Your own error log will show your weakest type.

### Can I write on the question paper?

On the paper-based test you can underline and make notes on the question paper, and you must copy your answers to the answer sheet within the hour. On the computer-delivered test you work on screen. Check the rules for your test centre.
