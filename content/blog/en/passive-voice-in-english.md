The **passive voice** puts the *receiver* of an action at the start of the sentence: *The window was broken* instead of *Someone broke the window*. It is not a "harder" or "more advanced" version of the active; it is a tool for choosing what your sentence is about. This guide shows how to form the passive in any tense, when it is the better choice, and the mistakes that examiners notice most.

## How to form the passive voice

The formula never changes: **a form of *be* + past participle (V3)**. The tense is carried by *be*; the main verb is always the past participle.

{{diagram: d1 | The object of the active sentence becomes the subject; the doer moves to an optional by-phrase}}

To change an active sentence into the passive:

1. Make the **object** the new subject: *the plan*.
2. Put ***be*** in the same tense as the original verb: *approved* (past simple) → *was*.
3. Add the **past participle**: *approved*.
4. Add *by* + the doer only if it is useful information.

{{diagram: d2 | Only the form of be changes from tense to tense; the past participle stays the same}}

Two forms deserve extra attention:

- **Continuous passive:** *be being* + V3. *The road **is being repaired**, so expect delays.*
- **Modal passive:** modal + *be* + V3. *Applications **must be submitted** by Friday.* In the past: modal + *have been* + V3. *The letter **should have been sent** yesterday.*

## When to use the passive voice

Choose the passive when the **action or its receiver** matters more than the doer. Four situations cover most uses.

### 1. The doer is unknown

*My bike **was stolen** last night.* You do not know who stole it, so the active (*Someone stole my bike*) adds an empty subject.

### 2. The doer is obvious or unimportant

*The suspect **was arrested** on Monday.* (Obviously by the police.) *English **is spoken** in many countries.* (By people; saying so adds nothing.)

### 3. You want to keep the topic in subject position

Good writing keeps the topic of a paragraph at the start of its sentences. Compare:

- *The Eiffel Tower is the symbol of Paris. Gustave Eiffel's company built it for the 1889 World's Fair.*
- *The Eiffel Tower is the symbol of Paris. It **was built** for the 1889 World's Fair.*

The second version keeps *the Eiffel Tower* as the topic, so the paragraph flows.

### 4. Formal, scientific and process writing

Reports, instructions and descriptions of processes focus on what happens, not who does it: *The samples **were heated** to 60°C. The bottles **are collected**, **cleaned** and **refilled**.* This is why the passive is essential in **IELTS Writing Task 1** process diagrams; our [IELTS line graph guide](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) covers the trend language for charts, and the passive takes over when the task shows a process.

## When the active is better

Overusing the passive makes writing vague and heavy. Prefer the active when the doer is important, especially in opinions and arguments:

- ✗ *It is believed by me that the policy will fail.*
- ✓ *I believe the policy will fail.*

Readers also want to know who is responsible: *The government **cut** the budget* is clearer and stronger than *The budget was cut* when the point is the government's decision. Examiners reward the *right* choice, not the *number* of passives, a point our article on [the sophistication trap in IELTS writing](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) makes about complex language in general.

## Verbs that cannot be passive

Only verbs with an object (transitive verbs) can be passive. Intransitive verbs have no object to move into subject position:

- ✗ *The accident was happened at night.* → ✓ *The accident happened at night.*
- ✗ *He was died in 1950.* → ✓ *He died in 1950.*
- ✗ *The train was arrived late.* → ✓ *The train arrived late.*

Other common intransitive verbs: *occur, appear, seem, exist, rise, fall, belong, consist (of)*. In IELTS Task 1, note that *rise* and *fall* are intransitive: *Sales rose*, never *Sales were risen*. Their transitive partners are *raise* and *lower*: *The company raised prices* → *Prices were raised*.

## Verbs with two objects

Verbs such as *give, send, offer, show, tell, teach, lend* have two objects, and either can become the passive subject:

- Active: *The company offered Maria a contract.*
- Passive 1: ***Maria** was offered a contract.* (more common: a person is usually the topic)
- Passive 2: ***A contract** was offered to Maria.*

## Passive reporting structures

To report what people in general say, think or believe, formal English uses two patterns:

- ***It is said that** the castle is haunted.*
- *The castle **is said to be** haunted.*

Other verbs that work this way: *believe, think, expect, know, report, consider, estimate*. For the past, use the perfect infinitive: *The fire **is thought to have started** in the kitchen.*

These structures are excellent for academic hedging, since they report a claim without fully committing to it. Our guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) shows how to combine them with *may* and *tend to*.

## The causative: have or get something done

When you arrange for **someone else** to do something for you, use *have* or *get* + object + past participle:

- *I **had my car serviced** last week.* (A mechanic did it.)
- *She's **getting her eyes tested** tomorrow.*

Compare *I repaired my laptop* (I did it myself) with *I had my laptop repaired* (a technician did it). *Get* is a little more informal than *have*.

The causative can also describe something bad that happened to you: *He **had his wallet stolen** on holiday.*

## Common mistakes with the passive voice

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| *The report was write by Tom.* | *The report was written by Tom.* | base form instead of V3 |
| *The house built in 1920.* | *The house was built in 1920.* | missing *be* |
| *The problem was occurred.* | *The problem occurred.* | intransitive verb |
| *It is being repair now.* | *It is being repaired now.* | missing -ed on V3 |
| *The results was published.* | *The results were published.* | *be* must agree with the subject |
| *I was given it by somebody.* | *Somebody gave it to me.* / *I was given it.* | empty by-phrase |

Irregular past participles cause many of these errors (*written, chosen, built, taken, given*). If they slow you down, drill them in short daily sessions; the [spaced repetition guide](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) explains the most efficient way.

## Self-check

Rewrite each sentence in the passive. Omit the doer when it adds nothing.

1. Someone cleans the office every evening.
2. They are building a new hospital.
3. The storm damaged several houses.
4. People have reported three cases.
5. You must keep the medicine in a cool place.
6. People say that the restaurant is excellent. (two ways)
7. A mechanic is going to repair my car. (causative with *have*)
8. Is this sentence possible in the passive? *The meeting lasted two hours.*

### Answers

1. *The office is cleaned every evening.*
2. *A new hospital is being built.*
3. *Several houses were damaged by the storm.* (Keep *the storm*: it is the useful information.)
4. *Three cases have been reported.*
5. *The medicine must be kept in a cool place.*
6. *It is said that the restaurant is excellent.* / *The restaurant is said to be excellent.*
7. *I'm going to have my car repaired.*
8. **No.** *Last* is not a transitive verb here; *two hours* is a duration, not an object.

Seven or eight correct shows solid control. When you read academic texts, notice how often the passive keeps the topic in subject position; the method in [sentence parsing for IELTS Reading](/en/blog/sentence-parsing-for-ielts-reading/) helps you find the real subject in long passive sentences.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the passive voice bad style?

No. Style guides warn against *overusing* it, not against using it. The passive is the natural choice when the doer is unknown, obvious or unimportant, and in scientific and process writing.

### Do I always need "by" in the passive?

No. Most passive sentences in real English have no by-phrase. Include *by* only when the doer is new or important information: *The novel was written **by a 17-year-old**.*

### What is the difference between "be" passive and "get" passive?

*Get* passives (*He got promoted*, *My phone got broken*) are informal and often describe something sudden, unexpected or bad. In formal writing, use *be*.

### Can the passive be used in IELTS Speaking?

Yes, naturally, for example *My hometown is known for its seafood* or *I was brought up by my grandparents*. Use it when it fits the meaning; do not force it to show range.
