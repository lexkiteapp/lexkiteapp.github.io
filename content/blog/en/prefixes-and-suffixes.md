Once you know a few common **prefixes and suffixes**, one new word becomes five. If you know *happy*, you can quickly understand *unhappy*, *happiness* and *happily*. Native speakers do this without thinking, and learners who study word parts usually grow their vocabulary faster than those who memorise isolated words. This guide gives you the most useful prefixes and suffixes in English, the spelling rules that trip people up, and a method to practise them for exams such as IELTS, TOEFL and TOEIC.

## What are prefixes and suffixes?

A **prefix** is a group of letters added to the **beginning** of a word. A **suffix** is added to the **end**. The main part, which carries the core meaning, is the **root** or base.

{{diagram: d1 | Prefix + root + suffix. Not every word has both: you can use only a prefix or only a suffix.}}

A prefix usually changes the **meaning** (*do → undo*) but not the word class. A suffix often changes the **word class** (*teach → teacher*, a verb becomes a noun), and sometimes also the meaning. This is the basis of word formation, one of the most tested skills in exams where you must change a word to fit a sentence. See also our guide to [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

## Common prefixes

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un- | not, opposite | unhappy, unfair, undo, unlock |
| in- / im- / il- / ir- | not | incorrect, impossible, illegal, irregular |
| dis- | not, opposite | disagree, dislike, disappear |
| mis- | wrongly | misunderstand, misspell, mislead |
| re- | again | rewrite, rebuild, return |
| pre- | before | preview, prepare, prehistoric |
| over- | too much | overwork, overeat, overcook |
| under- | not enough | underpaid, underestimate |
| co- | together | cooperate, coexist |
| anti- | against | anti-social, antibiotic |
| inter- | between | international, interact |
| sub- | under | subway, submarine |
| super- | above, beyond | supermarket, superhuman |
| semi- | half | semicircle, semi-final |
| multi- | many | multicultural, multilingual |

### Which negative prefix?

English has several, and there is no perfect rule. Some helpful tendencies:

- **un-** is the most common and goes with adjectives and verbs: *unusual, unlock*.
- **in-** changes to **im-** before *b, m, p*: *impossible, immature, imbalance*; to **il-** before *l*: *illegal*; to **ir-** before *r*: *irregular*.
- **dis-** often goes with verbs and nouns: *disagree, dishonest, disadvantage*.
- **non-** is common in technical and formal words: *non-fiction, non-stop*.

When unsure, check a dictionary: *unpossible* is wrong, and *dishappy* does not exist.

### Adverb suffix

**-ly** forms most adverbs from adjectives: *quick → quickly*, *careful → carefully*.

## Spelling rules when you add a suffix

1. **Silent e:** drop the *e* before a suffix beginning with a vowel: *create → creation*, *love → lovable*, but keep it before a consonant: *love → lovely*, *care → careful*. Exceptions exist: *noticeable*, *manageable*.
2. **Final y after a consonant:** change *y* to *i*: *happy → happiness*, *beauty → beautiful*, *easy → easily*.
3. **Doubling the final consonant:** for one-syllable words with one vowel and one final consonant: *run → running*, *big → bigger*, *stop → stopped*. Also for longer words with stress on the last syllable: *begin → beginning*.
4. **Adding -ly to words ending in -le:** drop the *e* and add *y*: *comfortable → comfortably*.
5. **Adding -ly to words ending in -ic:** add *-ally*: *basic → basically*.
6. **A prefix does not change the spelling of the root:** *un + necessary = unnecessary*, *dis + appear = disappear*, *mis + spell = misspell*.

## Word formation in exams

Many tests, including some Cambridge exams and part of TOEIC Part 5, ask you to choose or change a word form. In IELTS, correct word forms improve your grammar and vocabulary accuracy. Example: *"The ____ (educate) system needs more funding."* The gap comes before the noun *system*, so you need an adjective: **educational**. Practise with our guides to [TOEIC Part 5 strategies](/en/blog/toeic-part-5-grammar-and-vocabulary-strategies/) and [more TOEIC Part 5 tips](/en/blog/toeic-part-5-tips/). To improve your vocabulary score, read [lexical resource in IELTS](/en/blog/lexical-resource-ielts/) and the [academic word list](/en/blog/academic-word-list/).

A reliable method:

1. Look at the position of the gap. Is there an article, adjective or verb nearby?
2. Decide the **word class** needed (noun, verb, adjective, adverb).
3. Choose the suffix that matches that class.
4. Check the meaning: negative or positive? You may need a prefix.

## Worked examples: from one root to a whole family

Take the root **create**. Add parts one at a time and watch the meaning and word class change:

- *create* (verb) → *creation* (noun, drop the *e*, add *-ion*)
- *creative* (adjective, *-ive*) → *creatively* (adverb, *-ly*)
- *creativity* (noun, *-ity*) → *creator* (noun, person)
- *recreate* (verb, prefix *re-*) → *uncreative* (adjective, prefix *un-*)

Now **employ**: *employee* (the person who is employed), *employer* (the person who employs), *employment* (the state or the job), *unemployed* (without a job), *unemployment* (the situation), *reemployment* (employment again). That is eight useful words from one root, and all of them appear in news, essays and workplace emails.

A practical exercise: pick five root words from your current reading, write the family for each in a table with columns for noun, verb, adjective and adverb, then check each form in a dictionary. Gaps in the table are normal because not every root has all four forms; do not invent them.

## Learning tips

- **Learn in families.** When you meet *decide*, also learn *decision, decisive, decisively, undecided*.
- **Notice patterns in text.** When you read, underline word parts.
- **Use a dictionary.** English-English dictionaries show word families; see our [guide to using an English-English dictionary](/en/blog/how-to-use-an-english-english-dictionary/).
- **Review with spaced repetition.** Add the word family on one card. See [spaced repetition](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/).
- **Be careful with meaning.** *Flammable* and *inflammable* mean the same, and *valuable* and *invaluable* are both positive. Prefixes are not always opposites.

## Self-check

1. Add the correct prefix: ____ possible, ____ legal, ____ regular.
2. Form the noun: *decide, move, kind*.
3. Form the adverb: *happy, comfortable, basic*.
4. What happens to the *y* in *beauty + ful*?
5. Which word is wrong: *unusual, dishonest, unpossible*?

### Answers

1. **impossible, illegal, irregular.**
2. **decision, movement, kindness.**
3. **happily, comfortably, basically.**
4. It changes to *i*: **beautiful**.
5. **unpossible** (the correct form is *impossible*).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a prefix and a suffix?

A prefix comes at the start of a word and usually changes its meaning. A suffix comes at the end and often changes the word class.

### Do prefixes change the spelling of the root?

No. The root stays the same: *un + necessary = unnecessary*, with both n's.

### How many should I learn?

Start with 10 prefixes and 15 suffixes from the tables above. They cover thousands of words.

### Are all words with these parts regular?

No. Some words look like they have a prefix but do not, such as *understand* or *discuss*. Always check a dictionary.
