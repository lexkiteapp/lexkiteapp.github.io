If you want to know how to learn IELTS vocabulary so that it is still there on test day, the short answer is this: learn 10–15 words a day at your level, learn each one inside a phrase, test yourself instead of rereading, and bring every word back on a spacing schedule until you have used it in your own sentence. Long word lists copied into a notebook feel productive, but most of those words fade within days because they were never retrieved or used.

The rest of this guide turns that answer into seven concrete steps, a sample 30-minute routine, and a short self-check so you can see where your current method leaks.

## Why IELTS vocabulary disappears so quickly

Most learners who say "I have a bad memory" actually have a process problem. Three habits cause most of the loss.

- **Studying words that are too far above your level.** A band 5.5 candidate memorising *quintessential* and *ubiquitous* is spending effort on words they will rarely meet and almost never use correctly. Rare words are also the hardest to remember, because nothing you already know supports them.
- **Learning a word without its partners.** Knowing that *impact* means "effect" is not enough. You also need *have an impact on*, *a significant impact*, *the impact of X on Y*. Without these, learners produce *impact to* or *make an impact for*, which an examiner notices immediately.
- **No plan for review.** Memory drops steeply in the first days after learning. If the first review comes two weeks later, you are relearning, not reviewing.

Learners from different backgrounds lose marks in slightly different places. Speakers of Hindi, Urdu, Arabic or Tagalog often know a large passive vocabulary from school and media but struggle to produce precise word partnerships. Learners whose first language has no word endings for tense or plural, such as Chinese, Thai or Vietnamese, tend to know the base word but drop forms like *economically* or *developments*. Spanish and Portuguese speakers get help from shared Latin roots, yet fall into false friends such as *actually* (which does not mean "currently") or *sensible* (which does not mean "sensitive"). Knowing your own pattern tells you what to watch for in step 3.

## How to learn IELTS vocabulary in 7 steps

### Step 1: Check your base before chasing "IELTS words"

IELTS texts are built on everyday high-frequency words. If those are shaky, academic words will not save you, because you will still misunderstand the sentences around them. A quick way to check: read a short article on a familiar topic and underline every word you could not explain to a friend. If more than one word in twenty is underlined, spend a few weeks on core vocabulary first. The free [IELTS vocabulary lists by topic](/en/vocabulary/) are a useful map of what comes next.

### Step 2: Choose 10–15 words a day, not 50

Small daily batches beat heroic weekends. Ten new words a day is about 300 a month, and you can actually review them. Choose words that meet two conditions: you have seen them more than once in your reading or listening, and you can imagine using them in an answer. For most candidates aiming at band 6.5–7, that means words like *affordable*, *vulnerable*, *drawback*, *sustainable* and *contribute*, not literary rarities.

### Step 3: Learn chunks and context, not single meanings

Write every new word inside a phrase and an example sentence you made yourself. Compare these two notebook entries:

| Weak entry | Strong entry |
|---|---|
| *reliance* = dependence | *heavy reliance on imported fuel*; "Many island nations have a heavy reliance on imported fuel." |
| *tackle* = deal with | *tackle a problem / tackle unemployment*; "Governments need long-term plans to tackle youth unemployment." |

The strong entry gives you the preposition, a natural adjective and a ready sentence frame. For a deeper look at word partnerships, read the guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/). If a new word has a near-twin that you keep mixing up, study the pair together, as shown in [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

### Step 4: Say every word out loud

A word you cannot pronounce is a word you will not recognise in Listening and will avoid in Speaking. Check the stress and say the phrase, not just the word, three times: *a SIGnificant DROP*, *enVIronmental proTECtion*. Pay special attention to words whose stress moves between forms, such as *PHOtograph* and *phoTOgraphy*.

### Step 5: Review on a spacing schedule

Instead of reviewing everything every day, review each word just before you are likely to forget it: a day after learning, then a few days later, then a week or two later. Each successful recall pushes the next review further away. The logic and a sample calendar are explained in [spaced repetition for language learning](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/), and if you are curious how modern apps calculate those gaps, see [the FSRS algorithm explained](/en/blog/fsrs-algorithm-explained/).

### Step 6: Test yourself instead of rereading

Rereading a list feels comfortable because the words look familiar. Familiar is not the same as retrievable. Cover the meaning and try to produce it, or see the definition and type the word. The effort of pulling a word out of memory is what strengthens it. This is the core idea behind [active recall](/en/blog/active-recall-for-vocabulary/), and it works best when you are honest: if you hesitated for five seconds, mark the card as hard.

### Step 7: Use new words in Writing and Speaking within a week

A word becomes yours when you have produced it correctly in a real sentence. Each week, write one Task 2 paragraph or record a two-minute Speaking answer that uses at least five of that week's words. Then compare with a model: the [band 8 sample essays](/en/samples/) are a good place to see how strong candidates place words like *alleviate* or *disproportionately* in context.

## A sample 30-minute daily vocabulary routine

Consistency matters more than length. This routine fits a lunch break or a commute.

| Minutes | Activity | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | Review due flashcards | Keep old words alive |
| 10–18 | Learn 10 new words with phrases and your own sentences | Add new material carefully |
| 18–25 | Read or listen to one short text and catch familiar words | See words in real context |
| 25–30 | Write three sentences or speak for one minute using today's words | Turn recognition into use |

On a busy day, do only the first block. Reviews that pile up are the main reason people quit flashcards, so protect those ten minutes even when you skip new words. When planning your preparation timeline, structure your weekly targets following an actionable [IELTS self-study plan](/en/blog/ielts-self-study-plan/) so your lexical development directly supports all four modules.

## Common mistakes when building IELTS vocabulary

- **Collecting synonyms for paraphrasing.** Many learners keep lists like *big = huge = enormous = immense*. These words are not interchangeable: *a huge problem* is natural, *an immense problem* is less common, and *an enormous decision* sounds odd. Learn the phrase each word lives in.
- **Using every new word immediately.** Forcing *plethora* into a Speaking Part 1 answer about your hometown sounds unnatural. Match the word to the register.
- **Ignoring word forms.** The academic word *analyse* has *analysis*, *analytical* and *analyst*. Wrong word forms (*an economic growth*, *the develop of cities*) cost Lexical Resource and Grammar marks at the same time; the [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/) guide shows how to learn whole families.
- **Measuring progress by word count.** Two hundred words you can use beat a thousand you merely recognise. Building lexical recognition also underpins rapid [skimming and scanning in IELTS Reading](/en/blog/ielts-reading-skimming-and-scanning/), where spotting keywords quickly saves critical test minutes.

## Self-check: is your method working?

Answer each question honestly with yes or no.

1. Can you write a correct sentence with at least eight of the last ten words you studied?
2. Do you know the preposition that follows *emphasis*, *reliance* and *solution*?
3. Do you review on a schedule rather than "when I have time"?
4. Have you used any word from last week in a written or spoken answer?
5. Can you pronounce *environment*, *comfortable* and *development* with the correct stress?

**Answers and what they mean:** For question 2, the patterns are *emphasis on*, *reliance on* and *solution to*. For question 5, the stress falls on *enVIronment*, *COMfortable* (usually three syllables) and *deVELopment*. If you answered "no" to questions 1 or 4, your weak point is production: add step 7 every week. A "no" to question 3 means words are leaking between sessions, so set a fixed daily review time. Two or more "no" answers suggest you are learning too many new words; cut your daily batch in half for two weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many IELTS words should I learn per day?

For most self-studying candidates, 10–15 new words a day is sustainable alongside daily review. If your reviews start taking more than 20 minutes, pause new words for a few days until the queue shrinks.

### How many words do I need for IELTS band 7?

There is no official word count for any band. Research on reading coverage (Nation, 2006) suggests that understanding about 98% of the words in an authentic written text takes roughly 8,000–9,000 word families, which is why Reading gets easier long after you stop "learning IELTS words". For Writing and Speaking, examiners look at how precisely and flexibly you use words, so a few thousand well-controlled families with their collocations matter more than a large passive list.

### Should I learn vocabulary by topic or from a list like the AWL?

Use both for different purposes. Topic vocabulary (environment, education, technology) gives you ideas and phrases for Speaking and Task 2, while the Academic Word List covers words that appear across all academic texts, which helps most in Reading and Writing.

### Are flashcards enough to reach band 7?

Flashcards are excellent for remembering, but band descriptors reward the accurate and flexible use of vocabulary. Combine flashcards with reading, listening and regular writing or speaking practice so that words move from recognition to use.

### Is it worth learning synonyms for paraphrasing?

Yes, but learn them as phrases with their typical partners rather than as one-to-one swaps. Paraphrasing also uses changes of word form and sentence structure, not only synonyms.
