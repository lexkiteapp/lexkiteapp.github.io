**Make vs do** is one of the first vocabulary problems every learner meets, and one of the last to disappear. Both verbs can translate as the same word in many languages (*hacer* in Spanish, *fazer* in Portuguese, *faire* in French), and in Hindi many make-expressions use *karna*, the verb for *do*, so learners guess. The short rule: **make** is for **creating or producing** something, and **do** is for **performing an activity or task**. The longer truth is that many combinations are fixed collocations you simply have to learn. This guide gives you both: the logic and the lists.

## Make vs do: the core difference

{{diagram: d1 | Make focuses on the result you create; do focuses on the activity you perform}}

**Make** usually means bringing something into existence: you start with nothing (or with ingredients) and end with a product.

- *She **made** a cake.* (There is now a cake.)
- *They **made** a plan.* (There is now a plan.)
- *The engine **makes** a strange noise.* (A sound is produced.)

**Do** usually means carrying out an action, often a task, job or routine. The focus is on the activity, not on a new object.

- *I **do** the laundry on Sundays.*
- *He **did** his homework before dinner.*
- *What do you **do**?* (= What is your job?)

One test: ask *"What exists afterwards?"* If the answer is a new thing (a meal, a document, a decision, a phone call), think **make**. If the answer is "the job is finished" (clean dishes, completed exercises), think **do**.

## When to use make

Use **make** with:

- **Food and drinks:** *make breakfast, make tea, make a sandwich* (but *do the cooking*).
- **Plans and decisions:** *make a plan, make a decision, make arrangements, make a choice.*
- **Sounds and speech acts:** *make a noise, make a speech, make a complaint, make a suggestion, make a promise, make a comment.*
- **Changes and progress:** *make a change, make progress, make improvements, make a difference.*
- **Money and business:** *make money, make a profit, make a loss, make a deal, make an offer.*
- **Relationships and contact:** *make friends, make contact, make a phone call, make an appointment.*
- **Errors:** *make a mistake, make an error.*
- **Effort and attempts:** *make an effort, make an attempt, make sure.*
- **Causing a reaction:** *make someone laugh, make someone angry* (make + object + verb/adjective).

## When to use do

Use **do** with:

- **Housework and routines:** *do the dishes, do the laundry, do the shopping, do the ironing, do your hair.*
- **Work and study tasks:** *do homework, do an exercise, do a course, do research, do a test* (also *take a test*), *do business.*
- **Jobs and duties:** *do your job, do your duty, do overtime, do a shift.*
- **Sports without "play":** *do yoga, do karate, do exercise, do gymnastics.*
- **Effects (good or bad):** *do damage, do harm, do good, do someone a favour.*
- **Vague activity words:** *do something, do nothing, do anything, do everything, do whatever you like.*
- **Performance and results:** *do well, do badly, do your best.*

## Tricky collocations to memorise

These are the pairs learners get wrong most often, because the logic is weak or the opposite of their first language:

| ✓ Correct | ✗ Common error |
|---|---|
| make a mistake | do a mistake |
| make a decision | do a decision |
| make a complaint | do a complaint |
| make an effort | do an effort |
| make a phone call | do a phone call |
| make progress | do progress |
| make friends | do friends |
| do homework | make homework |
| do research | make research |
| do a favour | make a favour |
| do exercise | make exercise |
| do business | make business |
| do damage | make damage |
| do your best | make your best |

Notice some apparent contradictions: you *make breakfast* but *do the cooking*; you *make a bed* (arrange it) but *do the housework*. That is why collocation lists matter more than rules for the last 20% of cases. Our guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) explains how to learn word partners in chunks.

## Make and do in phrasal verbs and expressions

Both verbs form common phrasal verbs and idioms, which often have meanings you can't guess:

- **make up** (invent a story; reconcile after an argument): *He made up an excuse.* / *They argued but made up the next day.*
- **make up for** (compensate): *I'll make up for missing your party.*
- **make out** (see or hear with difficulty): *I couldn't make out the sign.*
- **make it** (arrive in time / succeed): *We made it to the airport just in time.*
- **do up** (fasten; renovate): *Do up your seatbelt.* / *They're doing up an old flat.*
- **do without** (manage without): *I can't do without coffee in the morning.*
- **have to do with** (be connected to): *The problem has to do with the battery.*
- **could do with** (would like / need): *I could do with a holiday.*

If phrasal verbs are a weak area, our list of [common phrasal verbs](/en/blog/common-phrasal-verbs/) covers the ones you will meet most often.

## Why learners mix up make and do

Many first languages use one verb for both meanings, so learners translate directly. A Spanish speaker thinks *hacer un error* and says *do a mistake*; a Hindi speaker uses *galti karna* and produces the same error. Other languages split the space differently from English, so even a two-verb system can mislead.

Three typical patterns:

1. **Overusing do** with nouns that need make: *do a decision, do a call, do a plan.*
2. **Overusing make** with tasks: *make homework, make the shopping, make sport.*
3. **Missing the causative make:** *The film made me cry* (not *did me cry*), *Don't make me wait* (not *make me to wait*: no *to* after make + object).

Collocation errors like these lower your Lexical Resource score in IELTS more than you might expect, because examiners notice them as non-native. Our guide to [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/) covers other pairs with the same problem.

## Self-check: make or do?

Fill each gap with the correct form of *make* or *do*.

1. I need to ___ an appointment with the dentist.
2. Have you ___ the washing-up yet?
3. Our team ___ a lot of progress this month.
4. She ___ a degree in engineering at Delhi University.
5. Smoking ___ serious damage to your lungs.
6. Can you ___ me a favour and close the window?
7. He ___ a really good point in the meeting.
8. Don't worry if you ___ a few mistakes at first.
9. We need to ___ some research before we ___ a decision.
10. That noise is ___ me crazy.

### Answers

1. **make** an appointment
2. **done** the washing-up (a household task)
3. **made** progress
4. **did** a degree (a course of study; *studied for* also works)
5. **does** damage
6. **do** me a favour
7. **made** a point
8. **make** mistakes
9. **do** research … **make** a decision
10. **making** me crazy (causative *make* + object + adjective; *driving me crazy* is also common)

9–10 correct: excellent. 6–8: review the tricky collocations table. Below 6: go back to the core rule, then learn the lists in small groups of five.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it "make homework" or "do homework"?

*Do homework.* Homework is a task you complete, so it takes *do*. The same applies to *do an exercise*, and *do a project*.

### Why is it "make a mistake" and not "do a mistake"?

*Mistake* is a fixed collocation with *make*, like *make an error* and *make a decision*. You can explain it as producing a result (the mistake now exists), but in practice it is best learned as a chunk.

### Is it "make exercise" or "do exercise"?

*Do exercise* (or simply *exercise*, as a verb). Use *do* for activities without a ball or opponent: *do yoga, do karate*. With ball games and competitive sports, use *play*: *play football, play tennis*.

### What does "make do" mean?

*Make do (with something)* means to manage with what you have, even if it isn't ideal: *There was no milk, so we made do with black coffee.* It is one of the few expressions that uses both verbs together.
