Money is the one area where a small language mistake can cost you something real: a missed payment, a blocked card, a fee you did not expect. The good news is that **English phrases at the bank** are quite fixed, and about thirty sentences cover most visits. This guide takes you through them in the order you will meet them: opening an account, everyday transactions, asking about fees, sending money, and what to say when something goes wrong.

It is written for newcomers and international students in the US, Canada, the UK, Australia and New Zealand. It is language help, not financial advice: the products, fees and rules differ between banks and countries, so ask your own bank for the exact terms and read anything you sign.

## Know the local words first

The same thing has different names depending on where you live, and bank forms use the local terms.

{{diagram: d1 | A few common banking words by country. Use the local term on forms and in conversation.}}

Other useful distinctions. A **checking** (US), **chequing** (Canada) or **current** (UK) account is the everyday account for your salary and payments, while a **savings account** is for money you keep and may earn interest. A **debit card** takes money from your account straight away; a **credit card** lets you borrow up to a limit and pay later. In the UK you may hear **direct debit** (an automatic payment you agree with a company) and **standing order** (a fixed payment you set up from your account). In Canada, **e-transfer** is a very common way to send money by email or phone number, and in the US people often use apps; ask your bank which options it supports.

## Opening an account: English phrases at the bank counter

When you arrive, someone usually greets you and asks how they can help. The phrases below are enough for the whole conversation.

**What you say**

- *Hi, I'd like to open an account, please.*
- *I'd like to open a checking account.* (US; use *current account* in the UK.)
- *What do I need to bring?* / *What documents do you need?*
- *Is there a monthly fee?*
- *Is there a minimum balance?*
- *Can I get a debit card with it?*
- *Can I set up online banking?*

**What you may hear**

- *Good morning. How can I help you today?*
- *Do you have a photo ID?* (A passport or a driver's licence.)
- *Can I see proof of address?* (A bill or a lease with your name on it.)
- *Are you opening an individual or a joint account?*
- *Please sign here.*

The documents you need can include ID, proof of address and sometimes immigration papers or a student letter, and they vary by bank, so ask when you book: *What do I need to bring to open an account as a new resident?* The word is **open** an account. *Make a bank account* is understood, but it sounds unnatural.

### A joint account, a business account, a student account

If you want something specific, name the type: *Do you offer a student account?* *We'd like to open a joint account.* *I'd like to ask about a business account for a small shop.* These are all standard questions at a counter, and the staff expect them.

## Everyday transactions: withdraw, deposit, transfer

- *I'd like to withdraw 200 dollars.* (Not *take out money 200*; put the amount right after the verb.)
- *I'd like to deposit this check.* (*Cheque* in British English.)
- *Could I get small bills, please?* (US; *small notes* in the UK, Australia and NZ.)
- *How do I transfer money to another account?*
- *Could you tell me my balance?* (The amount in the account.)
- *Can I get a statement for the last three months?* (A list of everything that went in and out.)
- *How long will the transfer take?*
- *Is there a limit on how much I can send?*

Use **How do I...?** for instructions: *How do I transfer money?* The learner version *How I can send money other account?* is understandable but breaks the question order: in English questions the helper verb (*do*, *can*) goes before the subject. If that rule is new to you, our [guide to forming questions in English](/en/blog/how-to-form-questions-in-english/) explains it step by step.

### Sending money abroad

For international transfers, the vocabulary changes a little. You may need the other person's *account number*, *bank name*, and sometimes a *SWIFT code* or *IBAN*, depending on the country. Useful questions: *What is the exchange rate?* *Are there transfer fees?* *Is there a fee for receiving money, too?* *How long will it take to arrive?* Compare the total cost, not only the headline fee, because the exchange rate can include a margin.

## Asking about fees

Fees are the part people hesitate to ask about, but a polite direct question is normal and expected.

- *Are there any fees for this account?*
- *Is there a fee for using other banks' ATMs?*
- *What's the fee for an international transfer?*
- *Is there a charge if my balance goes below the minimum?*
- *Could you explain this fee on my statement?*
- *Is there an overdraft fee?* (An *overdraft* is when you spend more than you have, if your bank allows it.)

The phrase *This is free or not?* sounds rude, though the grammar is understandable. *Are there any fees for this?* is neutral and polite. If an answer is not clear, you can say: *Could you put that in writing?* or *Could you send me the fee schedule?*

## When something goes wrong

Most stress at the bank comes from problems: a card that stops working, a payment you did not make, or a lost wallet. A calm, factual structure helps staff to act quickly.

{{diagram: d2 | State facts first, then ask for the action you want, then ask what happens next.}}

**Card declined or not working**

- *My card was declined yesterday. Can you check what happened?*
- *My card isn't working at the ATM.*
- *Could you unblock my card?*

**Lost or stolen card**

- *I think I lost my card. Can you cancel it?*
- *My wallet was stolen. I need to block my card right now.*
- *Can I get a replacement card? How long will it take?*

**A charge you do not recognise**

- *There's a charge on my account that I don't recognise.*
- *I didn't make this payment. It was on 3 March for 45 dollars.*
- *Can you investigate it, please?*
- *Do I need to fill in a form?* / *Will I get a reference number?*

Notice the order. *Somebody stole money from my account!* may be how you feel, but *There's a charge on my account that I don't recognise* gives the bank something it can check. If your card is lost, tell your bank at once; most banks also provide a phone number on their website or app for urgent cases. If you are not sure whether to call the bank or the police, ask: *Should I report this to the police as well?*

## Understanding what the teller says

- *Could you enter your PIN, please?* (Your secret number for the card.)
- *Would you like a receipt?*
- *Is that everything for today?*
- *Could you confirm your address and date of birth?*
- *For security, I need to ask a few questions.*
- *You'll receive a confirmation by text or email.*

Banks ask security questions to protect you. If you are not sure about a request, it is fine to say: *Could you explain why you need that?* A real bank will never ask you to share your full PIN or password over the phone or in a message; if someone does, hang up and call the bank's official number. This is a safety point, not a language point, but newcomers are common targets for scam calls, so it is worth knowing.

## Common mistakes in bank English

1. **"I want to make a bank account."** Use *I'd like to open an account.*
2. **"My card is not accept."** Use *My card was declined* or *wasn't accepted.*
3. **"How many money?"** Money is uncountable: *How much money?*
4. **"I take out money 200."** Use *I'd like to withdraw 200 dollars.*
5. **Confusing "borrow" and "lend".** The bank *lends* you money; you *borrow* from the bank. Our [guide to commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/) explains pairs like this.
6. **Being too blunt.** Start requests with *Could I...?* or *I'd like to...*, as in our [guide to polite email openings](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/), which also helps with written messages to your bank.

## Self-check

1. Politely ask whether an account has a monthly fee.
2. Fix: *I want to take out money 200.*
3. What is the difference between a *debit card* and a *credit card*?
4. Put in order: *transfer / How / money / do / I / ?*
5. Which sounds better to report a charge: *Somebody stole my money!* or *There is a charge I don't recognise.*?
6. What does *balance* mean on a bank statement?

### Answers

1. **Is there a monthly fee for this account?**
2. **I'd like to withdraw 200 dollars.**
3. A **debit** card spends money from your account at once; a **credit** card borrows money from the bank up to a limit, to be paid back later.
4. **How do I transfer money?**
5. **There is a charge I don't recognise.** It states a fact the bank can check.
6. The **amount of money** in the account at a given moment.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I need to open a bank account as a newcomer?

It depends on the bank and country, but photo ID and proof of address are common. Ask: *What do I need to bring to open an account as a new resident?*

### How do I ask about hidden fees?

Ask directly: *Are there any fees for this account?* and *Could you send me the list of fees?* Politely asking for the fee schedule in writing is normal.

### What should I say if my card is declined?

*My card was declined. Can you check what happened?* Then ask whether there is a block, a limit or a security check on the account.

### How do I report a payment I did not make?

State the facts first (*There's a charge I don't recognise, on 3 March for 45 dollars*), then ask the bank to investigate and ask what the next step is.
