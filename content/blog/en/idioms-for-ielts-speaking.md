The examiner asks, *"Do you like rainy days?"* and the candidate replies, *"Well, when it's raining cats and dogs I'm over the moon, because staying in is a piece of cake."* Three idioms in one sentence, and all three make the answer sound memorised. Used like this, **idioms for IELTS Speaking** work against you. Used sparingly, accurately and in the right place, they can show the kind of flexible, natural vocabulary that higher bands reward. This guide explains where that line is, which expressions are worth learning, which ones to drop, and how to make a handful of idioms genuinely your own.

## Do idioms for IELTS Speaking raise your band score?

Speaking is assessed on four criteria: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Idioms belong to **Lexical Resource**. The public band descriptors mention idiomatic vocabulary at the upper bands, and the qualities they stress are *natural* and *accurate* use. Nothing in them rewards quantity.

In practice that means three things:

- **One well-placed idiom**, said smoothly and with the right meaning, is evidence of a wide, flexible vocabulary.
- **A forced, outdated or slightly wrong idiom** draws attention to itself. It can make an otherwise good answer sound rehearsed.
- **No idioms at all** is not a disaster. Precise word choice, good collocations and the ability to paraphrase carry far more weight.

Think of idioms as seasoning rather than the meal. If your core vocabulary is thin, extra idioms will not hide it. In fact, forcing memorized idioms is one reason [why memorizing IELTS speaking sample answers](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/) routinely backfires. Build the base first with a system like the one in [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/), then add idiomatic language on top.

### Idioms, phrasal verbs and collocations are not the same thing

Learners often lump these together, but the difference matters for how you study them:

| Type | Example | Can you guess the meaning from the words? |
|---|---|---|
| Collocation | *make a decision*, *heavy traffic* | Yes – the words just habitually go together |
| Phrasal verb | *put off*, *come up with* | Sometimes |
| Idiom | *a far cry from*, *call it a day* | Rarely – the meaning is figurative |

Collocations are the safest way to sound natural and appear in almost every answer; our guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) covers the most useful ones. Idioms are the riskiest, because a single wrong word breaks the meaning.

## Idioms that still sound natural in the exam

The expressions below are in everyday use among fluent speakers today, are neither slang nor old-fashioned, and fit the topics that come up again and again in the test. For a longer list with IPA, definitions and examples, see our English [idioms for Speaking vocabulary set](/en/vocabulary/idioms), but apply the same filter before you learn any of them.

| Idiom | Meaning | Topics where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| *a breath of fresh air* | something new and pleasantly different | people, places, jobs |
| *hit the books* | start studying hard | education, exams |
| *go the extra mile* | make more effort than is expected | work, customer service, friends |
| *on the go* | busy and constantly moving | lifestyle, technology, food |
| *a far cry from* | very different from | past vs present, comparisons |
| *once in a blue moon* | very rarely | habits, free time |
| *call it a day* | stop working for now | work, study routines |
| *get the hang of* | gradually learn how to do something | new skills, hobbies |
| *a steep learning curve* | a lot to learn in a short time | jobs, moving abroad, technology |
| *in the long run* | over a long period of time | Part 3 opinions |
| *the tip of the iceberg* | a small visible part of a bigger problem | Part 3 social issues |

A few of these, such as *in the long run* and *get the hang of*, sit closer to fixed phrases than to colourful pictures. That is exactly why they are so useful: they sound natural in almost any register and are hard to get wrong.

## Idioms to avoid or handle with care

- **Textbook clichés.** *It's raining cats and dogs*, *a piece of cake* and *over the moon* are not wrong, but examiners hear them from rehearsed candidates every day. They no longer signal range; they signal a list.
- **Old-fashioned or literary expressions.** If you have only ever seen an idiom in a "100 idioms" handout and never heard anyone actually say it, leave it out.
- **Rude or very informal language.** The Speaking test is relaxed, but it is still a conversation with a professional stranger. Slang and crude expressions are a poor fit.
- **Idioms you are not sure about.** Getting an idiom wrong is worse than not using one. *Break a leg* wishes someone good luck before a performance; it has nothing to do with injuries.
- **Literal translations from your first language.** Many languages have their own version of "it's raining heavily" or "when pigs fly", and translating them word for word rarely produces an English idiom. If a phrase feels natural only because it exists in your language, check it in a dictionary first.

## How to use idioms in Part 1, Part 2 and Part 3

A well-used idiom usually has three features: it answers the question directly, it is followed by an explanation or example, and it appears once rather than in a cluster. Here is how that looks in each part of the test (all answers are original examples).

### Part 1: short and personal

**Question:** *Do you often cook at home?*

> *To be honest, only once in a blue moon. During the week I'm always on the go, so I usually pick up something quick near the office. At weekends, though, I try to make a proper meal with my flatmate.*

There are two idioms here, but each one carries part of the answer and is immediately explained. Remove them and the answer still makes sense, which is a good test of whether they are decoration or content.

### Part 2: inside a story

**Cue card:** *Describe a skill you learned recently.*

> *…For the first week I spent more time in the water than on the board. But after about a fortnight I finally got the hang of it, and now surfing is the highlight of my week. It's a far cry from my office job – no screens, no emails, just the sea and the wind…*

In a long turn, idioms should sit naturally inside the narrative rather than being announced. For planning the story itself, see our [IELTS Speaking Part 2 tips](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-tips/), which explain how to use the one-minute preparation time.

### Part 3: supporting an argument

**Question:** *Will online learning replace traditional schools?*

> *I doubt it will replace them entirely. Online courses are flexible, but in the long run children need face-to-face contact to build social skills. And the problems teachers are reporting now, such as short attention spans, may be just the tip of the iceberg.*

In Part 3 an idiom can never replace a reason or an example. Notice that both expressions sit inside a clear line of argument. If you need more ways to express the same idea without repeating yourself, the techniques in [how to paraphrase in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) apply to speaking as well as writing.

## A four-step routine to make an idiom your own

Knowing what an idiom means is not the same as being able to produce it under pressure. Take each new expression through four steps:

![Lexkite pronunciation practice with scoring: the phrase 'cut down on' and a microphone button for speaking it aloud, marked from your voice](media:practice-speak "Saying a fixed phrase aloud and getting it scored trains you to deliver it as one smooth unit")

1. **Hear it in real context.** Find it in a conversation, a podcast or a model answer, such as those in the English-language [IELTS Speaking question bank](/en/speaking/). Notice who says it, to whom and in what mood.
2. **Write two sentences about your own life.** With *go the extra mile*: *My manager always goes the extra mile for new staff* and *I went the extra mile to prepare for my sister's wedding.* Personal sentences are far easier to recall than dictionary examples.
3. **Say it aloud until it flows.** Idioms are spoken as one unit, with the stress on the key content words: *go the EXtra MILE*. A hesitant idiom sounds worse than a simple sentence said fluently. If your rhythm feels choppy, our guide to [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/) shows how words link together.
4. **Attach it to an exam topic.** Decide in advance that *a steep learning curve* belongs to "work" and "moving abroad". When the topic comes up, the phrase is already waiting.

Two or three idioms a week, each taken through all four steps, is plenty. Ten expressions you can use with confidence are worth more than a hundred you only recognise on paper. Repeating model answers aloud straight after hearing them, a method known as [shadowing](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/), is a good way to absorb the rhythm of phrases like these.

## Common mistakes learners make with idioms

- **Changing one word.** *Once in a blue sky* or *a far cry of* – idioms are fixed, and swapping a word usually destroys the meaning or sounds comic.
- **Wrong tense or form.** *I have got the hang of it yesterday* mixes the present perfect with a finished time. Say *I got the hang of it yesterday*. If that rule still trips you up, review [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/).
- **Using an idiom instead of an answer.** *"Do you like your job?" – "It's a piece of cake."* and then silence. The idiom should open or support an answer, not replace it.
- **Stacking idioms.** Three in one sentence, as in the opening example, makes the answer sound like a recited list.
- **Wrong register.** Most idioms are informal, so they suit Speaking but not an academic essay. The same is true in email: compare the tone advice in our guide to [writing a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/).

## Quick self-check

Choose the better option and decide why.

1. (a) *I'm over the moon about my new job, it's a piece of cake and the people are a breath of fresh air.* (b) *My new job is a breath of fresh air – my manager actually listens to ideas.*
2. (a) *Once in a blue moon I go to the cinema.* (b) *Once in a blue sky I go to the cinema.*
3. Which idiom fits? *Learning to code at work was hard at first – it was ______.* (a) a steep learning curve (b) the tip of the iceberg
4. (a) *I hit the books every night last month because my finals were coming.* (b) *I hit the books, it was good.*
5. Is *in the long run* acceptable in a Part 3 answer about city planning?

**Answers:**

1. (b) – one idiom, used accurately and followed by a specific reason. (a) stacks three and sounds rehearsed.
2. (a) – the fixed form is *once in a blue moon*; changing one word breaks it.
3. (a) – *a steep learning curve* means a lot to learn quickly. *The tip of the iceberg* refers to a small visible part of a larger problem.
4. (a) – the idiom is supported by a time reference and a reason; (b) gives the examiner nothing to assess.
5. Yes – it is neutral enough for any part of the test and naturally introduces a long-term consequence.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many idioms should I use in the Speaking test?
There is no required number. One idiom in a [Part 1 answer](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-tips/), or a few spread across the whole test, is plenty. Accuracy, the right context and smooth delivery matter far more than frequency.

### Can I use idioms in IELTS Writing?
Most idioms are informal, so they rarely suit a Task 2 essay or an Academic Task 1 report. An informal letter in General Training Task 1 can take a light idiom, and neutral phrases such as *in the long run* work almost anywhere. In general, keep idioms for Speaking.

### Where can I learn idioms that are not outdated?
From real, recent English: podcasts, interviews, TV series, reality shows and current news articles. If you hear an expression several times from different speakers, it is safe to use. If you have only seen it in a list, be cautious.

### Can I get band 7 in Speaking without using idioms?
Yes, it is possible. Your band depends on all four criteria, and Lexical Resource also looks at precision, flexibility, collocation and paraphrase. Accurate, varied vocabulary delivered fluently matters more than inserting idioms.
