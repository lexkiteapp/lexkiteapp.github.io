**TOEFL Complete the Words** is the first task in the Reading section of the TOEFL iBT format used since 21 January 2026. You see a short academic paragraph in which some words are cut in half: the first part of the word is shown, and you type the missing letters. It looks like a spelling game, but it actually tests three things at once: grammar in context, active vocabulary and accurate spelling. Because it is new, many prep books ignore it, and many test takers lose easy points.

This guide explains how the task works, the five clues that solve most gaps, the traps that catch strong students, and a practice paragraph with answers.

## How TOEFL Complete the Words works

The task uses a format that language testers call a **C-test**. A short paragraph on an academic topic (biology, history, economics, art) is shown with gaps:

- The paragraph begins with a complete sentence, so you can understand the topic.
- After that, a number of words lose their second half. The remaining letters are shown, followed by empty slots.
- You type the letters that complete each word. Only a fully correct word counts.

Here is a short example of the style:

*Many desert plants survive long dry periods. Their roo___ can reach deep under___ water, and th___ leaves are of___ small to red___ water loss.*

Answers: *roots, underground, their, often, reduce*. Notice that most of the missing words are common: a pronoun (*their*), an adverb (*often*), a verb (*reduce*). The challenge is not rare vocabulary; it is noticing grammar and spelling under time pressure.

## Five clues that solve most gaps

{{diagram: d1 | Work through the clues in order: grammar first, then letters, then meaning}}

**1. Position tells you the part of speech.** After *the* or *a* and before a noun, you usually need an adjective (*a sign___ change* → *significant*). After a subject and before an object, you need a verb. Between two clauses, you need a linking word or relative pronoun (*…the river, wh___ flows north* → *which*).

**2. The letters given narrow the options.** *Bec___* in the middle of a sentence followed by a clause is almost certainly *because*. *Thr___* before a noun could be *three* or *through*; the next clue decides.

**3. The number of missing letters matters.** Each slot is one letter. If *th__* has two slots, the answer is a four-letter word: *that, this, them, then, they, thus*. Combine this with grammar to choose.

**4. Agreement locks the form.** Check singular or plural (*these/this*), tense (*has incre___* → *increased*), and articles (*an* before a vowel sound). Learners whose first language doesn't mark plurals or verb endings (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Japanese, Korean) lose many points here, so slow down on every noun and verb ending.

**5. Meaning confirms the choice.** Read the sentence with your answer in place. If *the population has decreased* contradicts the next sentence about rapid growth, change it.

## Spelling traps that cost points

Because you must produce every letter, spelling errors that would not matter in a multiple-choice test now cost you the item. The most frequent problems:

- **Double letters:** *occurred, beginning, committee, necessary, accommodate.*
- **Silent or unstressed letters:** *environment, government, February, separate, definitely.*
- **-ible vs -able, -ance vs -ence:** *responsible, available, significance, difference.*
- **ie vs ei:** *receive, believe, science, their.*
- **Past forms:** *studied* (not *studyed*), *planned*, *preferred*, *referred*.
- **American vs British spelling:** TOEFL is an American test, so *color, center, analyze* are the forms you will see. If you learned British spelling, practise noticing the difference.

Keep a personal list of words you misspell. Ten minutes a day of typing them from memory (not just reading them) fixes most errors within a few weeks; [typing recall vs flashcards](/en/blog/typing-recall-vs-flashcards/) explains why producing a word helps memory more than recognising it.

## Practice paragraph

Complete the words. Read the whole paragraph first.

*Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor. How___, they sup___ about a quarter of all mar___ species. Re___ are built over thou___ of years by tiny ani___ called polyps, wh___ produce a hard skeleton. In rec___ decades, rising sea temper___ have caused wides___ "bleaching", a process in wh___ corals lose the al___ that give them col___ and food.*

### Answers

1. **However**: contrast with the first sentence (small area vs many species).
2. **support**: verb after the subject *they*.
3. **marine**: adjective before *species*.
4. **Reefs**: plural subject, matching *are built*.
5. **thousands**: *thousands of years*, a fixed pattern.
6. **animals**: plural noun after *tiny*.
7. **which**: relative pronoun after a comma, referring to polyps (not people).
8. **recent**: adjective before *decades*.
9. **temperatures**: plural subject of *have caused*.
10. **widespread**: adjective before a noun in quotation marks.
11. **which**: *a process in which*.
12. **algae**: the organisms that live in coral (a tricky spelling).
13. **color**: American spelling, as used on the TOEFL.

If you missed 4, 6 or 9, your weak point is plural endings. If you missed 1 or 11, review linking words and relative clauses; our [relative clauses guide](/en/blog/relative-clauses-who-which-that/) covers *in which*.

## Timing and test-day strategy

The Reading section is short, so do not spend long on any single gap.

- **Read the first sentence carefully.** It is complete and tells you the topic, which narrows the vocabulary.
- **Do a first pass for the easy gaps.** Function words (*the, which, because, their*) often take seconds.
- **Then return to content words**, using the clues above.
- **Don't leave blanks.** A reasonable guess based on grammar has a chance; an empty slot has none.
- **Check endings last.** A final scan for *-s*, *-ed* and *-ly* catches the commonest errors.

## How to prepare for Complete the Words

1. **Read academic texts daily.** Short science and history articles train you to predict common academic words. The [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/) covers many of the content words that appear in these paragraphs.
2. **Make your own C-tests.** Take any paragraph, delete the second half of every second word after the first sentence, wait a day, then fill it in. This is free, unlimited practice.
3. **Practise collocations.** Many gaps are solved by knowing which words go together: *play a role*, *rely on*, *a wide range of*. [Learning vocabulary from reading](/en/blog/learn-vocabulary-from-reading/) shows how to collect them.
4. **Train spelling by typing.** Write, don't just read, every new word.
5. **Review grammar weak spots.** Articles, plurals and verb forms solve a large share of gaps; [articles a, an and the](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/) is a good place to start.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is TOEFL Complete the Words multiple choice?

No. You type the missing letters yourself. That is why spelling matters so much: a word with one wrong letter does not count.

### How many letters are missing in each word?

Usually about half of the word, and each empty slot stands for one letter. Count the slots; they tell you the length of the missing part and help you choose between similar words.

### Do I need British or American spelling?

The TOEFL uses American English, so expect forms such as *color* and *center*. If you normally use British spelling, practise the American forms of common words before the test.

### How can I improve quickly?

Combine daily academic reading with self-made C-tests and a spelling list of your own mistakes. Most learners see faster progress from fixing plural and verb endings than from learning new rare words.
