In most English-speaking workplaces, how you say something matters almost as much as what you say. A message that is correct but too direct can sound cold or even rude, and an overly long apology can sound unsure. The skill is in the middle, and the right **polite English phrases for work** are small, learnable chunks. This guide shows you how to make requests, say no, disagree, ask people to repeat themselves and deliver bad news, in speech and in email.

## Why English sounds "indirect"

English politeness often works through **softening**. Instead of saying *"Send me the file,"* people say *"Could you send me the file when you have a moment?"* The meaning is the same, but the second version gives the other person room to answer. Softening tools include:

- **Modal verbs:** *could, would, might, may*
- **Hedging words:** *a little, a bit, possibly, perhaps, I wonder if*
- **Past tense for distance:** *I was wondering if…*, *I wanted to ask…*
- **Questions instead of commands:** *Would it be possible to…?*
- **Thanks and context:** *Thanks for your help with this. When you have a moment…*

Do not overuse them. Too many in one sentence sounds nervous: *"Sorry, I just wondered if maybe you could possibly…"* One or two softeners is enough. Our guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) explains the same idea in a more formal context.

## Polite English phrases for work: requests

| Situation | Phrases |
|---|---|
| A simple request | *Could you…?* / *Would you mind…-ing?* / *Would it be possible to…?* |
| An urgent request | *I would be grateful if you could… by Thursday.* |
| Asking for help | *Do you have a minute? I could use your help with…* |
| Checking understanding | *Just to confirm, you would like me to…?* |
| Following up | *I am just following up on my email from Monday. Have you had a chance to look?* |

Notice *Would you mind sending…?* uses the **-ing** form, and a "yes" answer means *"yes, I mind."* The natural answer is *"No, not at all"* or *"Sure, no problem."* If you want a quick reference for these forms, see our guide to [modal verbs](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/).

## Saying no politely

{{diagram: d2 | A four-step shape for a polite refusal that still protects the relationship.}}

Examples:

- *"Thank you for thinking of me. I am afraid I cannot take this on this week because of the Monday deadline. Could we look at it again next week?"*
- *"I would love to help, but I am at full capacity right now. Would it help if I asked Sam?"*
- *"That is a great idea, but I do not think we can do it within the current budget."*

*I am afraid* does not mean you are scared. It is a polite way to give bad news.

## Disagreeing without starting a fight

Start with agreement or understanding, then add your view:

- *"I see your point, and I think there is another angle to consider."*
- *"That makes sense. I wonder if we could also look at…"*
- *"I am not sure I agree entirely. In my experience,…"*
- *"Could we double-check the numbers before we decide?"*

Avoid: *"You are wrong"*, *"That will not work"* or *"Obviously…"*. Focus on the idea, not the person. More examples of meeting language are in our [business meeting phrases guide](/en/blog/business-english-meeting-phrases-guide/).

## When you do not understand

Asking for repetition is normal and professional:

- *"Sorry, could you say that again?"*
- *"I did not quite catch that. Could you repeat the last part?"*
- *"Could you explain what you mean by…?"*
- *"Just to make sure I understand, you are saying that…?"*

Use the last pattern often. It shows you were listening and fixes misunderstandings early.

## Chat messages and phone calls

Short channels such as Slack, Teams or text messages need shorter politeness. A bare *"Need the file"* feels abrupt, but a full formal email is too heavy. Try:

- *"Hi Sam, quick question: could you share the latest file?"*
- *"Thanks, that is really helpful."*
- *"No rush, but could you look at this before Friday?"*

On the phone, start with who you are and why you are calling: *"Hello, this is Linh from Finance. I am calling about the invoice."* If you need time, say *"Could you hold on for a moment?"* and not just *"Wait."* Close with *"Thanks for your help. Have a good day."* Our [business phone calls guide](/en/blog/business-phone-calls-in-english-guide/) has full call scripts.

## Polite emails

Emails need a clear subject, a friendly opening, one main request and a polite close.

> **Subject:** Quick question about the Friday report
>
> Hi Maria,
>
> Thank you for sending the draft. Could you let me know whether the figures on page 3 include the March sales? If they do not, I will add them before Friday.
>
> Thanks in advance for your help.
>
> Best regards,
> Linh

Other useful lines: *"I hope you are well."*, *"Please let me know if you have any questions."*, *"I look forward to hearing from you."* For longer messages see our guides to [writing a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/) and [workplace abbreviations](/en/blog/workplace-english-abbreviations-email-acronyms-guide/).

## Delivering bad news

Be clear first, then kind:

- *"Unfortunately, we will not be able to deliver on Friday."*
- *"I am sorry to say that the budget has been cut."*
- *"I wanted to let you know as soon as possible that…"*

Then give the next step: *"Here is what we can do instead."* For handling tense conversations, see our guide to [workplace conflict resolution](/en/blog/workplace-conflict-resolution-in-english-guide/) and [how to give feedback in English](/en/blog/how-to-give-feedback-in-english-at-work/).

## Cultural notes

Norms vary by country and company. In the UK, indirect language is often very strong, so *"That is quite interesting"* may mean *"I disagree."* In the US and Australia, people can be more direct but still use friendly softeners. When unsure, watch how colleagues write to each other and copy the level of formality. Being kind and clear is better than being formal and vague.

### Answers

1. *Could you give me the report when you have a moment?*
2. *I am afraid I will not be able to attend the meeting. Could someone share the notes?*
3. *I think there may be a small mistake here. Could we check it together?*
4. *Sorry, could you say that again?*
5. **opening** (*Would you mind opening…?*).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is "please" enough to be polite?

Not always. *"Send me the file, please"* is still a command. Add a modal and a softener: *"Could you send me the file, please?"*

### Can I say "no" to my manager?

Yes, if you do it respectfully: give a short reason and offer an alternative or ask what to prioritise.

### Do I need to apologise a lot?

No. Use *sorry* for real mistakes and *excuse me* or *thank you for your patience* for small things. Too many apologies reduce your authority.

### How do I know how formal to be?

Match the other person. If they write "Hi Sam", you can too. If they write "Dear Dr Lee", stay formal until they change.
