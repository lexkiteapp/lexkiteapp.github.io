A good **IELTS Writing Task 2 sample answer** is only useful if you can see why it works. This page gives an original essay for a discussion question, then goes through it paragraph by paragraph against the four marking criteria, shows what would lower it, and ends with a checklist you can use on your own writing. The essay was written for this guide to show the features of a strong band 7 response; it has not been scored by an examiner, and you should not memorise it.

## The question

*Some people believe that children should begin learning a foreign language at primary school. Others think it is better to wait until secondary school. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.*

This is a **discussion plus opinion** question. The task has three jobs: explain the first view, explain the second view, and state your own position. If one of these is missing, Task Response suffers. See [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/) for the five question types.

## The sample essay

> **Paragraph 1.** The age at which children should start learning a foreign language is a topic of lively debate. While some argue that primary school is the ideal time, others prefer to postpone lessons until pupils are older. In my view, early exposure is more beneficial, provided that lessons are playful rather than exam-driven.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Supporters of early learning point out that young children absorb sounds and patterns with remarkable ease. A seven-year-old who hears a second language in songs and games often develops a more natural accent and little fear of making mistakes, which are advantages that are harder to gain later. Learning through play also makes the language feel useful, since children connect new words with real activities. Moreover, an early start leaves more years for steady progress, so that by the end of secondary school students can use the language with confidence rather than merely pass tests in it.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** On the other hand, critics argue that primary classrooms should concentrate on literacy and numeracy. They worry that an additional language may confuse children who are still mastering their mother tongue, and that schools may lack teachers who are properly trained to work with young learners. Some parents add that homework in two languages may be too heavy for small children. These concerns are not unreasonable, since a poorly taught class can leave pupils bored and discouraged. However, they describe problems with how languages are taught, not with the age at which they are introduced.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** In conclusion, although a later start can work when resources are limited, I believe that primary-school language lessons offer greater long-term benefits, as long as schools invest in skilled teachers and engaging methods.

This is roughly 270 words (it is a model written for this guide and has not been marked by an examiner), within the comfortable range for Task 2, which requires at least 250.

## Reading the IELTS Writing Task 2 sample answer paragraph by paragraph

### Introduction: paraphrase plus position

The first sentence rephrases the question without copying it: *begin learning* becomes *start learning*, and the question's two views are summarised as *primary school* versus *postpone lessons until pupils are older*. The debate is described rather than repeated. The second sentence names both views, and the third gives a clear opinion with a condition. A reader knows the plan of the essay at once. For paraphrase practice, see [how to paraphrase in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/).

### Body 1: one idea, explained and exemplified

The topic sentence is the supporters' main claim: young children absorb sounds easily. The next sentence supplies an example (songs and games) and a consequence (natural accent, less fear). The paragraph then adds a second, related benefit: more years for progress. It stays on one theme and avoids a list of unrelated points.

### Body 2: the opposing view treated fairly

The critics' arguments are described with neutral verbs (*argue*, *worry*) and then answered in the last sentence. This is what Task Response rewards: a fair presentation, followed by the writer's judgement. The sentence *they describe problems with how languages are taught, not with the age* is the pivot that leads to the writer's own opinion.

### Conclusion: no new ideas

The conclusion repeats the position and the condition from the introduction, with one brief concession and no new argument. A conclusion of two sentences is enough.

## Why this essay is around band 7

Look for the following features, which together describe the level:

- A clear position from the first paragraph, kept to the end.
- Ideas that are explained and supported, not only stated.
- A range of sentence structures: *provided that*, *since*, *rather than*, relative clauses and a fronted adverbial.
- Linking that is varied and natural: *while*, *moreover*, *on the other hand*, *however*. See [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/).
- Accurate vocabulary with collocations such as *absorb patterns*, *natural accent*, *long-term benefits*.

For higher bands, the differences become subtle: more precise vocabulary, rarer but accurate structures and deeper development of ideas. If you want to understand how scores are worked out, read [IELTS band scores explained](/en/blog/ielts-band-scores-explained/).

## What would pull the same essay down

| Change | Likely effect |
|---|---|
| No opinion in the introduction | Task response weaker |
| Body paragraphs that list three weak ideas | Development too thin |
| Repeating "firstly, secondly, finally" | Cohesion sounds mechanical |
| Memorised phrases unrelated to the topic | Lexical resource unnatural |
| Frequent article and agreement mistakes | Grammar accuracy reduced |
| Fewer than 250 words | Task response penalised |

An essay stuffed with rare words can also score lower if the words are misused; the guide to [the sophistication trap in IELTS writing](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) shows why.

## A checklist for your own Task 2

1. Did I answer every part of the question?
2. Is my opinion clear in the introduction and conclusion?
3. Does each body paragraph have one main idea, an explanation and an example?
4. Are my sentences varied, with clauses used accurately? See [complex sentences for IELTS writing](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/).
5. Have I checked articles, plurals, tenses and spelling in the last few minutes?

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I memorise a sample answer for the exam?

No. Examiners notice memorised text, and it rarely fits the question you receive. Study the structure, the linking and the way ideas are developed, then write new sentences about each new topic.

### How long should a Task 2 essay be?

At least 250 words, so about 260 to 300 is a safe range. Longer essays are not scored higher on length alone, and they raise the risk of errors and lost time.

### Do I have to give my own opinion in a discussion question?

Yes, because the instruction asks for it. State your view clearly and make sure it matches what you say in the body paragraphs.

### Can I use "I" in IELTS Writing Task 2?

Yes. Phrases such as *in my view* are acceptable for an opinion, though you should not use them in every sentence.
