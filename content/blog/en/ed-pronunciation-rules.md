**Ed pronunciation** trips up learners at every level: *walked* is not "walk-ed", and *played* does not have two syllables. In fact, the regular past tense ending *-ed* has three possible sounds, /t/, /d/ and /ɪd/, and which one you use depends on the last *sound* of the verb, not its spelling. Learn the two-question rule below and you will pronounce thousands of regular verbs correctly without memorising them one by one.

## Why ed pronunciation matters

Mispronouncing *-ed* creates two kinds of problem. First, adding an extra syllable (*look-ed*, *stop-ed*) makes your speech sound slow and unnatural, and it can affect the rhythm of the whole sentence. Second, dropping the ending completely (*Yesterday I walk to work*) can make listeners think you made a grammar mistake, even when you know the rule. In IELTS Speaking, pronunciation is one of the four criteria, and clear endings also help the examiner hear your grammatical range.

The ending is also a listening problem: if you expect "walk-ed", you will not recognise the real /wɔːkt/ in fast speech. Our guide to [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/) explains how endings blend with the next word.

## The three rules of ed pronunciation

{{diagram: d1 | The last sound of the base verb decides the ending: /t/ or /d/ adds a syllable, voiceless takes /t/, voiced takes /d/}}

### Rule 1: /ɪd/ after /t/ and /d/

If the verb ends in a /t/ or /d/ sound, *-ed* adds a new syllable, pronounced /ɪd/ (some speakers use /əd/):

- *want* → *wanted* /ˈwɒntɪd/ (two syllables)
- *need* → *needed*
- *decide* → *decided*
- *start* → *started*
- *visit* → *visited*

This is the **only** case where *-ed* adds a syllable. It exists for a practical reason: saying /t/ or /d/ directly after another /t/ or /d/ would be almost impossible to hear.

### Rule 2: /t/ after voiceless sounds

A **voiceless** sound is made without vibration of the vocal cords. If the verb ends in a voiceless sound (/p/, /k/, /f/, /s/, /ʃ/, /tʃ/, /θ/), *-ed* sounds like /t/, with no extra syllable:

- *stop* → *stopped* /stɒpt/
- *look* → *looked* /lʊkt/
- *laugh* → *laughed* /lɑːft/ (gh = /f/)
- *miss* → *missed* /mɪst/
- *wash* → *washed* /wɒʃt/
- *watch* → *watched* /wɒtʃt/
- *fix* → *fixed* /fɪkst/ (x = /ks/)

### Rule 3: /d/ after voiced sounds

A **voiced** sound is made with vibration. All vowels are voiced, and so are /b/, /g/, /v/, /z/, /ʒ/, /dʒ/, /ð/, /m/, /n/, /ŋ/, /l/ and /r/. After these, *-ed* sounds like /d/, again with no extra syllable:

- *play* → *played* /pleɪd/
- *call* → *called* /kɔːld/
- *live* → *lived* /lɪvd/
- *use* → *used* /juːzd/
- *change* → *changed* /tʃeɪndʒd/
- *clean* → *cleaned* /kliːnd/

## The voicing test: feel it in your throat

Not sure whether a sound is voiced? Put two fingers lightly on your throat and make the sound continuously:

- Say a long *ssss*: no vibration → voiceless.
- Say a long *zzzz*: vibration → voiced.

Try the pair *ffff* / *vvvv* too. The mouth position is the same; only the vibration changes. That is exactly the difference between *laughed* (/t/) and *lived* (/d/).

## Sounds, not letters: the tricky cases

Because the rule depends on sound, spelling can mislead you:

- *laughed*, *coughed* → the letters are *gh*, but the sound is /f/ → **/t/**
- *fixed*, *relaxed*, *mixed* → *x* is /ks/, which ends in /s/ → **/t/**
- *danced*, *produced* → silent final *e*; the last sound is /s/ → **/t/**
- *loved*, *believed* → the last sound is /v/ → **/d/**
- *raised* /reɪzd/ versus *practised* /ˈpræktɪst/ → *s* can be /z/ or /s/

When unsure, look up the base verb's pronunciation in a dictionary, check its final sound, then apply the rule. A quick refresher on symbols helps; our [pronunciation library](/app/#/pron) covers every IPA sound with example words.

## Adjectives ending in -ed with /ɪd/

A small group of **adjectives** keep an older /ɪd/ pronunciation even though their last sound is not /t/ or /d/:

- *naked* /ˈneɪkɪd/, *wicked*, *crooked*, *rugged*, *ragged*, *wretched*
- *beloved* (*my beloved city*), *learned* as an adjective meaning "highly educated" (*a learned professor*), *aged* meaning "very old" (*an aged relative*), *blessed* (*a blessed relief*), *dogged* (*dogged determination*)

When these words are verbs, the normal rules apply: *She learned* /lɜːnd/ *quickly. He aged* /eɪdʒd/ *a lot that year.*

Adverbs ending in **-edly** also use /ɪd/: *supposedly*, *allegedly*, *markedly*, *deservedly*.

## The same logic for -s and -es

The *-s* ending (plurals, third person singular, possessives) follows a parallel three-way rule:

| Last sound | -s sounds like | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /s/ /z/ /ʃ/ /ʒ/ /tʃ/ /dʒ/ (hissing sounds) | /ɪz/ (extra syllable) | *buses, roses, washes, watches, changes* |
| other voiceless sounds | /s/ | *cats, stops, laughs, books* |
| other voiced sounds | /z/ | *dogs, plays, calls, lives* |

Learn the two endings together; the voicing test works for both.

## Endings in connected speech

In natural speech, a /t/ or /d/ ending often links into the next word or becomes very light:

- *I walked to work* → the /t/ of *walked* merges with the /t/ of *to*.
- *She used to live there* → *used to* is /juːst tə/, with /s/ and /t/, not /z/ and /d/.
- *I asked him* → /ɑːskt ɪm/ in casual speech.

This is why you can "know" a word but not hear it. Shadowing helps you train both the sound and the rhythm; see [the shadowing technique](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) for a step-by-step routine. For the bigger picture of rhythm, [English word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) shows which syllables carry the beat.

## Four common mistakes and how to fix them

**1. Adding a vowel to every ending.** Saying *stop-ed* or *call-ed* is the most frequent error. Fix: practise the base verb and the past form as a pair (*stop / stopped*, *call / called*) and check that both have the same number of syllables, unless the verb ends in /t/ or /d/.

**2. Dropping the ending completely.** *Yesterday I clean my room* often comes from not hearing the ending, rather than not knowing the grammar. Fix: exaggerate the final /t/ or /d/ slightly when practising, then relax it to a natural level once it feels automatic.

**3. Adding a vowel after the ending.** Some learners say *stopped-uh* or *watched-uh* because their first language does not end words in consonant clusters. Fix: hold the final consonant without releasing extra air, and practise clusters slowly: *-pt*, *-kt*, *-ft*, *-st*, *-tʃt*.

**4. Reading by spelling.** Treating *-ed* as letters leads to *laugh-ed* or *fix-ed*. Fix: always ask "what is the last sound?" and, if needed, check the dictionary pronunciation of the base verb.

A good weekly routine is to choose five regular verbs from something you read, sort them into the three groups, then say each one in a short sentence about your own week.

### Answers

1. **/t/** – *hope* ends in voiceless /p/.
2. **/ɪd/** – *end* ends in /d/.
3. **/d/** – *rain* ends in voiced /n/.
4. **/t/** – *kiss* ends in voiceless /s/.
5. **/ɪd/** – *wait* ends in /t/.
6. **/d/** – *rob* ends in voiced /b/.
7. **/t/** – *reach* ends in voiceless /tʃ/.
8. **/d/** – *allow* ends in a vowel sound.
9. **/t/** – *cough* ends in /f/ (spelled *gh*).
10. **/ɪd/** – one of the special *-ed* adjectives.

Nine or ten correct means the rule is clear in your head. The next step is making it automatic in speech: read a short story in the past tense aloud and record yourself, listening only for the endings.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why isn't "walked" pronounced with two syllables?

Because *walk* ends in /k/, a voiceless sound, so *-ed* is just a /t/: /wɔːkt/. Only verbs ending in a /t/ or /d/ sound get the extra /ɪd/ syllable.

### Is it /ɪd/ or /əd/?

Both are standard. Many British speakers use /ɪd/, and many American speakers use something closer to /əd/. Either is perfectly clear.

### Do irregular verbs follow these rules?

No. Irregular past forms (*went*, *took*, *wrote*) have their own pronunciation. The three rules apply to regular verbs that add *-ed*, and to regular past participles.

### Does ed pronunciation matter for IELTS?

Pronunciation is one of the four Speaking criteria, and missing or extra syllables affect clarity and rhythm. Clear endings also help the examiner recognise your use of past tenses and the passive, which supports your grammar score.
