In **IELTS Speaking Part 2** you get a cue card, one minute to prepare and then speak for one to two minutes on your own. The candidates who do well are rarely the ones with the most impressive vocabulary; they are the ones who use the minute to plan a *story* rather than a list, and who know three or four ways to keep talking naturally when the first ideas run out. This guide gives you a note-taking method for the preparation minute, a four-step frame for any topic, an original sample answer and practical ways to reach the two-minute mark without padding.

## How IELTS Speaking Part 2 works

The whole Speaking test lasts 11–14 minutes and has three parts. In Part 2 the examiner hands you a card with a topic and several prompts, plus paper and a pencil for notes. You have **one minute to prepare** and should then speak for **one to two minutes**. The examiner will stop you when the time is up and may ask one or two short questions to round off before moving to Part 3. The discussion that follows has its own [IELTS Speaking Part 3 guide](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-tips/).

Your performance is assessed on the same four criteria as the rest of the test: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Part 2 is the only section where you speak at length without interruption, so it is your best chance to show fluency and the ability to organise a longer turn.

A typical card looks like this (original example):

> Describe a skill you learned outside school. You should say: what the skill is, who taught you, how long it took to learn – and explain why this skill is useful to you.

## Use the one-minute preparation properly

Most candidates make one of two mistakes with the minute: they write full sentences and finish only half, or they panic and write nothing. Aim for **12–15 keywords** arranged so you can see your whole talk at a glance.

A simple layout:

- **Top line:** the one thing you will talk about. *Bike repair – uncle's workshop.* Decide within ten seconds and do not change your mind.
- **Under each prompt:** two or three words. *Who → uncle Rafi, Saturdays. How long → 3 months, first flat tyre.*
- **A detail box:** one sensory detail and one feeling. *Smell of oil; proud – fixed neighbour's bike.*
- **An ending note:** why it matters now. *Save money; patience.*

Keywords work better than sentences because you will speak more naturally when you build sentences in the moment. Reading out a written sentence usually sounds flat and slows your fluency. This is also [why memorizing IELTS speaking sample answers](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/) hurts candidates: examiners immediately spot unspontaneous delivery. The same skill of spotting what a prompt really asks for also helps in reading questions such as [True, False or Not Given](/en/blog/ielts-reading-true-false-not-given/).

## A four-step frame for any cue card

Prompts on the card are helpful, but answering them one by one in a rigid order often sounds like a checklist. A story frame keeps the talk connected:

1. **Set the scene (15–20 seconds).** Say what you are going to talk about and give the context: when, where, who.
2. **Tell what happened (30–40 seconds).** Describe the events in order, using past tenses and sequence words such as *at first*, *after a few weeks*, *eventually*.
3. **Zoom in on a detail (25–35 seconds).** Pick one moment and describe it closely: what you saw, heard or felt, what someone said.
4. **Reflect (20–30 seconds).** Explain why it matters, how you feel about it now, or what you would do differently.

The prompts on the card naturally fit into these steps, so you still cover everything, but the result sounds like a person talking rather than a form being filled in. You can also apply the Domino Method explained in [how to extend answers in IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/the-domino-effect-in-ielts-speaking/) to keep your momentum going across transitions. Stories about the past rely heavily on accurate tenses; if you mix up *I learned* and *I have learned*, our [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) guide sorts out the difference.

### Sample answer (original, about 1 minute 50 seconds)

*I'd like to talk about how I learned to repair bicycles, which is something I picked up from my uncle rather than at school. He has a small workshop near our home, and when I was about fifteen I started spending my Saturday mornings there, mainly because I was bored.*

*At first he only let me watch, which was a bit frustrating. After a couple of weeks he gave me simple jobs, like pumping tyres and cleaning chains, and gradually he trusted me with more complicated things, such as adjusting brakes. I'd say it took me about three months before I could do a basic repair on my own.*

*The moment I remember most clearly is the first time I fixed a puncture by myself. A neighbour came in with a completely flat tyre, my uncle was busy, and he just pointed at me. My hands were covered in oil and I was quite nervous, but when the tyre held its air, I felt incredibly proud – probably prouder than after any exam.*

*Looking back, the skill is useful in a practical sense, because I've never paid anyone to fix my bike. But I think the bigger lesson was patience. Repairs rarely work the first time, and that workshop taught me to slow down and check things step by step, which helps me with studying as well.*

Notice the features: a clear opening line, sequence words, one vivid moment, a reflective ending, natural phrases such as *I'd say* and *looking back*, and a mix of past simple, present perfect and *would*. It contains no showy idioms at all; if you want to add one, our guide to [idioms for IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/idioms-for-ielts-speaking/) explains how to place it inside a story.

## How to extend your talk naturally to two minutes

If you regularly stop after 50 or 60 seconds, the problem is usually a lack of development, not a lack of English. These techniques add real content rather than filler:

- **Add a sense.** What could you see, hear or smell? *The whole place smelled of oil and rubber.*
- **Contrast then and now.** *At the time I found it boring; now I actually miss it.*
- **Report a short exchange.** *He said, "Don't force it – find out why it's stuck."* Direct speech brings a story to life.
- **Give a reason, then a result.** *Because I'd watched him so often, I knew which tool to reach for, so the repair took only ten minutes.*
- **Add a comparison.** *It was much harder than learning to ride, because…*
- **Mention an alternative.** *If I hadn't gone to the workshop, I'd probably have spent those mornings on my phone.*

Natural thinking phrases (*Let me think…*, *What I mean is…*, *The thing is…*) are fine in moderation. Long silences and repeated *um* are not. If you lose a word, describe it: *the little tool you use to lift the tyre off* is better than stopping.

## Prepare a few flexible stories, not dozens of answers

Cue cards fall into a handful of families: a **person**, a **place**, an **object**, an **event or experience**, and an **activity or habit**. Instead of preparing a separate answer for every card you find online, prepare five or six real stories in depth and practise bending them to fit. The bicycle workshop above can answer far more than one card:

| Cue card | How the same story fits |
|---|---|
| Describe a skill you learned outside school | Bike repair, as in the sample |
| Describe a person who taught you something | Focus on the uncle: his patience, what he said |
| Describe a place where you like to spend time | The workshop: smell of oil, tools on the wall |
| Describe a time you helped someone | The neighbour's flat tyre |
| Describe a time you felt proud | Fixing the first puncture alone |

This is not memorising a script: the facts stay the same, but the focus, the details and the reflection change with each card, so you are still speaking spontaneously. It also removes the biggest time-waster in the preparation minute, which is searching for something to talk about.

## Mistakes that keep Part 2 scores down

- **Memorised answers.** Examiners are trained to notice rehearsed speeches, especially when the content only loosely fits the card. Prepare *stories* you can adapt, not scripts.
- **Answering the prompts as a list.** "Who taught me? My uncle. How long? Three months." This sounds mechanical and limits coherence.
- **Speaking too fast.** Nerves make many candidates rush. A steady pace with clear word stress is easier to follow; our guide to [English word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) shows why stress matters for intelligibility.
- **Flat delivery.** Linking words together smoothly sounds more natural than pronouncing each word separately; see [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/).
- **Changing topic mid-way.** Commit to your first choice. A modest story told well beats an exciting one abandoned halfway.
- **Ignoring the "explain" prompt.** The final prompt usually asks for reflection, and that is where you can show more complex language.

## Quick self-check

Which preparation note is more useful, and why?

1. (a) "My uncle taught me to repair bicycles when I was fifteen years old." (b) "Uncle – workshop – 15 – Saturdays"
2. Your talk ends at 1 minute 10 seconds. Which addition is better? (a) "So, yes, that's all, it was good." (b) "What surprised me most was how calm he stayed when I made mistakes."
3. The card asks you to describe a place. Which opening is stronger? (a) "Place. Beach. It is near my city." (b) "I'm going to describe a quiet beach about an hour from my home town, which I first visited with my cousins."

**Answers:** 1. (b) – keywords are faster to write and easier to glance at; full sentences waste the minute. 2. (b) – it adds a new, specific detail and reflection; (a) adds nothing. 3. (b) – it introduces the topic in a full, connected sentence and sets the scene.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if I have no real experience that fits the card?
You can adapt a real experience or describe something plausible. The test assesses your English, not the truth of your story. Choose something you can describe in detail, because invented stories with no details are hard to extend.

### Do I have to speak for the full two minutes?
You should aim to keep talking until the examiner stops you. Speaking for only a short time gives less evidence of your fluency and range, and the examiner cannot reward language you do not produce.

### Must I follow the prompts in the order they appear?
No. The prompts are there to help you. Covering them in a natural story order is fine, as long as you address the main topic and the final "explain" point.

### What should I do if I forget a word in the middle?
Keep going. Paraphrase it, use a simpler word or describe what it does. Smooth recovery is part of fluency, and a short paraphrase sounds far better than a long pause.
