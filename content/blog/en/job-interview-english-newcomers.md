Many newcomers arrive with strong experience and still feel stuck in the first **job interview**. The problem is rarely skill. It is usually a mix of unfamiliar customs, fast questions and the pressure of speaking a second language. This guide covers job interview English for newcomers: what interviewers in countries such as Canada, the UK, Australia and the US usually expect, the phrases that sound natural, and how to handle the awkward questions about gaps, accents and local experience.

## Job interview English for newcomers: what interviewers expect

In English-speaking workplaces, interviewers usually want to hear **specific examples told clearly**, not a list of duties. They also expect you to speak confidently about your own contribution. In some cultures, saying "I achieved" can feel boastful, and "we did" feels safer. In an English interview, use *I* for what you personally did and *we* for the team result: *"I rebuilt the reporting process, and we cut errors by about a third."* Only quote a number that is true.

Other common expectations:

- **Small talk first.** The first two minutes are often about the weather, the journey or the office. It is not a test of your English; it is a way to relax. A short, friendly answer is enough: *"The journey was easy, thank you. I took the train."*
- **Eye contact and a firm tone, not a long speech.** Answers of 60 to 90 seconds are normal.
- **Questions about "fit".** Interviewers ask how you work with others, how you handle pressure and what you want to learn.
- **Politeness is direct.** It is fine to ask someone to repeat a question. It is not fine to guess silently.

## Introduce yourself: the "present, past, future" answer

"Tell me about yourself" is the question almost every interviewer asks. Use three short parts:

1. **Present:** your current or most recent role and strengths.
2. **Past:** one or two relevant achievements.
3. **Future:** why this job is the next step.

> *I am an accountant with six years of experience in retail and logistics. At my last company I took over the month-end close and shortened it from eight days to five. I recently moved to Toronto and I am looking for a role where I can use my reporting skills and grow into financial analysis, which is why this position interested me.*

Note the pattern. It is only five sentences, it names a real result and it ends with a link to the job. ## Phrases that sound natural

Learn these as full chunks, not single words.

| Situation | Useful phrases |
|---|---|
| Starting an answer | *"That is a good question."* / *"Let me give you an example."* |
| Buying time | *"Let me think about that for a moment."* |
| Asking for repetition | *"Sorry, could you repeat that?"* / *"Do you mean in my current role or in general?"* |
| Describing responsibility | *"I was responsible for…"* / *"I led…"* / *"I worked closely with…"* |
| Showing results | *"As a result,…"* / *"This reduced…"* / *"The team was able to…"* |
| Being honest about a gap | *"I have not used that tool yet, but I have used something similar."* |
| Closing | *"Thank you for your time. I am very interested in the role."* |

Use the simple past for finished achievements (*I managed, I improved*) and the present perfect for experience that connects to now (*I have worked with…*). If this is shaky, review our [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) guide first.

## Handling the difficult questions

### "I do not have local experience"

Employers sometimes use "Canadian experience" or "UK experience" as shorthand for "will you understand how we work here?" Answer that real worry directly: *"My experience is from another market, but the skills transfer: I managed customers, budgets and deadlines. I have also completed a local certificate and I am learning how things work here."* Do not apologise for where you worked.

### The gap on your resume

Keep it short, true and forward-looking: *"I took a year to move my family and settle, and I studied English during that time. I am now ready to work full time."* Then move on. You do not need to share private details.

### Your accent

Having an accent is normal. What matters is clarity. If someone does not understand, repeat the key word slowly rather than the whole sentence. Spend your practice time on **stress and pauses** more than on single sounds; see our guides to [word stress](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) and [sentence stress](/en/blog/sentence-stress-in-english-speaking-guide/).

### "Why do you want to leave your last job?"

Stay positive and specific: *"I learned a lot, and I am looking for more responsibility."* Avoid criticising a former manager.

### Salary

If asked early, you can say: *"I would like to understand the full role first. Based on my research, I am expecting a range of X to Y."* Our [salary negotiation guide](/en/blog/salary-negotiation-in-english-guide/) has more language for this stage.

## Questions to ask at the end

Almost every interview ends with "Do you have any questions for us?" Say yes and ask one or two:

- *"What does a successful first six months look like?"*
- *"How is the team structured, and who would I work with most?"*
- *"What are the biggest challenges in this role at the moment?"*
- *"What are the next steps in the process?"*

Avoid starting with holidays, working hours or "how quickly can I be promoted".

2. "I did not work two years because of moving."
3. "Maybe I can learn this software."
4. "In my company we make report."
5. What can you say if you do not understand a question?

### Answers

1. *I have four years of customer service experience, mainly in busy retail stores.*
2. *I took two years to relocate and settle, and I am now ready to work.*
3. *I have not used this software yet, but I learn new systems quickly.*
4. *In my last company I prepared the weekly reports.* (Use a past form and an article.)
5. *Sorry, could you repeat that?* or *Do you mean in my current role?*

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should my answers be?

Aim for 60 to 90 seconds. If the interviewer wants more detail, they will ask.

### Is it okay to ask the interviewer to repeat a question?

Yes. It is better than answering a different question. Say *"Sorry, could you repeat that?"*

### Should I mention my visa or work permit?

Only if the form or interviewer asks. Answer briefly and factually; employers need to confirm that you can work legally.

### How can I reduce nerves in English?

Prepare three or four stories, say them aloud several times and record yourself once. Familiar stories make fast questions much easier.
