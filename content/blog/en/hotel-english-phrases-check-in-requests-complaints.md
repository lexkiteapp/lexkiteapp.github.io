At a hotel, most conversations are short and predictable, and a few ready **hotel English phrases** carry you from booking to check-out. Learn the polite openers (*I'd like to…*, *Could I…?*, *Would it be possible to…?*), the key nouns, and how to raise a problem without sounding angry, and you will manage almost any stay in English.

This guide covers booking a room, check-in, requests such as late check-out and extra towels, polite complaints, bills and extra charges, and the British and American differences that confuse people most. It is written for learners, including Vietnamese, German, Japanese and Korean speakers who are often very polite in their own language but sound blunt when they translate directly into English. For a wider travel phrase list, see our guide to [travel English phrases for abroad trips](/en/blog/travel-english-phrases-for-abroad-trips/).

## Booking a room: hotel English phrases for phone, email and desk

Whether you write an email or call, a good booking message says the dates, the number of guests, the room type, and any special need. Keep it short.

- *I'd like to book a double room for three nights, from the tenth to the thirteenth of May.*
- *Do you have any rooms available for next weekend?*
- *How much is a single room per night?* (Ask whether tax and breakfast are included.)
- *Is breakfast included in the rate?*
- *Could I have a quiet room away from the street, please?*
- *What is your cancellation policy?*
- *Is there free Wi-Fi, and is parking available?*

Useful nouns: *a single room* (one person), *a double room* (one double bed), *a twin room* (two single beds), *a suite* (larger room with a sitting area), *a rate* (the price per night), *a deposit*, *a reservation* (US) or *a booking* (UK, also common everywhere). A *booking confirmation* is the email that proves your reservation; keep it on your phone.

A short email example:

> **Subject:** Booking request, 10 to 13 May
>
> Dear Sir or Madam,
>
> I would like to reserve a twin room for two adults for three nights, arriving on 10 May. Could you let me know the price, and whether breakfast is included? We will arrive late in the evening, at around 10 pm. Please could you confirm that this is acceptable?
>
> Kind regards,
> Minh Anh Nguyen

For tone and layout of messages like this, see our guide to [how to write a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/).

## Check-in: what the receptionist says and what you can answer

Check-in is a script. You give your name, show ID, and agree to a few details. Hotel staff may ask for a card as a guarantee; the rules about holds and deposits vary by hotel and country, so ask if you are unsure.

> **Receptionist:** Good evening. Welcome to the Harbour Hotel. Do you have a reservation?
>
> **You:** Yes, it's under Tran. I booked a double room for three nights.
>
> **Receptionist:** Thank you. Could I see your passport, please? And a credit card for incidentals?
>
> **You:** Of course. Could you tell me what time breakfast is?
>
> **Receptionist:** Breakfast is from seven to ten in the restaurant on the first floor. Here is your key card. You're in room 507, and the lift is on your left.
>
> **You:** Thank you. Could I have a late check-out tomorrow, if that's possible?

Phrases to know:

- *I have a reservation under the name Schmidt.*
- *I'm checking in. My booking is for two nights.*
- *Is it possible to check in early? Our flight arrived this morning.*
- *Could I leave my luggage with you until the room is ready?*
- *What's the Wi-Fi password?*
- *Could you tell me where the lift is?*

Note *incidentals*: extra charges such as room service or minibar, for which a hotel may take a card hold. Ask *Will the hold be released at check-out, and how long does that take?*

**Learner mistake:** *I have a booking in your hotel* should be *I have a booking at your hotel*; or simply *I have a reservation*. The preposition is *at* for a place you go to. See our [guide to in, on and at](/en/blog/prepositions-in-on-at/).

## Hotel English phrases for polite requests

Requests are where learners sound too direct. The English formula is simple: *Could I…?* (asking permission), *Could you…?* (asking someone to do something), *Would it be possible to…?* (more formal), *I was wondering if…* (very polite). Add *please* and *thank you*. See our guide to [modal verbs like can, could and should](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/) for the logic behind them.

| Instead of this (too blunt) | Say this |
| --- | --- |
| *Give me more towels.* | *Could we have some extra towels, please?* |
| *I need late check-out.* | *Would it be possible to check out a little later?* |
| *Change my room.* | *Is there any chance of moving to a different room?* |
| *The wifi doesn't work.* | *I'm having trouble connecting to the Wi-Fi. Could you help?* |
| *Wake me at six.* | *Could I have a wake-up call at six, please?* |

More requests you might need:

- *Could we have an extra pillow and blanket?*
- *Could you send someone to clean the room, please?* / *Could housekeeping come by this afternoon?*
- *Could you recommend a good restaurant nearby?*
- *Is there an iron or a hairdryer I can borrow?*
- *Could you call a taxi for us for eight o'clock?*
- *Could we keep our bags here after check-out?*

Typical replies from staff: *Certainly.* / *Of course, I'll send someone up.* / *I'm afraid we're fully booked tonight, but I can check.* / *There may be an extra charge. Would you like me to confirm?* Late check-out may be free or paid depending on the hotel, so just ask: *Is there a charge for late check-out?*

## Polite complaints: sounding firm without sounding angry

When something is wrong, speak early (a problem is easier to fix while you are still at the hotel), and speak to the front desk first, not online. Say what is wrong and what you would like.

{{diagram: d2 | Four parts of a good complaint: who and where, the problem, a little detail, and a clear request.}}

Typical situations:

- *The air conditioning isn't working in my room.*
- *The room hasn't been cleaned.* / *The room wasn't made up today.*
- *There's no hot water.*
- *The room is very noisy, and I can hear the street all night.*
- *The room is not the one I booked. I reserved a room with a view.*
- *The bathroom smells of smoke, and I booked a non-smoking room.*

> **You:** Hi, this is room 412. I'm sorry to bother you, but the air conditioning isn't working.
>
> **Receptionist:** I'm very sorry. Has it been like this since you arrived?
>
> **You:** It worked this morning, but it stopped around lunchtime. The room is very warm now.
>
> **Receptionist:** I'll send someone from maintenance right away.
>
> **You:** Thank you. If it can't be fixed quickly, would it be possible to move to another room?
>
> **Receptionist:** Of course. I'll check what we have available.

Phrases that keep a calm, firm tone:

- *I'm afraid there's a problem with…*
- *I'm sorry to trouble you, but…*
- *Could you look into this for me?*
- *What can we do about it?*
- *I'd appreciate it if someone could fix this today.*
- *I'd like to speak to the manager, please.* (Use it politely, after the first request has not worked.)

**Avoid:** *This is a terrible hotel* or *You must fix it now*. They make staff defensive. Also avoid *It doesn't work*; it is better to say what does not work, and since when. If the problem is serious and not solved, you may later write a message to the hotel; keep the facts and dates, and avoid threats. Customer rights differ by country and booking site, so check your booking terms.

## Bills, extra charges and check-out

At check-out you settle the bill. Read it before paying.

- *Could I have the bill, please?* / *I'd like to check out of room 507.*
- *Could you explain this charge?* / *What is this charge for?*
- *I don't think I used the minibar. Could you check that, please?*
- *Is the city tax included, or is it extra?* (Some places add a local tax, in the room price or on top.)
- *Is service included?* (A *service charge* or *resort fee* may be added; check the booking terms.)
- *Could I have a receipt, please? / Could you email me the invoice?*
- *Could I pay by card?* / *Could I split the bill?*

> **You:** Good morning. I'd like to check out of room 507, please.
>
> **Receptionist:** Certainly. Here's your bill. Did you use the minibar?
>
> **You:** No, we didn't. But I see a charge for it here. Could you check that?
>
> **Receptionist:** I'm sorry, you're right. I'll remove it. Anything else?
>
> **You:** No, that's all. Could you send the receipt to my email, please?

If you leave something behind, say: *I think I left a charger in room 507. Could you check, please?* (Lost property rules vary by hotel.)

## British and American hotel words you will hear

{{diagram: d1 | A few hotel words differ between American and British English. Staff will usually understand both.}}

- **Lift / elevator.** The same machine; the word depends on the country. If you ask *Where's the elevator?* in London, staff will understand you.
- **Ground floor / first floor.** In the UK, Australia and many other places, the *ground floor* is the entrance level and the *first floor* is the next one up. In the US and Canada, the *first floor* is usually the entrance level (in Canada this can vary by building). A message like *breakfast is on the first floor* can mean different floors, so ask *Do you mean one floor up from the entrance?* when in doubt.
- **Reception / front desk.** *Reception* is the British word for the desk, *front desk* the American one. *Concierge* is a person who helps with tickets, transport and tips, mainly in larger hotels.
- **Bill / check.** In a US restaurant the *check* is the bill; in a hotel, Americans usually say *bill*, *folio* or *statement*. In the UK it is simply the *bill*.
- **Toilet, restroom, bathroom.** In the US, *restroom* or *bathroom* is the polite word for a toilet; in the UK, *toilet* is normal in public places and *bathroom* means the room with a bath or shower.
- **Vacancy.** A sign saying *Vacancy* means rooms are available; *No vacancy* means full. In British English, you will often hear *We're fully booked*.

Do not worry about perfect regional choice. Be polite, speak slowly and ask again if a word is unclear.

## Mistakes learners make in hotels

1. **Too blunt.** *I want a room* sounds abrupt. Use *I'd like a room* or *Do you have any rooms available?*
2. **Saying "I have a reservation of Tran".** The natural form is *under the name Tran*.
3. **Mixing *make a reservation* and *do a reservation*.** In English you *make* or *book* a reservation.
4. **Forgetting *check in* and *check-in*.** The verb has a space (*We check in at three*), the noun has a hyphen (*Check-in is at three*). Same for *check out* and *check-out*.
5. **Using *room service* wrongly.** *Room service* means food brought to your room; for cleaning, say *housekeeping*.
6. **Saying *thank you* only once.** Hotel English is full of *please*, *thank you* and *sorry to bother you*. They are not extra; they are normal.

## Self-check: choose the best option

1. Which is more polite: *Give me another towel* or *Could we have another towel, please?*
2. In London, which floor is directly above the *ground floor*?
3. Complete: *I have a reservation ______ the name Kim.*
4. You want to leave your bags after check-out. What can you ask?
5. The air conditioning does not work. Make a polite complaint.
6. In American English, what is a *front desk*?

**Answers**

1. *Could we have another towel, please?* It uses a question with *could* and *please*.
2. The *first floor*. In the US and Canada, the *second floor* is above the entrance level.
3. **under.** *Under the name Kim* is the natural phrase.
4. *Could we leave our bags here until this afternoon?* or *Could you keep our luggage after check-out?*
5. For example: *I'm sorry to bother you, but the air conditioning isn't working in room 412. Could someone take a look?*
6. The hotel reception desk, where you check in and ask questions.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I ask for a late check-out politely?

Say *Would it be possible to check out a little later tomorrow?* or *Could I have a late check-out, please?* Then ask *Is there an extra charge?* Whether it is free or paid depends on the hotel, the rate and how busy the hotel is, so ask the front desk rather than assuming.

### What is the difference between a booking and a reservation?

They mean almost the same thing. *Reservation* is more common in American English, and *booking* in British English, but both are understood everywhere. You *make* or *book* a reservation, and the hotel sends a *confirmation*.

### How do I complain about a hotel room politely?

Contact the front desk early, name your room, say what is wrong and since when, and ask for a solution: *I'm sorry to trouble you, but the heating isn't working in room 214. Could someone take a look, or could we move to another room?* Staying calm and specific usually gets quicker help.

### Is the first floor the same in British and American English?

No. In the UK and Australia, the *ground floor* is the entrance level and the *first floor* is one level above. In the US and Canada, the *first floor* usually means the entrance level. If a direction matters, confirm it: *Is that one floor up from the lobby?*
