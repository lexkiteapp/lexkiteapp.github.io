The **IELTS Listening question types** look like a long list, but they all test the same skill: following a spoken message and writing down the exact detail or idea you are asked for. The test has 40 questions in four sections, each recording is played once, and the order of the questions follows the order of the speaking. Once you know what each type asks and how it usually tricks you, a section stops feeling like a surprise and starts feeling like a routine.

## How the four sections are built

The sections get harder as you go:

| Section | Situation | Typical task style |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A conversation between two people in an everyday setting | Form or note completion with names, numbers and addresses |
| 2 | A monologue in an everyday setting | Multiple choice, labelling a plan or map |
| 3 | A discussion between up to four speakers in an education setting | Matching, multiple choice, sentence completion |
| 4 | A monologue on an academic subject | Note, summary or sentence completion |

The recordings are played once. Before each section you get a short time to read the questions, and that time is the most valuable thirty seconds of the test.

## A rhythm that works for all IELTS Listening question types

Four habits carry over to everything below:

- **Predict before you listen.** Decide whether the gap needs a name, a number, a date, a noun or a verb. That narrows what you listen for.
- **Follow the speaker, not the paper.** The audio moves in the same order as the questions. If you lose your place, jump to the next question, not back.
- **Write at once.** Do not keep an answer in your head for later; the next question arrives almost immediately.
- **Check grammar and spelling at the end.** On the paper test you get ten minutes after the recording to copy answers to the answer sheet; on the computer test the review time is much shorter, so check as you go. Wrong spelling and wrong word forms lose the mark even when you heard correctly.

## Completion tasks: form, note, table, summary, sentence

These tasks give you text with gaps to fill using words from the recording. Read the instruction for the **word limit** (for example, no more than two words and/or a number). Writing three words when the limit is two makes the answer wrong.

What helps:

- Use the words in the recording, not your own synonyms.
- Look at the words around the gap. If the sentence before the gap ends with *a*, the answer starts with a consonant sound; if it has *is*, a singular noun is likely.
- Names are often spelled out letter by letter. Practise the alphabet and common number patterns such as 13 versus 30.
- Watch for a **correction**: *the meeting is on the fifteenth... sorry, the sixteenth.* The corrected detail is the answer.

Original example: a speaker says, "I can pick you up at half past three... actually, make it four, the bus is late." The answer for *Pick-up time:* is *4 o'clock* or *4.00*, not three thirty.

## Multiple choice

For multiple choice, the speaker will mention all the options in some form. Only one fits what the speaker means. The usual pattern is that a speaker mentions an option, then changes their mind, or says it applies to someone else.

A method:

1. Underline the key idea in the question and in each option while you read.
2. Mark an option only when the speaker has finished the thought, not when you hear a matching word.
3. If you are unsure, keep your best guess and move on; there is no penalty for a wrong answer.

When there are several answers to choose, the order of the options usually does not match the order they are heard.

### A worked multiple-choice example

Read this original recording script: "Most people choose the morning tour because it is cheaper, but I'd recommend the afternoon one. It's a bit longer, and the guide takes you inside the old library, which the morning group doesn't see." The question asks: *Why does the speaker recommend the afternoon tour?* The options are A it is cheaper, B it is shorter, C it includes the library, D it has a better guide. The words *cheaper* and *guide* both appear, which is the trap. Option A belongs to the morning tour, B contradicts *a bit longer*, and D uses *guide* in a different role. Only C matches what the speaker says.

Notice that you could only be sure after hearing the whole sentence.

## Matching

In matching tasks you link items, such as people and opinions, to a list of options. The audio generally follows the order of the items on the left, while the options on the right can be mentioned in any order. Read the options first so you recognise paraphrases when you hear them. This is where practice with paraphrase pays off; the guide to [paraphrasing in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) will help.

## Map, plan and diagram labelling

For maps and plans, spend your preview time understanding the layout: where the entrance is, what the compass directions and landmarks are. Then follow the speaker's directions as if you were walking.

Useful language to know: *opposite*, *next to*, *beside*, *in front of*, *behind*, *between*, *at the end of*, *on your left*, *straight ahead*, *past the...*. Candidates lose marks because they hear *next to the library* and choose a building *opposite the library*. Trace with your pencil as the speaker moves.

## Sentence completion and short answers

These tasks ask for facts. Predict the word type, then listen for the answer near the key words of the question. Take care with plurals (*machine* versus *machines*) and with singular versus plural agreement. Check the word limit again before you move on.

## Common traps across all types

| Trap | What happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Distractors | A wrong option is mentioned first | Wait for the speaker's final statement |
| Corrections | The first answer is replaced | Listen for *actually*, *sorry*, *I mean* |
| Spelling | You hear it right but write it wrong | Learn common topic words and place names |
| Word limit | Too many words | Read the instruction every time |
| Lost place | You stay on a missed question | Jump to the next and rejoin the recording |
| Number confusion | 15 versus 50 | Learn the stress patterns: thirTEEN versus THIRty |

Fast speech can add to the problem; [how to catch fast speech in IELTS Listening](/en/blog/how-to-catch-fast-speech-in-ielts-listening/) and [connected speech in English](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/) explain why words seem to disappear. For a broader list of habits, see [IELTS Listening tips](/en/blog/ielts-listening-tips/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many question types are there in IELTS Listening?

There are around ten formats, including form, note, table, flow-chart and summary completion, multiple choice, matching, plan, map and diagram labelling, sentence completion and short-answer questions. Each test combines a mixture of them across the four sections.

### Is the recording played twice?

No. Each recording is played once, so you need to read, predict and write in rhythm with the speaker.

### Does spelling matter in IELTS Listening?

Yes. A correctly heard but wrongly spelled answer is marked incorrect. Both British and American spellings are accepted if they are consistent and correct.

### What should I do if I miss an answer?

Let it go and move to the next question immediately. Staying with a missed answer can make you miss the next one too.
