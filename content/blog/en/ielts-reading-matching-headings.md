**Matching headings** is the IELTS Reading task many candidates fear most. You get a list of headings (always more than you need) and must choose the one that best summarises each paragraph. It feels slow because you cannot simply scan for a word; you have to understand what each paragraph is *about*. The good news is that the skill is learnable: once you can summarise a paragraph in a few words and recognise the common traps, matching headings becomes one of the most predictable tasks in the test.

## How matching headings works in IELTS Reading

In the Academic Reading test you have 60 minutes for three passages and 40 questions. A matching headings task usually looks like this:

- A list of headings labelled with Roman numerals (i, ii, iii…).
- Instructions to choose the correct heading for some or all of the paragraphs (A, B, C…).
- More headings than paragraphs, so some are never used.
- Sometimes an example already completed; that heading cannot be used again.

When this task appears, it often comes first in the questions for that passage. Many teachers recommend doing it first too: summarising each paragraph gives you a map of the text, which makes the passage's other questions faster.

## Why matching headings feels difficult

Most IELTS question types reward **scanning**: you find a name, date or keyword, then read closely around it. Our guide to [skimming and scanning](/en/blog/ielts-reading-skimming-and-scanning/) explains those skills. Matching headings is different. It tests **global understanding**: the main idea, the writer's purpose and how the paragraph fits the whole text.

Three things make it hard:

1. **Paraphrase.** The heading rarely uses the paragraph's words. *Rising costs* may appear as *financial pressures*.
2. **Distractors.** Wrong headings often describe a detail or an example that really is in the paragraph.
3. **Similar headings.** Two headings may share a topic (both about *energy*), but only one matches the paragraph's *purpose* (a problem, a cause, a solution).

## The five-step method for matching headings

{{diagram: d1 | Read the paragraph, summarise it in your own words, then find the heading that matches your summary}}

**Step 1: Cross out the example heading.** If paragraph A is done as an example, its heading is gone. Remove it from the list so it does not distract you.

**Step 2: Read one paragraph quickly but completely.** A popular shortcut is to read only the first sentence, but it is not reliable. In many texts the first sentence is a link from the previous paragraph, and the main point arrives later, often after *however* or *but*. Read the whole paragraph at a brisk pace.

**Step 3: Summarise it in 3–6 words.** Before you look at the headings, decide what the paragraph does: *explains why the project failed*, *describes early attempts*, *suggests a solution*. This step is the heart of the method, because it stops the headings from steering your reading.

**Step 4: Match by meaning.** Now compare your summary with the headings. Look for the heading that covers the *whole* paragraph, including its purpose. Paraphrase skills matter here; [how to paraphrase in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) shows the common ways ideas are reworded.

**Step 5: Eliminate and return.** If two headings seem possible, note both and move on. Later paragraphs often use one of them, which settles the doubt. Leave the hardest paragraph for last, when fewer headings remain.

## Main idea vs detail: the classic trap

{{diagram: d2 | The distractor is usually true for one sentence; the right heading is true for the whole paragraph}}

Imagine a paragraph that discusses why cities are planting more trees. It mentions that one city planted 10,000 trees last year, then explains the main reasons: shade, lower temperatures and cleaner air. Consider two headings:

- *A record-breaking planting programme* → a detail (one city, one number).
- *The benefits that motivate urban tree planting* → the main idea.

The first heading is tempting because the number stands out. But it covers one sentence, not the paragraph.

### Signals that help you find the main idea

- **Contrast words** (*however*, *yet*, *in fact*): the writer's real point often follows them.
- **Purpose language** (*the main reason*, *one solution*, *the consequence was*): these mark the paragraph's job.
- **Repeated concepts** in different words across several sentences: that is the topic.
- **Examples** (*for instance*, *such as*): these support the main idea; they are rarely the main idea themselves.

When long sentences slow you down, the technique in [sentence parsing for IELTS Reading](/en/blog/sentence-parsing-for-ielts-reading/) helps you find the core subject and verb quickly.

## Try it: a sample passage

Match each paragraph (B–D) with the correct heading. Paragraph A is the example.

**A** Most people picture a library as a quiet room full of books. For centuries, that picture was accurate: libraries existed to store printed material and lend it to readers who could not afford their own copies.

**B** Over the past few decades, however, the role of public libraries has widened considerably. Many now offer free internet access, help with job applications and classes in basic computer skills. For people without a home computer, these services can be the only route to government forms that are increasingly available online.

**C** This expansion has not been easy to fund. Budgets in many areas have been cut, and some branches have reduced their opening hours or closed entirely. Librarians report that demand for digital help is growing at the same time as the money to provide it is shrinking.

**D** Some libraries have responded by forming partnerships. Local businesses sponsor coding workshops, universities send student volunteers to run homework clubs, and health services use library rooms for advice sessions. These arrangements bring in skills and resources that libraries could not afford on their own.

**List of headings**

i. The traditional purpose of libraries (*example: A*)
ii. Why fewer people visit libraries today
iii. Working with other organisations to fill the gap
iv. New services beyond lending books
v. A shortage of money as demand rises
vi. The history of the printing press

### Answers

- **B → iv.** The paragraph's main idea is the widened role: internet access, job help, computer classes. Note the contrast word *however*, which signals the shift from the old role.
- **C → v.** *Not been easy to fund*, *budgets cut* and *money… shrinking* all express a shortage of money, while *demand… growing* matches *as demand rises*.
- **D → iii.** *Partnerships*, *sponsor*, *volunteers* and *health services* all describe working with other organisations, and *could not afford on their own* is the gap being filled.
- **ii** is a distractor: no paragraph says fewer people visit libraries; paragraph C says demand is *growing*. **vi** shares the topic of print but is not discussed.

Notice that no correct heading repeats the paragraph's key words exactly. That is typical.

## Time management

With 60 minutes for three passages, you have about 20 minutes per passage, including transferring answers on paper. A useful target for a matching headings task of five to seven paragraphs is around 8–10 minutes, leaving time for the passage's other questions. If one paragraph has taken more than two minutes, write your best guess, mark it and move on; you can return if time allows.

Speed comes from vocabulary as much as technique. Academic paraphrase depends on words such as *significant*, *decline*, *factor* and *approach*; the [Academic Word List guide](/en/blog/academic-word-list/) shows how to learn them efficiently. Our [topic vocabulary lists](/en/vocabulary/) group useful words by theme.

## Common mistakes in matching headings

- **Matching a single word.** A shared word is not proof; check the whole paragraph.
- **Reading headings first and too closely.** This makes you hunt for headings rather than understand the paragraph. A quick look at the list is fine; summarise before you compare.
- **Assuming the first sentence is the topic sentence.** Sometimes it is, often it isn't.
- **Getting stuck.** One difficult paragraph should not cost you three other questions.
- **Forgetting the example.** Using the example's heading again is a common, avoidable error.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I read the headings or the paragraphs first?

Glance at the headings so you know the range of ideas, then read each paragraph and summarise it before comparing. Reading every heading closely first tends to make you search for word matches instead of understanding the paragraph.

### Can the same heading be used twice?

Normally no. Each heading fits one paragraph, and there are more headings than paragraphs. Always follow the exact instructions on the question paper, though.

### Is the first sentence always the topic sentence?

No. It often is in simple texts, but in IELTS passages the first sentence may connect to the previous paragraph, and the main point may come after a contrast word such as *however*. Read the whole paragraph.

### How can I get faster at matching headings?

Practise summarising paragraphs in 3–6 words on any English text, such as news articles and reports, not only exam passages. As this habit becomes automatic, the task speeds up naturally.
