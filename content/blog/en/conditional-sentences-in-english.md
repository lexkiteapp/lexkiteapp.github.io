**Conditional sentences** describe a condition and its result: *If you heat ice, it melts. If I won the lottery, I'd travel.* The forms look confusing in lists, but they follow one logic. Ask two questions: **Is this real or imaginary?** and **When is it: always, the future, the present, or the past?** The answers tell you which conditional to use, and the verb forms follow automatically.

## How conditional sentences work

Every conditional has two parts:

- the **if-clause** (the condition): *If it rains…*
- the **main clause** (the result): *…we'll stay inside.*

Either part can come first. When the if-clause comes first, put a comma after it: *If it rains, we'll stay inside.* When the main clause comes first, no comma is needed: *We'll stay inside if it rains.*

## Zero conditional: facts that are always true

**Form:** *if* + present simple, present simple

- *If you heat water to 100°C at sea level, it boils.*
- *If I drink coffee after 4 p.m., I can't sleep.*

Use it for scientific facts, rules and habits, where the result follows every time. Here *if* means almost the same as *when* or *whenever*: *When I drink coffee late, I can't sleep.*

## First conditional: real future possibilities

**Form:** *if* + present simple, *will* + base verb

- *If you leave now, you'll catch the train.*
- *If the price goes down, we'll buy two.*

The situation is genuinely possible. The most common error is putting *will* in the if-clause:

- ✗ *If it will rain tomorrow, we will stay home.*
- ✓ *If it rains tomorrow, we will stay home.*

The main clause can also use other modals or the imperative: *If you finish early, you **can** go. If you see Ana, **tell** her to call me.*

### Unless, as long as, provided that

**Unless** means *if … not*: *You won't improve **unless** you practise* = *You won't improve if you don't practise.* Do not use a negative after *unless*: ✗ *unless you don't practise*.

**As long as** and **provided (that)** mean *only if*: *You can borrow the car as long as you return it by six.*

## Second conditional: imaginary present or unlikely future

**Form:** *if* + past simple, *would* + base verb

- *If I had more free time, I would learn Japanese.* (I don't have much free time.)
- *If you lost your passport abroad, what would you do?* (unlikely, hypothetical)

The past form here does **not** mean past time. It signals distance from reality. That is why the second conditional is perfect for imagining, advising and speculating.

### "If I were you"

With *be*, standard and formal English uses **were** for all persons: *If I were you, I'd apply. If she were here, she'd know.* *If I was* is common in informal speech, but *were* is the safe choice in exams and is the only form in the fixed advice phrase *If I were you*.

**First or second?** The choice depends on how likely you think the situation is. *If I get the job, I'll move* (I have a real chance). *If I got the job, I'd move* (I doubt it will happen).

## Third conditional: imaginary past

**Form:** *if* + past perfect, *would have* + past participle

- *If we had left earlier, we would have caught the train.* (We didn't leave earlier; we missed it.)
- *If she hadn't helped me, I wouldn't have passed.*

Use it for regrets, criticism and imagining a different past. Remember that both parts describe things that **did not happen**. You can replace *would* with *could* or *might* for ability or possibility: *If I had known, I **could have** helped.*

In speech, *had* and *would* both contract to *'d*: *If I'd known, I'd have called.* The first *'d* is *had* (past perfect) and the second is *would*. Do not write ✗ *If I would have known*; the if-clause takes the past perfect.

## Mixed conditional sentences

Real life does not always keep the time of the condition and the time of the result the same. **Mixed conditionals** combine them.

### Past condition, present result

**Form:** *if* + past perfect, *would* + base verb

{{diagram: d2 | An unreal decision in the past changes the present: the most common mixed conditional}}

- *If I had taken that job in Berlin, I would be living there now.*
- *If you hadn't missed the bus, you wouldn't be late.*

### Present condition, past result

**Form:** *if* + past simple, *would have* + past participle

- *If I were better at maths, I would have chosen engineering.* (I'm not good at maths, a general present fact, so I didn't choose it.)

Mixed conditionals are a natural way to show grammatical range in **IELTS Speaking Part 3**, where examiners ask about hypothetical situations and causes. Our [IELTS Speaking Part 3 guide](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-3-tips/) shows how to use them without sounding rehearsed.

## Formal variants: inversion

In formal writing you can drop *if* and invert the subject and auxiliary:

- *Had I known about the problem, I would have acted sooner.* (= If I had known)
- *Were the government to raise taxes, consumption would fall.* (= If the government raised taxes)
- *Should you require further information, please contact us.* (= If you require)

These are useful in formal emails and academic writing, but one well-placed example is enough; overusing inversion sounds stiff.

## Conditional sentences in writing and speaking

In **IELTS Writing Task 2**, conditionals help you explain consequences: *If governments invested more in public transport, fewer people would rely on cars.* Combine them with the cautious modals in our guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/), and place them in the body paragraphs described in [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/).

The main clause relies on modal verbs (*will*, *would*, *could*, *might*), so if those feel shaky, review [modal verbs explained](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/) first. You can hear conditionals used naturally in the band 8 answers on our [IELTS Speaking topics pages](/en/speaking/).

## Common mistakes

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right |
|---|---|
| *If I will see him, I'll tell him.* | *If I see him, I'll tell him.* |
| *If I would have money, I would buy it.* | *If I had money, I would buy it.* |
| *If I would have known, I would have come.* | *If I had known, I would have come.* |
| *Unless you don't hurry, you'll be late.* | *Unless you hurry, you'll be late.* |
| *If I am you, I would apologise.* | *If I were you, I would apologise.* |

## Self-check

Put the verbs in brackets into the correct form.

1. If you mix red and white, you ___ (get) pink.
2. If it ___ (be) sunny tomorrow, we'll go to the beach.
3. If I ___ (know) the answer, I'd tell you, but I don't.
4. If they had booked earlier, they ___ (get) cheaper seats.
5. If I ___ (not / stay) up so late last night, I wouldn't be so tired now.
6. You won't pass ___ (if / unless) you start revising.
7. ___ (I / know) about the delay, I would have taken a later flight. (formal inversion)
8. If I ___ (be) you, I'd ask for a refund.

### Answers

1. **get** – zero conditional, a fact.
2. **is** – first conditional; no *will* in the if-clause.
3. **knew** – second conditional, unreal present.
4. **would have got** (or *would have gotten* in American English) – third conditional.
5. **hadn't stayed** – mixed: past condition, present result.
6. **unless** – *unless* = *if not*.
7. **Had I known** – inversion of *If I had known*.
8. **were** – the fixed advice form.

Score 7 or more and you are ready to use conditionals actively in speaking. If tense forms slowed you down, a quick review of [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) will help, since the past perfect builds on the same participles.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many types of conditional sentences are there?

Most grammar courses teach four main types (zero, first, second and third) plus mixed conditionals. Real English also uses many other combinations, such as *If you've finished, you can go*, which follow the same logic of reality and time.

### Can "will" ever go in the if-clause?

Rarely, and not for simple future time. *Will* in the if-clause can express willingness or a polite request: *If you'll wait a moment, I'll check.* For a normal future condition, use the present simple.

### Is "if I was" wrong?

It is common in informal speech, so it is not exactly wrong. In formal writing, exams and the fixed phrase *If I were you*, use *were*.

### What is the difference between "if" and "when"?

*When* shows you expect something to happen; *if* shows it may or may not happen. *When I get home, I'll call you* (I will get home). *If I get home early, I'll call you* (I might not).
