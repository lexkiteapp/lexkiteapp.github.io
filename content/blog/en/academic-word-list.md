The Academic Word List (AWL) is a list of 570 word families that appear frequently across academic texts in many subjects, compiled by Averil Coxhead in 2000 and divided into 10 sublists by frequency. Words such as *analyse*, *significant*, *factor*, *evidence* and *approach* belong to it. For IELTS Academic and TOEFL candidates, it is one of the most efficient lists to study, provided you already know everyday English well and you learn each family as a set of usable forms rather than as single translations. Many of these words also appear in the shorter, adaptive [new TOEFL iBT format](/en/blog/new-toefl-ibt-format-2026/).

Below you will find where the list came from, why it matters for exams, a realistic study plan by sublist, and a self-check on the word forms that cause the most errors.

## What is the Academic Word List?

Coxhead built the list from a corpus of university-level texts across four broad areas: arts, commerce, law and science. A word family made the list only if it appeared often, across many subjects, and was not already among the most common everyday words of English (those were covered by an older general service list). The result is vocabulary that is not technical in any one field but sits at the heart of academic writing everywhere.

A **word family** is a headword plus its related forms. The family *analyse* includes *analysis*, *analyses*, *analyst*, *analytic*, *analytical*, *analytically* and *analysed*. Counting families rather than individual forms is why "570" sounds small but actually represents several thousand forms.

The list is split into **10 sublists**. Sublists 1 to 9 contain 60 families each and sublist 10 contains 30. Sublist 1 holds the most frequent families, so the first sublists give you the biggest return. The table below shows a sample from five sublists; the complete lists are on the [vocabulary pages](/en/vocabulary/).

| Sublist | Example families |
|---|---|
| 1 | analyse, approach, benefit, concept, data, establish, factor, significant |
| 2 | achieve, affect, conclude, consequent, distinct, impact, relevant, resource |
| 3 | alternative, emphasis, framework, negate, outcome, physical, scheme, sufficient |
| 5 | academy, capacity, challenge, decline, generate, logic, objective, trend |
| 10 | adjacent, albeit, forthcoming, notwithstanding, persist, whereby |

## Why the academic word list matters for IELTS Academic and TOEFL

In her original study, Coxhead reported that AWL words made up roughly one in ten words of the academic texts she analysed (about 10%), compared with under 2% of a fiction corpus of similar size. That contrast is the whole point of the list: these words are common in lectures and journals but rare in everyday reading, so most learners do not pick them up by accident. That may sound modest, but those words often carry the key idea of a sentence. Compare:

> *The **data** **indicate** that the new **policy** had a **significant** **impact** on the **income** of rural households.*

Remove the AWL words and the sentence loses almost all its meaning. This is why the list helps in several parts of the test.

| Skill | How the AWL helps |
|---|---|
| Reading | Passages and questions are full of AWL words, and paraphrased statements often swap one AWL word for another (*rise* → *increase*, *show* → *indicate*). This matters a lot in tasks like [True/False/Not Given](/en/blog/ielts-reading-true-false-not-given/) and when executing [active reading for IELTS](/en/blog/active-reading-strategies-for-ielts/). |
| Writing | Task 1 and Task 2 reward precise, appropriately formal vocabulary. *Factor*, *trend*, *proportion* and *significant* do a lot of work. |
| Listening | Part 4 is an academic-style talk, and recognising AWL words by sound helps you follow it. |
| Speaking | Useful in Part 3 when you discuss abstract issues, less so in the personal questions of Part 1. |

One warning: the AWL does not replace general vocabulary. If you are still unsure of the most common 2,000–3,000 words, build that base first, for example with a core list such as the NGSL (about 2,800 high-frequency words).

## How to study the AWL sublist by sublist

Trying to swallow all 570 families at once is the quickest way to give up. A steadier plan:

1. **Start with Sublist 1, even if it looks easy.** You probably recognise *benefit* and *data*. The test is whether you can use *beneficial*, *beneficiary* and *the data indicate* correctly.
2. **Learn 8–12 families a day.** A 60-family sublist then takes about a week, and the whole list roughly two to three months.
3. **Learn the family, not one form.** For each headword, note the noun, verb, adjective and adverb you are likely to need, plus one natural collocation. The guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) shows how to choose partners.
4. **Write your own example sentence** on a topic you might meet in Task 2: education, health, environment, technology, work.
5. **Keep reviewing old sublists while learning new ones.** Without spaced reviews, Sublist 1 will be faded by the time you reach Sublist 4. The workflow in [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/) explains how to balance new words and reviews.
6. **Test yourself at the end of each sublist.** Take a quick quiz, then write a 100-word paragraph that uses at least eight words from it.

### Example: one study session with Sublist 1

Here are five families as you might note them:

- *establish* (v) – *establishment* (n), *established* (adj). "The university was established in 1965." Very common in the passive.
- *consist* (v) – *consistent* (adj), *consistency* (n). "The panel consists of five experts." Almost always *consist of*.
- *require* (v) – *requirement* (n). "Entry requirements vary between courses."
- *assess* (v) – *assessment* (n). "Students are assessed through coursework and exams."
- *distribute* (v) – *distribution* (n). "The uneven distribution of wealth remains a concern."

Notice the details: *consist of*, the passive with *establish*, the plural *requirements*. Small points like these decide whether a word sounds natural.

## Word forms: where most AWL errors happen

Because the list is organised by family, the most common mistake is not misunderstanding meaning but choosing the wrong form. Learners whose first language does not change word shape for part of speech, such as Chinese, Vietnamese or Indonesian speakers, are especially prone to this, but it affects almost everyone.

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *This is a significance change.* | *This is a significant change.* | An adjective is needed before a noun. |
| *The government should priority education.* | *The government should prioritise education.* | A verb is needed after *should*. |
| *It is economic to cycle to work.* | *It is economical to cycle to work.* | *Economical* means saving money; *economic* relates to the economy. |
| *Researchers analysis the results.* | *Researchers analyse the results.* | *Analysis* is the noun. |

Pronunciation also shifts within families: *ANalyse* but *aNAlysis*, *ecoNOMic* but *eCONomy*. If you mishear the stress, you may miss the word in Listening; the guide to [word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) covers the suffix patterns behind these changes. For pairs that look alike, see [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

## Self-check: choose the correct form

Fill each gap with the correct form of the word in brackets.

1. There was a ______ increase in online shopping last year. (significance)
2. The report gives a detailed ______ of the survey results. (analyse)
3. Many factors ______ to climate change. (contribution)
4. The new rules were applied ______ across all schools. (consist)
5. Small cars are generally more ______ to run. (economy)
6. The museum was ______ by a group of local teachers. (establishment)

**Answers:** 1. *significant* (adjective before a noun). 2. *analysis* (noun after *a detailed*). 3. *contribute* (verb after the subject *factors*). 4. *consistently* (adverb describing *applied*). 5. *economical* (cheap to run, not related to the economy). 6. *established* (passive: *was established*).

If you got four or more right, move on to the next sublist. Fewer than four means you should add the other family members to your cards before learning new headwords.

## Mistakes to avoid with academic vocabulary

- **Using AWL words everywhere.** "I utilise public transport to reach my workplace" sounds stiff in [Speaking Part 1](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-tips/). Save formal words for Writing and Speaking Part 3.
- **Learning translations only.** One-word translations hide grammar such as *consist of* or *be subject to*.
- **Overloading sentences.** A sentence with six academic words is not automatically better. Clarity first; vocabulary second. For a natural academic tone, also look at [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/).
- **Ignoring the general list.** Without the everyday words around them, AWL words cannot be used accurately.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many words are in the Academic Word List?

The AWL contains 570 word families in 10 sublists. Because each family includes several forms (noun, verb, adjective, adverb), the total number of individual words is much larger than 570.

### Do I need the AWL for IELTS General Training?

It helps, but it is not the top priority. General Training texts focus on everyday and workplace English, so high-frequency vocabulary matters more. The first few sublists are still useful for Writing Task 2.

### How long does it take to learn the whole AWL?

At 8–12 families a day with regular reviews, most learners can work through the list in two to three months. Being able to use the words in writing usually takes longer than recognising them.

### Is the AWL still relevant if it was published in 2000?

Yes. Core academic vocabulary changes slowly, and words like *analyse*, *evidence* and *approach* remain central to academic English. Newer lists exist, notably the New Academic Word List (Browne and colleagues, 2013) and the Academic Vocabulary List (Gardner and Davies, 2014), which were built from larger, more recent corpora. They overlap heavily with the AWL, so the AWL remains a sound and widely used starting point, especially for exam preparation.
