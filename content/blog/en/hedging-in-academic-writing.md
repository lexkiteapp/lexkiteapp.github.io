Compare two sentences: *Social media causes depression in teenagers* and *Heavy social media use may contribute to depression in some teenagers.* The first claims more than any single study could prove. The second is careful, precise and much harder to attack. That difference is hedging in academic writing: using language that shows exactly how certain you are about a claim.

Hedging is not about sounding unsure. It is about matching the strength of your words to the strength of your evidence. Used well, it makes your writing more credible; used badly, it makes it vague. This guide shows the main tools, when to use them, when to avoid them, and how to edit an over-confident paragraph.

## Why academic writers hedge

Academic writing deals with claims that rest on evidence, and evidence is almost never complete. A survey covers some people, not all. A trend appears in one country, maybe not in others. If you write *always, all, prove, definitely*, a careful reader immediately thinks of an exception, and your argument loses authority.

Hedging does three jobs:

1. **Accuracy.** It states the claim at the level your evidence supports.
2. **Protection.** A reader cannot dismiss your point with one counterexample.
3. **Politeness.** It acknowledges that other researchers may see things differently, which is the expected tone in academic discussion.

### Where learners go wrong

Writing traditions differ. In some, a strong, unqualified statement signals confidence and authority, and learners carry that habit into English: *Technology destroys family relationships.* In others, writers soften almost every sentence, and the essay ends up with no clear position at all. Learners translating from their first language also tend to use *maybe* at the start of sentences, which sounds conversational in formal English.

Both extremes cost marks in exams like IELTS, where a clear position and precise language are expected. The goal is a confident **thesis** supported by carefully hedged **claims**.

## The toolkit for hedging in academic writing

### Modal verbs: a scale of certainty

| Certainty | Example |
|---|---|
| Very high | Rising sea levels **will** affect coastal cities. |
| High | Remote work **is likely to** remain popular. |
| Medium | Longer school days **may** improve results. |
| Lower | The policy **might** reduce traffic in some areas. |
| Possibility | Such measures **could** help, if properly funded. |

*May* and *might* are the workhorses. *Could* often introduces a possibility or a suggestion. *Would* softens an opinion: *It would seem that…*, *I would argue that…*.

### Cautious verbs and reporting verbs

Instead of *show* or *prove*, academic writers often choose verbs that leave room for interpretation:

- *suggest*: The data **suggest** a link between sleep and memory.
- *indicate*: The results **indicate** a gradual decline.
- *appear / seem*: Younger users **appear** to prefer short videos.
- *tend to*: People **tend to** overestimate their own driving skills.

Reporting verbs also carry different strengths: *claim* and *argue* suggest the idea is open to debate; *demonstrate* and *establish* signal strong support. Choose deliberately.

### Adverbs and phrases that limit the claim

- **Frequency and degree:** *often, generally, usually, in most cases, to some extent, largely*.
- **Probability:** *probably, possibly, arguably, presumably*.
- **Scope:** *in many developing countries, among younger users, in the short term, according to recent data*.

Scope phrases are underused by learners and very effective. *Cars cause pollution* becomes *In large cities, private cars are a major source of air pollution.* The claim is now specific and defensible.

### Hedging nouns and introductory phrases

*There is some evidence that…*, *One possible explanation is…*, *It is possible that…*, *This may be due to…*. Use these sparingly; they add words, so they work best when the claim really is uncertain.

### Impersonal and conditional hedges

Two more structures let you distance yourself from a claim without sounding unsure:

- **Impersonal reporting:** *It is widely believed that…*, *Remote work **is thought to** reduce commuting costs*, *X **is said to**…*. These attribute the view to a wider group rather than to you.
- **Conditions:** *If current trends continue, demand **is likely to** double.* *Under certain conditions…* A condition states exactly when your claim holds, which is often more precise than a modal alone.

### Boosters: the other side of the scale

The linguist Ken Hyland, whose research on academic writing popularised the terms, pairs hedges with **boosters**: words that *increase* commitment, such as *clearly, undoubtedly, it is evident that, demonstrate, certainly*. Good academic writing uses both. Boost what your evidence firmly supports (*The data **clearly** show a decline after 2015*), hedge what goes beyond it (*This **may** reflect changes in funding*). A paragraph that only hedges sounds evasive; one that only boosts sounds like an advertisement.

## Before and after: editing an over-confident paragraph

**Before**

> Online learning is better than classroom learning. Students learn faster at home because there are no distractions. This proves that schools are no longer necessary, and all universities will move online in the future.

Problems: *better* with no criteria, a causal claim with no support, *proves*, *no longer necessary*, *all*, *will*.

**After**

> Online learning **can offer** clear advantages over classroom teaching, **particularly for adult learners** who need flexible schedules. **Some** students **appear to** progress faster at home, **possibly because** they can learn at their own pace. **However**, this **does not necessarily mean** that schools are unnecessary, and **it seems likely that** most universities will **combine** online and face-to-face teaching **rather than** abandon campuses entirely.

The position is still clear: online learning has real advantages, but it will not replace campuses. The difference is that each claim now fits what a reasonable writer could support.

## When not to hedge

Over-hedging is just as damaging as over-confidence. *It could perhaps be possibly argued that some people might sometimes…* says nothing.

Do **not** hedge:

- **Your thesis or position.** In IELTS Task 2, the question often asks for your opinion. State it clearly: *I strongly agree that…* or *I believe the advantages outweigh the disadvantages.*
- **Facts that are well established.** *Water boils at 100°C at sea level.* No hedge needed.
- **Data you are describing directly.** In IELTS Task 1, *Sales rose from 20 to 45 units* is a fact on the chart. You can hedge interpretations (*This was probably due to…*), but only if the task allows comment, and Academic Task 1 generally does not ask for causes.
- **Twice in one claim.** *may possibly*, *might perhaps* is double hedging. One hedge per claim is usually enough.

Here is the same idea at three strengths, so you can feel the difference:

| Too strong | Too weak | About right |
|---|---|---|
| Fast food causes obesity. | It could perhaps be said that fast food might possibly be linked to weight in some ways. | Regular fast food consumption is likely to contribute to obesity. |
| Tourism destroys local culture. | Maybe tourism may have some effects on culture sometimes. | Mass tourism can weaken some local traditions, particularly in small communities. |

The right-hand column keeps a clear message and adds exactly one or two limits.

### A quick decision guide

1. Is it your main position? → State it clearly.
2. Is it an established fact or a number from the data? → No hedge.
3. Is it a generalisation about people, causes or the future? → Hedge once.
4. Is it a generalisation with an obvious exception? → Add a scope phrase.

## Hedging in IELTS and other exams

In IELTS Writing Task 2, appropriate hedging contributes to Lexical Resource (precise word choice) and Task Response (a well-developed, credible argument). In Task 1, hedging appears mainly in approximations: *just over half, roughly a third, approximately 200 units*. Our guide to [IELTS Writing Task 1 line graphs](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) shows how to use approximation language accurately.

For essay structure, see [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/); a well-built body paragraph moves from a clear topic sentence to hedged supporting claims. Hedging also works with contrast and concession, so pair this guide with [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/). Many hedging words, such as *indicate, significant, approximately*, are on the [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/).

In university assignments and research papers the same principles apply, with one addition: hedges are often attached to citations (*Previous studies suggest that…*).

## Self-check

Rewrite or judge each sentence.

1. *Video games make children violent.* (Hedge it.)
2. *Maybe the government should invest more in public transport.* (Make it more academic.)
3. *The chart shows that sales might possibly have increased to 50 units.* (What's wrong?)
4. *I may perhaps agree with this statement.* (Fix it for a Task 2 thesis.)
5. *All students prefer online exams.* (Add a scope limit.)

### Answers and explanations

1. *Some research suggests that violent video games may increase aggressive behaviour in some children.* A cautious verb, a modal and a scope limit.
2. *Arguably, the government should invest more in public transport* or *It would be sensible for the government to invest more in public transport.* *Maybe* is informal in academic writing.
3. Data on the chart is a fact; there is also a double hedge. Write: *Sales increased to 50 units.*
4. Your position must be clear: *I largely agree with this statement* or *I agree with this statement to a large extent.*
5. *Many students, particularly those who work part-time, prefer online exams.*

## Frequently asked questions

### Does hedging make my writing weaker?

No, if you hedge claims and not your position. A clear thesis supported by carefully limited claims reads as more expert, not less. Weakness comes from hedging everything, including your own opinion.

### Is "maybe" wrong in IELTS Writing?

It is not wrong, but it is informal and often appears at the start of a sentence in a way that sounds spoken. *Perhaps, possibly* or a modal verb such as *may* usually fit academic style better.

### Do I need hedging in IELTS Speaking?

Light hedging helps in Part 3, where you discuss abstract topics: *I'd say…*, *It probably depends on…*, *That's generally true, but…*. It makes your answers sound thoughtful. Do not overuse it in [IELTS Speaking Part 1](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-tips/), where simple, direct answers are expected.

### How many hedges should one essay have?

There is no fixed number. Check each claim instead: hedge generalisations, causes and predictions once; leave your thesis, facts and data unhedged.
