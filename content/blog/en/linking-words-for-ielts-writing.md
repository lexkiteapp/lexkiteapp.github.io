The best linking words for IELTS Writing are the ones that show a real logical relationship, such as contrast (*however*, *whereas*), cause (*because of*, *as a result*) or addition (*moreover*, *in addition*), used accurately and only where the reader needs a signal. Examiners do not count connectors. Under Coherence and Cohesion they judge whether your ideas progress logically and whether cohesive devices are used appropriately; too many, or the wrong ones, can lower that score just as much as too few.

So the goal is not a longer list. It is choosing the right word for the relationship, punctuating it correctly and, often, linking ideas without a connector at all. This guide covers all three, with an exercise at the end.

## How linking words affect Coherence and Cohesion

Coherence and Cohesion is one of the four criteria in both Writing tasks, alongside Task Achievement or Task Response, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Each counts equally towards the task score.

**Coherence** is about the logic of the whole text: a clear position, paragraphs with one central idea each, and a sensible order of points. **Cohesion** is about the language that ties sentences together: linking words, pronouns (*this*, *these*, *it*), synonyms and repeated key nouns, and phrases that refer back (*this trend*, *such policies*).

The public band descriptors draw a clear line. At band 6, cohesive devices are used effectively, but cohesion within or between sentences may be faulty or mechanical. At band 7, a range of cohesive devices is used appropriately, with some under- or over-use. Band 8 manages all aspects of cohesion well, and at band 9 cohesion attracts no attention at all. In other words, a band 8 essay does not *look* full of connectors; it simply reads easily.

## Linking words for IELTS Writing by function

Group connectors by what they do, not alphabetically. Notice the grammar column: some words join two clauses inside one sentence; others start a new sentence.

| Function | Joins clauses in one sentence | Starts or links a new sentence | Followed by a noun phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addition | *and*, *not only … but also* | *Moreover,* *In addition,* *Furthermore,* | *in addition to*, *as well as* |
| Contrast | *but*, *whereas*, *while*, *although* | *However,* *In contrast,* *On the other hand,* *Nevertheless,* | *despite*, *in spite of* |
| Cause | *because*, *since*, *as* | – | *because of*, *due to*, *owing to* |
| Result | *so*, *so … that* | *As a result,* *Consequently,* *Therefore,* | *as a result of* |
| Example | – | *For example,* *For instance,* | *such as*, *including* |
| Concession | *although*, *even though* | *Admittedly,* *Of course,* | *despite*, *regardless of* |
| Purpose | *so that*, *in order to* | – | *for the purpose of* |
| Conclusion | – | *In conclusion,* *Overall,* (Task 1) | – |

Three grammar points cause most errors:

- *Despite* and *in spite of* take a noun or *-ing* form: *Despite the high cost, …* / *Despite being expensive, …* Never *despite it is expensive*.
- *However* is not a conjunction. Write *Cars are convenient. However, they pollute.* or *Cars are convenient; however, they pollute.* Not *Cars are convenient, however they pollute.*
- *Although* and *but* do not go together. *Although it is cheap, but it is slow* is a very common error among speakers of Chinese, Vietnamese, Hindi and Urdu, whose languages often pair the two. Use one or the other.

## Five ways linking words get overused

**1. One connector per sentence.** Starting every sentence with *Moreover*, *Furthermore*, *In addition*, *Besides* makes the essay sound mechanical, which is exactly what the descriptors warn against. Many sentences need no connector because the order is already clear.

**2. The wrong relationship.** *Moreover* adds a point in the same direction. Using it before a contrasting idea confuses the reader. Likewise, *On the other hand* introduces the other side of an argument, not just any new point.

**3. Spoken or informal connectors.** *Besides that*, *Plus*, *Also,* at the start of a sentence and *Last but not least* sound conversational in an academic essay. They are fine in Speaking, less so in Task 2.

**4. Overly grand phrases.** *It is undeniable that*, *Every coin has two sides* and *In this day and age* add words without adding meaning. Examiners recognise memorised templates.

**5. "Firstly, secondly, thirdly" as the whole structure.** Ordinal markers are acceptable, but if they are your only cohesive devices, paragraphs read like a list rather than an argument.

## Connecting ideas without linking words

Strong writers rely heavily on referencing and lexical chains. Look at the difference.

> **Overloaded:** *Firstly, many cities are building more roads. Moreover, this is because traffic is increasing. However, more roads attract more cars. Therefore, traffic does not improve. In addition, building roads is expensive.*

> **Natural:** *Many cities respond to congestion by building more roads. This approach rarely works for long, because new lanes tend to attract additional drivers. Within a few years, traffic returns to its previous level, and the city has spent a large sum with little to show for it.*

The second version uses only one connector (*because*). Cohesion comes from:

- **Reference words:** *This approach* points back to building roads.
- **Synonyms and word families:** *congestion* → *traffic*; *roads* → *new lanes*.
- **Time and sequence phrases:** *Within a few years* shows progression without *Firstly* or *Then*.
- **Logical sentence order:** cause, then effect, then consequence.

Reference words need care, though. A vague *this* or *it* can make a reader stop and search for what you mean. Compare *Some parents limit screen time, while others do not. This can affect sleep.* (Which one affects sleep: limiting it, or not limiting it?) with *Some parents limit screen time, while others do not. Unlimited use can affect children's sleep.* When the previous sentence contains two possible ideas, add a noun after *this*: *this restriction*, *this lack of control*. Learners whose first language often drops subjects or objects, such as Japanese, Korean or Chinese speakers, sometimes leave out the reference word entirely; check that every sentence makes clear who or what you are talking about.

Hedging language also helps an argument flow while staying careful; see [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) for phrases like *tends to* and *is likely to*.

## Linking words in Task 1 vs Task 2

In Task 1 the relationships are mostly comparison and sequence: *while*, *whereas*, *compared with*, *followed by*, *by contrast*, *over the same period*. A clear overview usually starts with *Overall,*. Avoid *In conclusion* in Task 1, since you are summarising data, not concluding an argument. The [line graph guide](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/) shows these phrases in context.

In Task 2 you need the full range: concession, cause and effect, examples and a conclusion. Each body paragraph should open with a clear topic sentence, and connectors should mark the key turns in the argument, not every sentence. The paragraph plan in [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/) shows where those turns usually fall.

## Self-check: fix the linking

Each sentence has a problem with its linking word. Correct it.

1. Although public transport is cheap, but many people prefer to drive.
2. Despite the government spends more on health, waiting times are still long.
3. Online learning is flexible. Moreover, it can feel isolating for some students.
4. Prices rose sharply, however, demand stayed high.
5. Many young people move to cities because of they want better jobs.
6. In conclusion, the chart shows that sales of bicycles increased.

**Answers:**
1. Remove *but*: *Although public transport is cheap, many people prefer to drive.*
2. Use a noun phrase or a clause connector: *Despite higher government spending on health, …* or *Although the government spends more on health, …*
3. Wrong relationship: the second idea contrasts with the first. Use *However,*.
4. Punctuation: *Prices rose sharply; however, demand stayed high.* or two sentences.
5. *Because of* needs a noun phrase. Use *because they want better jobs*.
6. Task 1 needs an overview, not a conclusion: *Overall, bicycle sales increased.*

If you missed two or more, review the grammar column of the table above before your next essay.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many linking words should a Task 2 essay have?

There is no target number. Use a connector when the relationship between two ideas is not already obvious. In many strong essays, connectors appear at the start of only some sentences, while reference words and synonyms do the rest of the work.

### Will "firstly, secondly, finally" lower my score?

Not by themselves. They become a problem when they are the only cohesive devices you use, or when they replace genuine logical links. Mix them with topic sentences and referencing.

### What is the difference between "however" and "although"?

*Although* joins two clauses in one sentence (*Although it is expensive, it is popular*). *However* links to the previous sentence and is followed by a comma (*It is expensive. However, it is popular*). They express similar contrast but need different punctuation and structure.

### Do I need linking words in Task 1 as well?

Yes, but different ones. Task 1 relies mostly on comparison and time sequence words, plus *Overall* for the overview. Argument connectors like *Consequently* or *In conclusion* are rarely needed.
