The affect vs effect meaning question has a short answer: **affect is usually a verb meaning "to influence", and effect is usually a noun meaning "a result"**. *The weather affects our mood. The weather has an effect on our mood.* Same idea, two different jobs. That one rule is right in roughly nine uses out of ten, and the rest of this guide covers the other one, the two exceptions you will actually meet, and the phrases that learners most often get wrong in emails, essays and exams.

If you only need the quick version, jump to the diagram below. If you write in English for study or work, keep reading: the exceptions are exactly where confident writers slip.

## Affect vs effect meaning: the core rule

Think of a cause and a consequence. The cause *affects* something; the consequence *is* the effect.

{{diagram: d1 | The two words sound almost the same, but they do different jobs in a sentence. Check the job first, then the spelling.}}

- *Lack of sleep **affects** concentration.* (verb, present simple)
- *Lack of sleep has a serious **effect** on concentration.* (noun, after *a serious*)
- *The new tax will **affect** small businesses.*
- *The new tax will have a **big effect** on small businesses.*

You can often convert one into the other, which is a useful way to check your choice: **affect** (verb) ⇄ **have an effect on** (noun phrase). *It affected me* = *It had an effect on me.* If you can make the conversion, you have the right pair.

### Why the spelling trips people up

Both words begin with an unstressed vowel, so in fast speech they sound nearly identical: /əˈfekt/ and /ɪˈfekt/. Many speakers pronounce them the same, which is why the mistake is so common even among native speakers. Because you cannot rely on your ears, rely on the sentence: look at the job the word does.

A memory aid some teachers use is the acronym **RAVEN**: *Remember, Affect is a Verb, Effect is a Noun.* It does not cover the exceptions, but it fixes most everyday errors.

## Three quick tests before you write

{{diagram: d2 | Three checks you can run in a few seconds on any sentence with affect or effect.}}

1. **The "the/an" test.** Can you put *the*, *an*, *this*, or an adjective like *positive* before the word? Then it is a noun, so it is probably **effect**. *The effect was immediate. A lasting effect. This effect is small.*
2. **The "influence" test.** Can you replace the word with *influence* (or *change*, *have an impact on*)? Then it is probably **affect**. *The delay will affect/influence our plans.*
3. **The grammar test.** After *to*, *will*, *may*, *can*, *does* you need a verb. After *an*, *the*, *any*, *no*, *little*, *side* you need a noun. *Will it affect us? Is there any effect?*

## Affect as a verb: meanings and patterns

The main meaning is "to have an influence on someone or something". It is a transitive verb, so it needs an object: you affect *something*.

- *The strike affected thousands of passengers.*
- *Humidity can affect the quality of the paint.*
- *Her words clearly affected him.* (They also touched him emotionally.)
- *Allergies affect about one in five people in some regions.* (Be careful with real statistics: only quote a number if you have the source.)

A second, rarer meaning is **to pretend or put on a feeling or style**, usually in a formal, slightly critical way: *He affected an air of boredom.* You will meet this mainly in literary writing. The related adjective *affected* means "artificial, not natural": *an affected accent*.

### Passive and adjective forms

*Affected* is often used in the passive and as an adjective: *The areas affected by the flood are still closed.* *Families affected by the closure will receive help.* Notice that you say *affected **by*** something and *the effect **of** X **on** Y*. Many errors are really preposition errors: *the effect of exercise **on** sleep*, not *the effect of exercise to sleep*. For more on this kind of pattern, see our guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/).

## Effect as a noun: meanings and collocations

The main meaning is "a change that is a result of something else". Because it is a noun, it can take articles, adjectives and plurals.

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| have an effect on | *The new law will have an effect on rents.* |
| adjective + effect | *a positive / negative / lasting / immediate / harmful effect* |
| side effect | *The tablets can cause drowsiness as a side effect.* |
| take effect | *The new rules take effect on 1 January.* |
| come into effect | *The ban comes into effect next month.* |
| in effect | *In effect, the two companies are now one.* (meaning "in practice") |
| to that effect | *She said something to that effect.* (roughly that) |
| special effects | *The film's special effects won an award.* |
| cause and effect | *There is a clear cause and effect relationship.* |

*Effects* in the plural can also mean "belongings", as in *personal effects*, a formal term used for the things a person carries or owns.

Be careful with two common phrases. People say *to good effect* ("with a good result") and *to no effect* ("without success"). And *the effect of X on Y* names the direction of influence: X is the cause, Y is what changes.

## The exceptions: affect as a noun, effect as a verb

You can write for years without needing these, but they appear in exams and in academic reading, so it is worth knowing them.

### Effect as a verb: "to bring about"

When *effect* is a verb it does not mean "influence". It means "to make something happen", and the object is nearly always a change, such as *change*, *reform*, *a transition*, *a cure*, *an escape*.

- *The new CEO wants to **effect** major changes in how the team works.*
- *The prisoners effected their escape through a drain.*

Compare: *The reforms **affected** the economy* (they influenced it) and *The government **effected** the reforms* (it brought them about). If you are unsure, use *bring about* or *make*, which are clear to everyone.

### Affect as a noun: a psychology term

In psychology and medicine, *affect* (stress on the first syllable: /ˈæfekt/) means a person's observable emotional expression or mood. You will see it in phrases like *flat affect* or *positive affect*. Unless you study or work in that field, you will rarely need to write it. If you meet it, the stress pattern is your clue that you are reading a technical term.

## Typical mistakes and how to fix them

| Mistake | Better |
|---|---|
| *The pollution effects the river.* | *The pollution **affects** the river.* |
| *Pollution has a bad affect on the river.* | *Pollution has a bad **effect** on the river.* |
| *The effect to health is serious.* | *The effect **on** health is serious.* |
| *It will not effect me.* | *It will not **affect** me.* |
| *Take affect from Monday.* | *Take **effect** from Monday.* |

Two other pairs deserve a note because they travel together with this one. *Impact* is often used instead of both words in formal writing (*have an impact on*, *impact on*), and it is a sensible alternative when you are not sure. And *influence* works as both noun and verb: *a strong influence on children*, *to influence a decision*. For a wider list of look-alike pairs, our guide to [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/) covers the others learners meet most.

## Using affect and effect in academic writing

In essays and reports, cause-and-effect language is everywhere. Precise use of these words is a small but visible sign of control, and it matters in tests that mark vocabulary accuracy, like [IELTS Writing Task 2](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/).

Good patterns:

- *Rising prices **affect** low-income families disproportionately.*
- *Social media has had a **significant effect on** how young people communicate.*
- *One **effect of** remote work has been a decline in commuting.*
- *Researchers examined the **effects of** sleep on memory.*

When you write about causes, mix the structures rather than repeating one word: *affects*, *has an effect on*, *influences*, *has an impact on*, *leads to*, *results in*. Careful writers also soften claims that are not certain: *may affect*, *is likely to have an effect*. Our guide to [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) shows how.

## Learn them in context, not as a pair

Learners often memorise the rule, then forget it under time pressure. A more reliable way is to store each word with its typical neighbours: *affect + noun* (affect health, affect the outcome), *have an effect on + noun*, *side effect*, *take effect*. Short chunks are quicker to recall than abstract rules, and they carry the right preposition with them. This is the same idea behind [active recall for vocabulary](/en/blog/active-recall-for-vocabulary/): cover the word, produce it from the sentence, then check.

## Self-check: affect or effect?

1. The new schedule will ____ everyone in the office.
2. The film's special ____ were created by a small team.
3. What is the ____ of caffeine on sleep?
4. Stress can badly ____ your health.
5. The law comes into ____ in March.
6. The manager hopes to ____ a change in culture. (Formal: bring about.)
7. The medicine had no side ____.
8. How does noise ____ children's learning?

### Answers

1. **affect.** Verb after *will*, meaning "influence".
2. **effects.** Noun after *special*; *special effects* is a fixed phrase.
3. **effect.** Noun after *the*; the pattern is *the effect of X on Y*.
4. **affect.** Verb after *can badly*.
5. **effect.** *Come into effect* is a fixed phrase.
6. **effect.** Formal verb meaning "bring about", with *change* as its object.
7. **effects.** Noun after *no side*; the plural form is also correct here.
8. **affect.** Verb after *does ... noise*, meaning "influence".

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between affect and effect?

Affect is mostly a verb meaning "to influence", and effect is mostly a noun meaning "the result of a change". *The storm affected the flights. The storm had an effect on the flights.*

### Can effect be a verb?

Yes, but it is rare and formal. *To effect change* means to bring change about. If you want to say that something influenced something else, use affect.

### Is "affect" ever a noun?

Rarely. In psychology, affect (stressed on the first syllable) means a person's emotional expression, as in *flat affect*. Outside that field, use mood, emotion or feeling.

### How do I remember which one to use?

Try the three tests above: if you can put *the* or *an* before it, use effect; if you can replace it with *influence*, use affect. The RAVEN memory aid (Affect is a Verb, Effect is a Noun) also covers most cases.
