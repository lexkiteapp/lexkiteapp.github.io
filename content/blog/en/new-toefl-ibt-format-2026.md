The **new TOEFL format 2026** took effect on January 21, 2026, and it is a different test from the one most prep books describe. The TOEFL iBT is now shorter (about 1.5 hours), adaptive (the difficulty responds to your answers), scored on a band scale of 1–6, and built around new task types such as Complete the Words, Read in Daily Life and Build a Sentence. If you are studying with older materials, your vocabulary and general skills still count, but your knowledge of the test structure and your timing strategy need an update.

## What changed in the new TOEFL format 2026

Three changes matter most for test takers.

### 1. A shorter test

The four sections (Reading, Listening, Writing and Speaking) now take about 1.5 hours in total. A shorter test means each task carries more weight; a few minutes of lost focus has a bigger effect than it used to.

### 2. Adaptive difficulty

The test adjusts to your performance: strong answers early on can lead to harder material later. As a result, the exact number of questions and the time can vary slightly from one test taker to another.

The practical consequence: don't treat the first tasks as a warm-up. Every answer helps decide what comes next.

### 3. A 1–6 band scale

Each section and the overall score are reported on a scale from 1 to 6 in half-band steps, and the overall score is the average of the four sections. During a transition period, score reports also show an equivalent score on the familiar 0–120 scale.

This matters if you are applying to a university or organization whose requirement is still written as a 0–120 number. Ask them directly which scores they accept and how they interpret the new scale.

## Test structure at a glance

The figures below come from the reference structure used in the Lexkite TOEFL module. Because the test is adaptive, treat them as approximate.

| Section | Questions (approx.) | Time (approx.) | Task types |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading | 50 | 30 min | Complete the Words, Read in Daily Life, Read an Academic Passage |
| Listening | 47 | 29 min | Listen and Choose a Response, Listen to a Conversation, Listen to an Announcement, Listen to an Academic Talk |
| Writing | 12 | 23 min | Build a Sentence, Write an Email, Write for an Academic Discussion |
| Speaking | 11 | 8 min | Listen and Repeat, Take an Interview |

That makes 12 task types in total. Here is what each section looks like and how to approach it.

## Reading: three task types, two of them new

**Complete the Words.** You read a short academic paragraph in which some words have their second half missing, and you type the missing letters. *Example (original):* *Bees commun___ the location of flowers through a ser___ of movements.* (Answers: *communicate*, *series*.) This task checks vocabulary, spelling and grammar at the same time. Read the whole sentence before filling a gap, because part of speech, tense and singular/plural usually decide the answer.

**Read in Daily Life.** Short everyday texts such as notices, emails, flyers and messages, followed by multiple-choice questions. This is a notable shift: the TOEFL is no longer only long academic passages.

**Read an Academic Passage.** An academic text with questions on details, negative facts, inferences, vocabulary in context and the author's purpose. Questions generally follow the order of the passage; for NOT/EXCEPT questions, eliminate the options that do appear in the text.

## Listening: short responses and academic talks

**Listen and Choose a Response.** You hear one short line and choose the most appropriate reply. It tests whether you understand the speaker's intention, not just the words.

**Listen to a Conversation** and **Listen to an Announcement.** Exchanges and notices set on a university campus or in a class.

**Listen to an Academic Talk.** A short lecture with questions about the main idea, details, purpose and inferences.

**Tip:** take brief notes and listen hard after signal words like *however*, *so* and *actually*, because key information often follows them. *Example (original):* *I was planning to take the geology course, but actually I've signed up for statistics instead.* A question about the speaker's choice points to *statistics*. Our guide to [improving English listening](/en/blog/how-to-improve-english-listening/) has routines that build this kind of attention.

## Writing: from sentence building to a discussion post

**Build a Sentence.** Arrange given phrases into a grammatically correct sentence. For instance, *the lab / until six / is open / on Fridays* becomes *The lab is open until six on Fridays.* You need a solid grasp of word order in statements and questions.

**Write an Email.** Respond to a situation in about 7 minutes, covering every point the task asks for. Test takers more often lose points by missing a required point than by using simple vocabulary. Our guide on [how to write a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/) covers openings, polite requests and closings.

**Write for an Academic Discussion.** Contribute a post to an online class discussion in about 10 minutes. Your post should add a **new** idea with a reason, not simply repeat what other students have said. Measured language (*this may suggest*, *in many cases*) makes academic claims more convincing; see [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/). Our [TOEFL Academic Discussion guide](/en/blog/toefl-academic-discussion-writing/) walks through a full model post.

## Speaking: short but demanding

**Listen and Repeat.** Hear a sentence and repeat it exactly, from short to longer sentences. This task rewards accuracy: dropping a word, changing a tense or missing a final sound such as /s/ or /t/ can cost you. Practicing with the [shadowing technique](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) is excellent preparation.

**Take an Interview.** Answer interview-style questions, about 45 seconds each. A good answer is direct, gives a reason and adds an example. A simple frame: answer, reason, example, wrap-up. *Example (original), on study habits:* *I usually study in the evening because the house is quiet then. For instance, last term I reviewed my notes for twenty minutes every night after dinner, and I felt far more prepared for exams.*

## Common mistakes when switching from old-format prep

Many test takers who prepared for the previous version carry over habits that no longer fit:

- **Pacing for long passages only.** The new Reading section mixes very short everyday texts with academic passages. Spending too long on a simple notice leaves less time for harder items.
- **Ignoring spelling.** In multiple-choice tests, recognizing a word was enough. In Complete the Words you must produce the letters, so *recieve* or *enviroment* costs you the item.
- **Writing long essays.** The writing tasks are short and timed. A focused email that covers every required point beats a long one that misses a point.
- **Paraphrasing in Listen and Repeat.** In most speaking tasks, rephrasing is a strength. Here, the task is exact repetition, so changing *is going to* into *will* counts as an error.
- **Skipping everyday vocabulary.** Words for schedules, fees, deadlines and campus services appear in daily-life texts and announcements, not just academic terms.

A quick diagnostic before you start: try one set of each new task type under time pressure and note which feels least familiar. That task deserves the first week of your study plan.

## How to prepare for the 2026 TOEFL iBT

1. **Drop old-format materials for test strategy.** Vocabulary and reading or listening skills still transfer, but the task types are different.
2. **Strengthen academic vocabulary and spelling.** Complete the Words and Build a Sentence punish spelling and word-order errors. The [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/) is a strong resource, and the [vocabulary page](/en/vocabulary/) has more sets by exam.
3. **Practice everyday texts too,** not only academic ones, for Read in Daily Life and Listen and Choose a Response.
4. **Write under time limits** for the Email and Academic Discussion tasks.
5. **Train exact repetition** for Listen and Repeat: record yourself and compare with the original.

### A four-week plan for switching to the new format

| Week | Focus | Daily work (about 45 minutes) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnose | One timed set of each of the 12 task types; log every miss by cause (spelling, word order, vocabulary, timing) |
| 2 | Production tasks | Complete the Words and Build a Sentence daily; spelling review of words you missed; Listen and Repeat with recordings |
| 3 | Integrated timing | Read in Daily Life plus Read an Academic Passage under time; one Write an Email (7 min) and one Academic Discussion post (10 min) |
| 4 | Full rehearsal | Two section-length practice runs; Take an Interview answers at 45 seconds; review only your mistake log |

Adjust the order to your diagnostic: if Week 1 shows Listening is your weakest section, swap Week 2 and Week 3 focus. The plan builds familiarity with the tasks; it does not guarantee any particular score.

## Frequently asked questions

### When did the new TOEFL iBT format start?
It applies from January 21, 2026. If your test date is after that, prepare for the new structure.

### How does the 1–6 band scale relate to the 0–120 scale?
During the transition period, score reports also show an equivalent on the 0–120 scale. For a specific requirement, ask the university or organization receiving your scores how they use them, and check official ETS information.

### Does the adaptive design make the test harder?
It adjusts difficulty to your answers, so test takers see different levels. The key is to perform well from the start, because earlier results influence later difficulty.

### How long is the new TOEFL iBT?

About 1.5 hours for all four sections. Because the test is adaptive, the exact number of questions and the time can vary slightly between test takers, so plan for the full duration and arrive with time to spare.

### Is the new format easier than the old one?

Not necessarily. It is shorter, but the adaptive design means strong performance leads to harder material, and the production tasks (Complete the Words, Build a Sentence, Listen and Repeat) leave little room for guessing. Many test takers find it more intense per minute rather than easier overall.

### What band score do I need?

That depends entirely on the university, employer or agency receiving your scores. Requirements may be written on the 1–6 scale, the 0–120 scale or both during the transition, so check the exact wording with each institution.

### Are old TOEFL prep books still useful?
Partly. Academic vocabulary and general reading and listening skills remain valuable. But the structure, task types and timing have changed, so you need practice with the new tasks as well.
