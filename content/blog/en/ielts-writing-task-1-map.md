An **IELTS Writing Task 1 map** question, which appears in the Academic test, shows two or more maps of the same place at different times, or a plan before and after development. You describe what changed, what stayed the same and where. There are no numbers, so the examiner looks closely at your location language, your choice of tense and how well you group the changes. This guide gives a method for comparing maps, a language bank for direction and change, and an original model report based on invented maps.

## What a map question tests

Map questions test the same four criteria as other Task 1 types: task achievement, coherence and cohesion, lexical resource, and grammatical range and accuracy. For a map, task achievement means that you identify the main changes and describe positions correctly. Cohesion depends on grouping: you should not describe every building in the order that you notice it. Lexical resource includes the precise words for places and change, and grammar covers tenses and passive forms.

To see how the four criteria turn into a band score, read [IELTS band scores explained](/en/blog/ielts-band-scores-explained/). If you are weaker at data tasks than at maps, compare with the [bar chart guide](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-bar-chart/).

## A five-step method for an IELTS Writing Task 1 map

1. **Read the title and dates (30 seconds).** Are both maps past, or is one a plan for the future? That decides your tenses.
2. **Find the compass (30 seconds).** Maps normally show north at the top. Use it to describe positions.
3. **Compare area by area (2 minutes).** Divide the map into north, centre, south or into zones such as the coast, the town centre and the outskirts. Mark each change.
4. **Decide your two groups (1 minute).** For example, new buildings and removed features; or changes in the north and changes in the south.
5. **Write (14 minutes) and check (2 minutes).**

## A model report

> The maps illustrate how a village changed between 2000 and 2020.
>
> Overall, the village became more developed over the period, with new housing, a supermarket and better school facilities replacing farmland and open fields, although the woodland to the east was preserved.
>
> In the north of the village, an area of farmland was converted into a large housing estate, which was connected to the centre by a new road. In the middle of the village, the small primary school was enlarged, and a playing field was added on its western side, while the main road continued to run through the centre without change.
>
> Further south, a supermarket was built beside the main road, close to the centre of the village. By contrast, the woodland in the east remained unchanged, although a cycle path was constructed along its edge, linking the housing estate with the school and the shops. No other part of the village appears to have been altered.

The report has about 157 words and uses only the information in the maps.

## Reading two maps side by side

Place your pencil on the same spot on both maps and ask three questions: what is different, what has moved, and what has not changed? Something can be **added** (a new supermarket), **removed** (farmland cleared), **replaced** (a car park becoming a garden), **extended** (a larger school) or **moved**. Naming the type of change before you write helps you pick the right verb. If a feature appears on only one map, check whether the other map really has nothing there, or whether you missed a small label.

Plans for the future need extra care. If the second map is labelled *proposed*, you must use future language and avoid saying that something *was* built. It is a small point, but tense errors in this task are common and easy to avoid.

## Why the report works

- **The overview** states the main trend (more development) and one exception (the woodland).
- **The details are grouped by area**: north and centre in one paragraph, south and east in another.
- **The tense is past**, because both maps show past years. Passive verbs (*was converted*, *was built*) fit because the agent is not important.
- **Location words are precise**: *in the north*, *on its western side*, *beside the main road*, *along its edge*.
- **There are no explanations that the maps do not show**, such as why the school grew.

## Direction and location language

| Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|
| Compass | *in the north, to the east of, in the south-west corner* |
| Position | *beside, next to, opposite, between, behind, in front of* |
| Distance | *close to, near, a short distance from, on the outskirts* |
| Relative | *at the centre, on the left of the map, at the top* |
| Along a line | *along the river, across the road, at the end of the street* |

Take care with *opposite*: it means on the other side of a road or river, not simply *next to*. The grammar lesson on [prepositions](/app/#/grammar/prepositions) and the blog guide to [prepositions in, on, at](/en/blog/prepositions-in-on-at/) help you avoid small errors.

## Tenses for maps

The tense depends on the dates:

| Situation | Tense |
|---|---|
| Two past dates (1990 and 2010) | Past simple, with *had been* only for something earlier than the first date |
| A past date and the present | Present perfect or past simple, depending on the wording |
| A present map and a future plan | Future forms: *will be built, is planned to, is expected to* |
| One map with no date | Present simple |

If both maps show past years, do not slip into the present simple halfway through. Read your final sentence to check.

## Common mistakes

1. **Describing buildings one by one.** Group the changes by area or by type.
2. **Forgetting the unchanged features.** Say what stayed the same; it shows full reading.
3. **Wrong prepositions of place.** *In the north of the village* is not *at the north of*.
4. **Inventing reasons.** *Because the population grew* is not on the map unless the question says it.
5. **Mixing tenses.** Check every verb against the dates.
6. **Copying words from labels without grammar.** Turn labels into sentences.

For the wider list of mistakes, see [IELTS writing mistakes that cost marks](/en/blog/ielts-writing-mistakes/); for variety in sentences, see [complex sentences for IELTS writing](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should an IELTS map report be?

At least 150 words. A report of 160 to 190 words is comfortable and still leaves time to check.

### Do I need to describe every change on the map?

You should cover all the important changes, but grouping them is better than listing each one. Minor details can be combined or left out.

### Which tense should I use?

Use the tense that matches the dates: past simple for two past dates, future forms for a plan, and present simple for a map with no dates.

### Can I use the passive voice?

Yes, and it is natural for describing constructions and changes: *was built*, *was converted*. Mix it with active verbs such as *remained* or *stayed*.
