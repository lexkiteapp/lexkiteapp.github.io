The **Cambridge C1 Advanced format** has four papers, Reading and Use of English, Writing, Listening and Speaking, and it tests English at CEFR level C1, which is the level often expected for university study and professional work. C1 Advanced was formerly known as Certificate in Advanced English, or CAE. It is demanding because every paper asks for precision: exact words, complex structures and a controlled tone. This guide describes each paper and part, the skills behind them and a preparation plan. Exam details can change, so check the current handbook on the official Cambridge English website before you plan your test date.

## The four papers of the Cambridge C1 Advanced format

{{diagram: d1 | Four papers; Reading and Use of English carries the largest share of the final result}}

Reading and Use of English takes about an hour and a half, Writing about an hour and a half, Listening about 40 minutes, and Speaking about 15 minutes with another candidate. Cambridge averages equally weighted scores for Reading, Use of English, Writing, Listening and Speaking, so the combined Reading and Use of English paper counts for about 40 per cent of the result. Results use the Cambridge English Scale (the C1 level is reported at roughly 180 to 199 points; check the handbook for current grade boundaries), and you can read about the CEFR levels in [CEFR levels explained](/en/blog/cefr-levels-explained/). If you are building toward this level from B2, the [B2 First format guide](/en/blog/cambridge-b2-first-format-guide/) is a useful comparison.

## Reading and Use of English: eight parts

{{diagram: d2 | Parts 1 to 4 test language, parts 5 to 8 test reading}}

**Part 1: multiple-choice cloze.** You choose the word that best fits each gap from four options. The task tests collocation, shades of meaning and phrasal verbs. At C1 level, the wrong options are often close in meaning, so the surrounding words decide the answer.

**Part 2: open cloze.** You write one word for each gap. The answers are mostly grammar words, such as prepositions, pronouns, auxiliaries and linkers, but occasionally a content word. Decide the word class first, and read the whole sentence.

**Part 3: word formation.** You change a given word to fit a gap. You may need a prefix, a suffix or both, and sometimes a plural or a negative form.

**Part 4: key word transformation.** You complete a second sentence so that it means the same as the first, using a given key word and between three and six words in total, including the key word. Grammar and spelling must be right. Original item: *"I regret not taking the job." (WISH) → "I wish I had taken the job."* Check that the key word is unchanged and the meaning matches.

**Part 5: long text with multiple choice.** You read a text of several hundred words and answer questions about opinion, attitude, purpose and detail. Wrong options often use language from the text but change the meaning.

**Part 6: cross-text multiple matching.** You read four short texts on the same topic and decide which writer agrees or disagrees with another. This part tests comparison and inference, not detail.

**Part 7: gapped text.** Paragraphs have been removed from a long text, and you choose the correct one for each gap. Pronouns, time words and topic shifts decide the answer.

**Part 8: multiple matching.** You match statements to sections of a long text or to several short texts. Quick scanning and careful reading of two or three candidates are both needed.

Because the grammar and vocabulary parts reward breadth, study [collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/), [hedging in academic writing](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) and [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

## Writing: two parts

**Part 1 is a compulsory essay.** You respond to a prompt on a general topic and use points that are given in the task, adding your own view. You are expected to evaluate ideas, give reasons and use a formal register.

**Part 2 offers a choice** of text types, such as a letter or email, a proposal, a report or a review. Each type has its own style, for example persuasive and formal for a proposal, neutral and structured for a report. The question tells you who the reader is, and you must adapt your language to that reader.

Answers are marked for content, communicative achievement, organisation and language. At C1, examiners look for a range of structures, precise vocabulary and a natural tone. For work on cohesion, see [linking words](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/) and [complex sentences](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/). Many of the same skills appear in the [IELTS opinion essay](/en/blog/ielts-opinion-essay/) guide.

## Listening: four parts

Recordings are played twice. Part 1 has three short extracts with multiple-choice questions, Part 2 is a monologue with sentence completion, Part 3 is a conversation or discussion with multiple choice, and Part 4 asks you to match statements to speakers in two tasks at once. Many questions test attitude, opinion and purpose, not just facts. Use the pause to read the questions and use the second hearing to check, not to start. For daily habits, see [how to improve English listening](/en/blog/how-to-improve-english-listening/).

## Speaking: four parts

You take the test with another candidate and two examiners. The parts include a conversation about yourself, a long turn comparing pictures, a collaborative task with your partner and a discussion. You are assessed on grammar and vocabulary, discourse management, pronunciation and interactive communication. Practice with a partner is essential because interaction counts. Try [shadowing](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) and [sentence stress](/en/blog/sentence-stress-in-english-speaking-guide/) work to improve natural rhythm.

## A twelve-week preparation plan

| Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Learn the format, take a full practice test, start an error log |
| 3–5 | Use of English: collocations, word formation, transformations |
| 6–8 | Reading parts 5 to 8; writing one essay and one other text type each week |
| 9–10 | Listening and Speaking with timing and a partner |
| 11 | Two full practice papers under test conditions |
| 12 | Review the error log and rest |

Keep your error log by part. A pattern will appear: for example, if you keep missing Part 7, work on cohesion clues before doing more tests.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long is the Cambridge C1 Advanced exam?

The four papers add up to roughly four hours in total, with Speaking taken separately in many centres. Check the current handbook for timings in your centre and test type.

### What is the difference between C1 Advanced and IELTS?

C1 Advanced is a certificate exam at one CEFR level, reported as a score and a grade, while IELTS reports a band across a wider range. Choose the exam that your university, employer or authority accepts.

### Is C1 Advanced the same as CAE?

Yes. CAE, Certificate in Advanced English, was the older name of the exam now called C1 Advanced.

### Which part is the hardest?

It depends on the candidate. Many find Part 4 key word transformations and Part 6 cross-text matching demanding because they need exact grammar and careful comparison. Your own practice results will show where you lose most marks.
