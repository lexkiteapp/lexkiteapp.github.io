You know every word in *What do you want to do?*, yet when a fluent speaker says it, you hear something like *wha-dya-wanna-do?* That is connected speech: the way English words join, shrink and change sound when they are spoken at natural speed. It is not lazy or slang. It is how the language works in every accent, from news presenters to university lecturers.

Connected speech follows a small number of predictable patterns. Once you know them, fast English stops sounding like noise, and your own speaking starts to flow.

## Why you understand the words but not the sentence

Most learners first meet English through text. Your brain stores each word as a clean, separate unit: *want* /wɒnt/, *to* /tuː/. In real speech, those units rarely appear in their dictionary form. *Want to* becomes /ˈwɒnə/; *to* becomes /tə/; the word boundary disappears.

English also leans towards **stress timing**: speakers tend to space stressed syllables at fairly regular intervals, and the unstressed syllables between them get squeezed. In *I'd LIKE to GO to the PARK*, the words *to, the* are compressed into tiny, quick sounds. If you are expecting full forms, you miss them, and then you lose track of the sentence while trying to work out what you missed.

This is especially hard for speakers of **syllable-timed** languages, such as Spanish, French, Italian, Turkish, Tagalog, Hindi or Yoruba, where every syllable gets roughly equal time. It is also hard for speakers of languages that avoid consonant clusters or final consonants, for example Japanese, Korean, Thai or Vietnamese, because English links often depend on final consonants moving onto the next word.

## The five processes of connected speech

### 1. Consonant-to-vowel linking

When a word ends in a consonant and the next begins with a vowel, the consonant moves across and starts the next syllable.

- *turn it off* → /tɜː.nɪ.tɒf/ (sounds like *tur-ni-toff*)
- *an apple* → /ə.næpl/
- *pick it up* → /pɪ.kɪ.tʌp/

Many learners insert a tiny pause or glottal stop before each vowel-initial word: *turn | it | off*. That makes speech sound choppy and slows you down.

When the same consonant ends one word and starts the next, English says it once, slightly longer, instead of twice: *bad dog* /bæd dɒɡ/ with one held /d/, *black cat*, *some milk*. Releasing both consonants separately (*bad-uh dog*) is a common giveaway of word-by-word speech.

### 2. Vowel-to-vowel linking

When one word ends in a vowel and the next begins with one, English slides between them with a light /j/ or /w/. Textbooks sometimes call this *intrusion*, because a sound appears that is not in the spelling.

- After /iː, aɪ, eɪ, ɔɪ/ → /j/: *I agree* /aɪ jəˈɡriː/, *the end* /ði jend/
- After /uː, əʊ, aʊ/ → /w/: *go out* /ɡəʊ waʊt/, *do it* /duː wɪt/

In non-rhotic British accents, a word ending in *r* in spelling links with /r/ before a vowel: *far away* /fɑː rəˈweɪ/, *four apples*. Many speakers even add an /r/ with no *r* in spelling, as in *law and order* /lɔː rən ˈɔːdə/. American speakers pronounce the *r* anyway, so the link happens naturally.

### 3. Elision: sounds that disappear

Some sounds, especially /t/ and /d/ between two consonants, are often dropped.

- *next day* /neks deɪ/
- *last night* /lɑːs naɪt/
- *facts* /fæks/, *hands* /hænz/
- *I don't know* → /aɪ dəʊn nəʊ/, often reduced further to /aɪ dənəʊ/

Weak vowels can disappear too: *camera* /ˈkæmrə/, *chocolate* /ˈtʃɒklət/, *interesting* /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/, *family* /ˈfæmli/.

### 4. Assimilation: sounds that change

A sound changes to become more like its neighbour, usually to save mouth movement.

- /n/ → /m/ before /p, b, m/: *ten boys* /tem bɔɪz/, *in person* /ɪm ˈpɜːsn/
- /t/ → /p/ or /k/: *that boy* /ðæp bɔɪ/, *that girl* /ðæk ɡɜːl/
- /d/ + /j/ → /dʒ/: *did you* /ˈdɪdʒu/, *would you* /ˈwʊdʒu/
- /t/ + /j/ → /tʃ/: *don't you* /ˈdəʊntʃu/, *what you* /ˈwɒtʃu/

The last two explain *Didja see it?* and *Whatcha doing?* in casual speech.

### 5. Weak forms of function words

Around 40 common grammar words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) have a **strong form**, used when stressed or at the end of a phrase, and a **weak form**, used most of the time.

| Word | Strong | Weak | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| can | /kæn/ | /kən/ | I can swim /aɪ kən swɪm/ |
| to | /tuː/ | /tə/ | going to work |
| of | /ɒv/ | /əv/, /ə/ | a cup of tea |
| and | /ænd/ | /ən/, /n/ | fish and chips |
| for | /fɔː/ | /fə/ | a gift for you |
| was | /wɒz/ | /wəz/ | It was late |
| them | /ðem/ | /ðəm/, /əm/ | Tell them |
| have | /hæv/ | /həv/, /əv/ | They must have left |

Notice the pattern: the weak vowel is almost always schwa /ə/. If you can hear and produce /ə/ comfortably, you are halfway to understanding fast English. Weak forms build directly on word stress; if that topic still feels shaky, read our guide to [word stress rules](/en/blog/english-word-stress-rules/) first.

### The five processes at a glance

| Process | What happens | Typical example | What to listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linking | Final consonant or a /j/, /w/, /r/ glide joins the next word | *turn it off*, *go out*, *far away* | Word boundaries that "move" |
| Elision | A sound drops out | *next day*, *I don't know* | Missing /t/ and /d/ in clusters |
| Assimilation | A sound changes towards its neighbour | *ten boys*, *did you* | /m/ for /n/, /dʒ/ and /tʃ/ across words |
| Weak forms | Function words shrink to schwa | *a cup of tea*, *I can swim* | /ə/ in *of, to, can, and, for* |
| Contractions | Auxiliaries fuse with the word before | *I'd*, *she's*, *should've* | Short /d/, /z/, /v/ endings that carry grammar |

Contractions are the written tip of connected speech: *should've* is the weak form /əv/ fused to *should*, which is why learners sometimes write the error *should of*.

## The American flap t

In General American English, a /t/ between vowels (and after /r/ before a vowel) usually becomes a quick tap of the tongue, close to a soft /d/: *water* /ˈwɔːt̬ɚ/, *better*, *city*, *party*. Across word boundaries it happens too: *get it* sounds like *ge-dit*, *a lot of* like *a lo-da*.

In many British accents, a /t/ at the end of a word or before a consonant is often replaced by a glottal stop: *not now* /nɒʔ naʊ/, *football* /ˈfʊʔbɔːl/.

You do not need to copy either feature. You do need to recognise them, because listening exams and real conversations use a range of accents.

## Should you learn connected speech for listening or for speaking?

**Recognise everything, produce the core.**

For listening, aim to recognise all five processes and both main accents. That is where most comprehension problems come from.

For speaking, focus on three things that improve fluency and clarity without sounding forced: consonant-to-vowel linking, weak forms of the most common function words, and avoiding pauses between every word. Forced reductions such as *gonna* and *wanna* in a formal presentation or exam answer can sound too casual. In speech they are very common, but in writing they belong only in informal messages.

A good speaker is not the one who reduces the most. It is the one whose stressed words are clear and whose unstressed words are light.

## A seven-step routine for any audio clip

Use a 20–40 second clip with a transcript.

1. **Listen once without the transcript.** Note what you understand.
2. **Listen again and write down** what you hear, gaps included.
3. **Compare with the transcript.** Circle every place where you missed or misheard something.
4. **Label each miss**: linking, elision, assimilation, weak form, flap/glottal t, or an unknown word.
5. **Listen to each circled section** several times, slowed down if possible, until you can hear the connected form.
6. **Dictate it again.** Write the whole clip from audio only.
7. **Say it aloud** along with the speaker. This is the [shadowing technique](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/), and it moves the patterns from your ears into your mouth.

Step 4 is the one most learners skip, and it is the most valuable. After a few clips you will see your personal pattern; many learners find that weak forms cause most of their misses.

## Self-check

Write how each phrase usually sounds in natural speech, then check.

1. *Give it to me.*
2. *Did you finish?*
3. *a lot of people* (American)
4. *She can come.*
5. *the next train*
6. *Go away!*

### Answers and explanations

1. /ˈɡɪvɪtəmi/: consonant-to-vowel linking (*give it*) and weak *to*.
2. /ˈdɪdʒə ˈfɪnɪʃ/: /d/ + /j/ becomes /dʒ/, and *you* weakens.
3. /əˈlɑːt̬ə ˈpiːpl/: flap t, plus *of* reduced to /ə/.
4. /ʃi kən ˈkʌm/: weak *can*. A strong /kæn/ here would sound like contrast or emphasis.
5. /ðə neks treɪn/: /t/ dropped between /s/ and /t/.
6. /ɡəʊ wəˈweɪ/: vowel-to-vowel linking with /w/.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need connected speech to get a high IELTS Speaking score?

You do not need slang reductions, but natural linking and weak forms help your fluency and pronunciation. Examiners assess features such as rhythm and how easily you can be understood. Speech with full forms and pauses between every word is clear but tiring to follow.

### Why do people say "gonna" and "wanna"?

They are connected speech forms of *going to* and *want to*, created by elision and assimilation. They are standard in casual speech across English-speaking countries. Use them in conversation if you like, but not in formal writing.

### Will connected speech help with TOEIC Listening?

Yes. TOEIC audio uses natural speed and several accents, so recognising weak forms and linking makes it easier to catch the key words in each statement or conversation.

### How long does it take to get used to connected speech?

It depends on how much focused listening you do. Many learners notice a clear difference after a few weeks of daily transcript work with short clips. Passive background listening alone is much slower.
