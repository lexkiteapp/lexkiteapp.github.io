A visit to the doctor is one of the hardest conversations to have in a second language. You are worried, the words are technical, and the person across the desk is busy. The good news is that **English phrases at the doctor** follow a short, predictable pattern: you book, you describe what is wrong, you answer a few questions, and you leave knowing what to do next. Learn about thirty phrases and the pattern becomes easy to follow, even when you do not understand every word.

This guide is for newcomers and travellers in the US, the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. It covers booking an appointment, describing symptoms, mentioning allergies, asking about medicine, and the questions the doctor is likely to ask. It is language help only: it is not medical advice, and for anything serious or urgent you should call the emergency number or go to a hospital straight away.

## Before the visit: words that change by country

Health systems use different words for the same places and people. Knowing the local word makes booking easier.

{{diagram: d1 | The same place or person has different names. If you are unsure, ask: "Do I need to see a GP or go to a clinic?"}}

Some quick notes. In the US and Canada, you often have a *family doctor* or *primary care doctor*, and for minor, same-day problems there are *walk-in clinics* and *urgent care* centres. In the UK, Australia and New Zealand, the first point of contact is usually a *GP*. Emergency numbers are 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia and 111 in New Zealand. In the UK, 111 is also the NHS non-emergency advice line. Always check your own local health service for how it works, because insurance and registration rules are different in each place.

## Booking an appointment: English phrases at the doctor's office

You will usually call or use an online form, and the receptionist asks a few standard questions.

**What you say**

- *I'd like to make an appointment, please.*
- *I'd like to see a doctor as soon as possible.*
- *Do you have anything available this week?*
- *Could I book for Thursday morning?*
- *I need to cancel / reschedule my appointment.*
- *It's my first visit. Do I need to register first?*

**What you may hear**

- *Are you a new or existing patient?*
- *Can I take your date of birth?*
- *What's the appointment for?*
- *Please arrive ten minutes early and bring your ID and insurance card* (or your health card).

*Make an appointment* is the set phrase, and learners often say *meet the doctor*, which sounds odd. If you are asked what the visit is for, a short answer is enough: *I have a bad cough*, *I need a check-up*, *I need a prescription refill*. You do not need to explain the whole story on the phone.

## Describing symptoms: the four-part pattern

Doctors ask four things almost every time: what is wrong, when it started, how bad it is, and what changes it. If you prepare each part, you can describe almost any problem.

{{diagram: d2 | A simple order for describing a symptom. You can write the four answers on your phone before you go.}}

### 1. What is wrong

Use *I have + a symptom* or *I feel + an adjective*.

- *I have a headache / a sore throat / a cough / a rash / a fever.*
- *I feel really tired / dizzy / sick / weak.*
- *My stomach hurts.* / *I have stomach pain.*
- *I think I have the flu.* (*Flu* is short for influenza.)

Notice the verb for pain. *Pain* is a noun, so you say *I have a pain in my chest* or use the verb *hurt*: *It hurts when I swallow.* A sentence like *It is pain when I swallow* is a very common learner error.

### 2. Since when

Use the present perfect with *since* or *for* for something that started in the past and is still true. *I've had a headache **since** yesterday.* *I've had a cough **for** three days.* If the problem is over, use the simple past: *I had a fever last night, but it's gone.* If you are not sure about the difference between these two tenses, see our guide to the [present perfect and past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/).

### 3. How bad is it

Doctors often ask for a number: *On a scale of one to ten, how bad is the pain?* You can answer *About a six*. Other useful words: *mild*, *moderate*, *severe*, *sharp*, *dull*, *burning*, *throbbing*, *constant*, *on and off*.

### 4. What changes it

*It gets worse when I lie down.* *It hurts when I swallow.* *It's better after I eat.* *Nothing helps.* This last part helps the doctor decide what to examine.

## Questions the doctor may ask

You do not need to understand every medical word if you recognise the question type.

- *What seems to be the problem?* / *What brings you in today?* (Why did you come?)
- *How long have you had this?*
- *Do you have a fever?* / *Have you had any vomiting?*
- *Are you taking any medication?* (Medicine you use regularly, including vitamins.)
- *Do you have any allergies?*
- *Is there a history of this in your family?*
- *Does it hurt when I press here?*
- *Take a deep breath in... and out.*

Some answers worth practising: *No, I'm not taking anything.* *Yes, I take a tablet for blood pressure every morning.* *I'm allergic to penicillin.* *I'm pregnant.* *Not that I know of.*

### Allergies and medicine: say it clearly

Always mention allergies at the start. The pattern is **allergic to + the substance**: *I'm allergic to penicillin / peanuts / latex.* Not *I have allergy with penicillin*. If you do not know the English name of a medicine or an ingredient, show the box or a photo on your phone.

## Asking about medicine and treatment

After the examination you might get a *prescription* (a written order for medicine) or advice to buy something from a pharmacy. The questions you should be ready to ask are simple.

- *How often should I take this?*
- *How long should I take it for?*
- *Should I take it with food?* / *Before or after meals?*
- *Are there any side effects?*
- *Can I drive or drink alcohol while I'm taking it?*
- *When should I come back if it doesn't get better?*
- *What should I do if it gets worse?*

Note that we **take** medicine, tablets and pills; we do not *drink* them unless they are liquid. The set answers you will hear are *twice a day*, *three times a day*, *every eight hours*, *once a day at bedtime*, *with food*, *on an empty stomach*. Writing the instructions down while the doctor speaks avoids mistakes later.

### When you do not understand

It is perfectly normal to ask for repetition. Doctors prefer that to a silent patient who nods and goes home confused.

- *Sorry, could you explain that in simpler words?*
- *Could you say that again, please? A little slower?*
- *What does that word mean?*
- *Could you write that down for me?*
- *Is there a leaflet or a website I can read?*
- *Can I bring an interpreter?* (Many clinics can arrange one; ask when you book.)

*Sorry, I don't understand anything* sounds distressed, whereas *Could you explain that in simpler words?* gets you the help you need politely.

## Common mistakes learners make at the doctor

1. **Saying "I have headache"** instead of *a headache*. Symptoms usually need an article: *a cough*, *a fever*, *a sore throat*. Uncountable ones do not: *I have diarrhoea* (British spelling) or *diarrhea* (American).
2. **"I am feeling not good in my body."** Keep it simple: *I don't feel well.*
3. **"My head is pain."** Use *My head hurts* or *I have a headache.*
4. **"Drink the medicine."** For tablets say *take*.
5. **Mixing up *should*, *must* and *can* in advice.** *You should rest* is advice; *You must come back* is stronger. Our [guide to modal verbs](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/) shows the difference.
6. **Hiding embarrassing symptoms.** Doctors hear everything, and clear words are better than hints. *I have a problem with my stomach* is enough to begin.

## Self-check

1. Fix this sentence: *I have fever from two days.*
2. Which verb goes with medicine: *take* or *drink*?
3. Complete: *I'm allergic ___ penicillin.*
4. How do you politely ask a doctor to speak more simply?
5. The doctor asks *On a scale of one to ten?* What kind of answer do they want?
6. What is the difference between a *GP* and an *ER* (or *A&E*)?

### Answers

1. **I've had a fever for two days.** Use the present perfect with *for* and the article *a*.
2. **take.** We take tablets, pills and medicine; *drink* is for liquids.
3. **to.** *Allergic to* + substance.
4. **Could you explain that in simpler words, please?**
5. **A number**, such as *about a six*, to describe how severe the pain is.
6. A **GP** (family doctor) handles everyday health problems, while **ER/A&E** is the hospital department for emergencies that cannot wait.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I say first when I see the doctor?

Start with the main problem in one sentence, then add the time: *I've had a bad cough for a week.* The doctor will ask follow-up questions, so you do not need to explain everything at once.

### How do I say I am allergic to something?

Use *I'm allergic to* and the name: *I'm allergic to penicillin.* If you do not know the English word, write it on your phone or show the packaging.

### How can I understand medical words?

Ask *What does that mean?* and request simpler words or a written note. Many clinics also have leaflets or interpreters on request.

### Is this guide medical advice?

No. It is only language help for appointments. For symptoms that worry you, contact a doctor or the local emergency number.
