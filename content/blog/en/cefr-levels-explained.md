**CEFR levels** are the six-step scale used across the world to describe how well someone uses a foreign language: **A1 and A2** (basic user), **B1 and B2** (independent user), **C1 and C2** (proficient user). The scale comes from the *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages*, published by the Council of Europe in 2001 and updated with a Companion Volume in 2020. Schools, employers, visa systems and exam boards use it because it describes what you can **do** with the language, not just how many grammar rules you know.

This guide explains each level in plain terms, shows how the levels line up with IELTS, TOEFL and Cambridge exams, and helps you find your own level.

## How the CEFR scale works

{{diagram: d1 | Six levels in three pairs: A for basic users, B for independent users, C for proficient users}}

The framework is built on **can-do descriptions**: short statements about real tasks, such as following a train announcement, writing a complaint or taking part in a meeting. Each level describes reading, listening, speaking, writing and interaction separately. That design has two consequences that many learners miss:

1. **Levels are bands, not points.** "B1" covers a wide range. Someone who has just reached B1 and someone almost at B2 are both B1.
2. **You probably have a profile.** It is normal to read at B2 but speak at B1, especially if you learned English mainly from textbooks or study for written exams.

The 2020 Companion Volume also added a **Pre-A1** stage and made the "plus" levels (A2+, B1+, B2+) more visible, so you will sometimes see them on course descriptions.

## A1 and A2: basic user

**A1 (Beginner).** You can introduce yourself, ask and answer very simple questions about where you live and what you like, and understand slow, clear speech with lots of repetition. You rely on memorised phrases. *Example:* filling in a hotel form, ordering a coffee with a set phrase.

**A2 (Elementary).** You can handle routine, predictable exchanges: shopping, directions, describing your family and job in simple terms. You understand short, clear messages and notices. Grammar is mostly present and past simple, and your vocabulary covers everyday topics. *Example:* writing a short message to reschedule a meeting; understanding a simple product description.

A useful sign that you have reached A2: you can survive a day as a tourist without switching language, even if conversations are slow and basic.

## B1 and B2: independent user

**B1 (Intermediate).** You can deal with most situations while travelling, talk about experiences, plans and opinions, and write simple connected text on familiar topics. You understand the main points of clear standard speech on work, school or leisure topics. You still make errors, but they rarely block communication. *Example:* explaining a problem to a landlord and following the reply.

**B2 (Upper-intermediate).** You can interact with fluency and spontaneity, so a conversation with a native speaker is possible without strain for either side. You understand the main ideas of complex text, including technical discussion in your field, and can write clear, detailed text giving arguments for and against a view. *Example:* taking part in a work meeting, writing a report that compares options.

B2 is the level many universities and employers use as their minimum. The jump from B1 to B2 is large and usually takes longer than learners expect, because it depends on vocabulary range, listening to natural speed, and grammar that is accurate under pressure, not on new grammar topics.

## C1 and C2: proficient user

**C1 (Advanced).** You understand long, demanding texts and implicit meaning, express yourself fluently without obvious searching for words, and use language flexibly for social, academic and professional purposes. Your writing is well structured, with controlled use of linking devices. *Example:* presenting research at a seminar and answering unexpected questions.

**C2 (Proficiency).** You understand virtually everything you hear or read with ease, can summarise information from different sources, and express fine shades of meaning precisely even in complex situations. C2 is not the same as being a native speaker; it describes a highly skilled user of the language.

## CEFR levels compared with IELTS, TOEFL and Cambridge exams

{{diagram: d2 | Approximate alignment between CEFR levels and common English exams; check each organisation for official tables}}

Most international exams publish an alignment with the CEFR. The figures above are the commonly used approximations:

- **IELTS:** bands 4.0–5.0 are roughly B1, 5.5–6.5 roughly B2, 7.0–8.0 roughly C1, and 8.5–9 C2. A requirement of "IELTS 6.5" is therefore a B2-level requirement in practice.
- **TOEFL iBT:** since 21 January 2026 the test reports scores on a 1–6 band scale designed to line up with CEFR levels; our guide to the [new TOEFL format](/en/blog/new-toefl-ibt-format-2026/) explains the change.
- **Cambridge English:** each exam targets one level: A2 Key, B1 Preliminary, B2 First, C1 Advanced and C2 Proficiency.

Two cautions. First, alignments are approximate and are updated by the exam boards, so always check the official table of the institution that will read your score. Second, an exam score measures performance on one day in one format. If you need a specific band, our [IELTS self-study plan](/en/blog/ielts-self-study-plan/) and the [TOEIC test format guide](/en/blog/toeic-test-format/) explain how to prepare for those tests specifically.

## How long does it take to move up a level?

There is no fixed number, but exam boards publish rough guides. Cambridge English, for example, suggests around 200 guided learning hours to go from beginner to A2, and roughly 200 more hours for each step after that, with the higher levels taking longer. "Guided learning hours" means time with a teacher; real progress also needs independent practice.

What changes the speed:

- **Daily contact.** Twenty minutes every day beats three hours on Sunday, because memory needs repetition over time. [Spaced repetition](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) is the most efficient way to keep vocabulary.
- **Input volume.** Moving from B1 to B2 requires a lot of reading and listening at natural speed. Our [listening guide](/en/blog/how-to-improve-english-listening/) gives a weekly routine.
- **Output with feedback.** Speaking and writing improve when someone (or something) points out your recurring mistakes.
- **Your first language.** Speakers of languages close to English often progress faster at the lower levels; everyone slows down above B2.

### The intermediate plateau

Many learners stay at B1 for a long time. They can communicate, so the pressure to improve fades, and their mistakes become habits. Breaking the plateau usually means changing what you practise: more unfamiliar input, deliberate vocabulary work with collocations, and targeted correction. The ideas in [how to break the IELTS band plateau](/en/blog/how-to-break-ielts-band-plateau/) apply to general English too.

## How to find your CEFR level

1. **Take a placement test.** A short test gives a quick estimate of your vocabulary and grammar level. It is fast and objective, but it usually does not measure speaking or listening fully.
2. **Read the can-do descriptions honestly.** For each skill, ask: *Could I do this today, without preparation?* Mark the highest level where you say yes to most statements.
3. **Test the weak skill.** If you suspect your speaking is lower, record yourself answering a B1 question (*Describe a trip you enjoyed*) and a B2 question (*Should cities ban cars from the centre? Why?*). Listen back: can you keep going for a minute with clear organisation?
4. **Compare with an exam if you need proof.** For a visa, university or job, only an official exam result counts.

## Self-check: which level fits?

Match each description to a CEFR level (A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2).

1. Maya can follow a TV debate on a topic she knows, give her opinion with reasons, and write a structured essay, but she still searches for words when the topic is unfamiliar.
2. Jun can introduce himself and ask where the bathroom is, using phrases he memorised.
3. Leila reads academic articles comfortably, understands irony and implied meaning, and gives conference talks with few hesitations.
4. Omar can describe his last holiday and his plans for next year, and gets through most travel situations, though he makes frequent grammar mistakes.
5. Ana can buy a train ticket, describe her job in simple sentences and understand short notices.
6. Pierre can summarise several complex sources into one coherent presentation and adjust his tone precisely for each audience.

### Answers

1. **B2**: arguments with reasons and fluency on familiar topics are typical B2 skills.
2. **A1**: memorised phrases for immediate needs.
3. **C1**: implicit meaning and fluent academic use.
4. **B1**: experiences and plans, with errors that do not block communication.
5. **A2**: routine, predictable exchanges.
6. **C2**: synthesising sources and fine control of style.

## Frequently asked questions

### What CEFR level is fluent?

"Fluent" is not a CEFR term. Most people use it to describe B2 or above, where you can talk with native speakers without strain. Employers who ask for "fluent English" usually expect at least B2, often C1.

### Is B2 good enough for university?

Many universities accept B2 (around IELTS 5.5–6.5) for undergraduate study, while postgraduate and some professional courses ask for C1 (IELTS 7.0 or higher). Always check the exact requirement of your programme.

### Can I skip a level?

You cannot skip the skills a level describes, but you can move quickly through a level if you already have some of them, for example strong reading from your studies. Placement tests help you start at the right point instead of repeating material you already know.

### Do CEFR certificates expire?

The CEFR itself does not expire, but exam results often have a validity period set by the receiving institution. IELTS and TOEFL scores, for example, are usually accepted for two years after the test date.
