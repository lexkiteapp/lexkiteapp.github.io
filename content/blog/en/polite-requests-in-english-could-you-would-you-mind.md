**Polite requests in English** are built from a few reliable frames: *Could you…?*, *Would you mind + -ing?*, and *I was wondering if you could…*. Use them with a calm tone, a small softener such as *just* or *when you get a chance*, and a short reason, and you will sound respectful without sounding stiff.

Most learners already know the grammar. What goes wrong is the choice between "direct" and "too direct", the intonation, and what to say when someone refuses. This guide covers all of it, with workplace and everyday dialogues, mistakes that Vietnamese, German, Japanese and Korean speakers often make, and a short quiz at the end.

## Why polite requests in English feel different from other languages

In many languages, politeness is built into the grammar: verb endings, honorific forms, or a choice of pronoun. English has very little of that. Instead, politeness comes from **extra words and an indirect shape**. *Pass the salt* is grammatically fine, but it sounds like an order. *Could you pass the salt?* turns the order into a question, and the listener feels free to say yes or no.

So the rule of thumb is simple: **the bigger the favour, the longer and softer the sentence.** Asking a colleague for a pen can be *Got a pen?* in a friendly office. Asking your manager to move a deadline needs more: *I was wondering if there's any chance we could move the deadline to Friday.*

Three things make a request polite in English:

1. **A question form** instead of a command (*Could you…?*, *Would you…?*).
2. **A softener**: *please*, *just*, *a bit*, *when you get a chance*, *if that's okay*.
3. **A friendly tone**, including a gentle, friendly melody.

## Could, would and "would you mind": the core frames

### Could you…? and Would you…?

*Could you…?* is the all-purpose polite request. It works with a friend, a shop assistant, a teacher or a manager.

- *Could you help me with this form, please?*
- *Could you speak a little more slowly?*
- *Would you send me the address?*

*Would you…?* is a little more formal. Both are fine, and the difference is small. One mistake to avoid is mixing the verbs: *Could you to help me?* and *Would you helping me?* are both wrong. After *could* and *would* use the base verb: *Could you help me?*

Compare *can* and *could*. *Can you…?* is natural among friends and colleagues who know each other. *Could you…?* is safer with strangers, customers and senior people. For the wider picture of *can*, *could*, *should* and *must*, see our guide to [modal verbs](/en/blog/modal-verbs-can-could-should-must/).

### Would you mind + -ing

*Would you mind…?* is very common in British English and still natural in American English. The structure is fixed: **Would you mind + verb-ing**.

- *Would you mind closing the door?*
- *Would you mind waiting a moment?*
- *Would you mind if I opened the window?* (asking permission, past tense after *if*)

The confusing part is the answer. *Would you mind…?* literally asks "Is it a problem for you?", so a bare "yes" can sound like a refusal.

- To agree: *No, not at all.* / *Of course not.* / *Sure, no problem.*
- To refuse: *I'm afraid I do mind, actually.* (rare and direct) or, much more often, *I'm sorry, I'd rather you didn't.*

Many learners answer *Yes, sure* and the other person freezes, unsure whether it was a yes or a no. If you are not certain, add a clear word: *Sure, go ahead.* Why *-ing* and not *to*? After *mind*, English uses a gerund. Our guide to [gerunds and infinitives](/en/blog/gerunds-and-infinitives/) explains which verbs work this way.

### I was wondering if…

*I was wondering if you could…* is the most polite everyday frame. The past continuous makes the request feel tentative, as if you are not forcing anything.

- *I was wondering if you could look at my draft.*
- *I was wondering whether you had any time on Thursday.*
- *I don't suppose you could lend me a charger, could you?* (very British, very polite)

Notice that these are questions about possibility or ability, not about the person's feelings. That is why they sound so respectful: you let the other person say "no, that's not possible" without losing face.

## Softeners: just, a bit, maybe and friends

Small words do a lot of work in English requests. They make the favour sound smaller.

| Softener | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| *just* | *Could you just check this for me?* | "It is only a small thing." |
| *a bit / a little* | *Could you speak a bit louder?* | Makes the criticism lighter. |
| *maybe* | *Maybe we could start at nine?* | A suggestion, not a demand. |
| *when you get a chance / when you have a moment* | *Could you reply when you get a chance?* | No pressure on timing. |
| *if that's okay / if you don't mind* | *I'll sit here, if that's okay.* | Invites a "no". |
| *sorry to bother you, but…* | *Sorry to bother you, but is this seat taken?* | Warms up a stranger. |
| *please* | *Could you wait here, please?* | Adds courtesy, usually at the end. |

Be careful with *just*. In a request it softens (*Could you just sign here?*). In a complaint or an order it can sound impatient (*Just wait*). In British English, understatement is a form of politeness: *It's a bit noisy* often means *It is too noisy, please do something*. Americans use softeners too, but a request is more likely to be taken at face value.

## Tone and stress: how to sound polite, not cold

You can use perfect words and still sound rude if the melody is wrong. Two habits help.

**1. Let your voice rise a little on the final part of a Yes/No request.** *Could you open the window?* usually ends with a rise. A flat, falling voice makes the same words sound like an order or irritation.

**2. Stress the key content word, not every word.** In *Could you send me the **report**?* the main stress falls on *report*. Do not stress *could* or *you* (*COULD YOU SEND ME…*) unless you are showing annoyance. For *please*, a short, light *please* sounds friendly; a long, heavy *pleeease* sounds impatient or sarcastic. More detail is in our guide to [sentence stress in English speaking](/en/blog/sentence-stress-in-english-speaking-guide/).

## Saying yes and saying no politely

A request is a two-way conversation. Learners often prepare the question but not the answer.

**Agreeing:**

- *Sure, no problem.* / *Of course.* / *Happy to.*
- *Yes, I can do that by Thursday.* (add a time, which is helpful)
- *Absolutely. Anything else?*

**Refusing politely.** A bare *No* is possible but harsh. A polite refusal usually has three parts: a soft start, a reason, and an alternative.

- *I'm afraid I can't do that today. I have a deadline at five. Could it wait until tomorrow?*
- *I'd love to help, but I'm tied up this afternoon. Maybe Priya could take a look?*
- *Sorry, I'm not able to share that. You could ask the finance team.*

Useful verbs in refusals: *I'm afraid…* (UK and US, formal-soft), *I'd rather not…*, *I'm not able to…*, *Let me check and get back to you.* The last one buys time when you do not want to commit at once.

> **Maya:** Hi Tom, sorry to bother you. Would you mind looking at my slides before the call?
>
> **Tom:** Of course not. When's the call?
>
> **Maya:** At two. I was wondering if you could just check the numbers on slide four.
>
> **Tom:** I'm afraid I'm in a meeting until one-thirty, but I can look at them straight after. Would that be okay?
>
> **Maya:** That would be perfect. Thanks so much.

Notice what Tom does: he refuses the exact timing, gives a reason, and offers an alternative. Everyone keeps face.

## Work and everyday life: choosing the right level

The right level depends on three factors: **how big the favour is, how senior the person is, and how well you know them.**

**At work.**

- Peer, small favour: *Could you send me the link?* / *Can you cover the call for me?*
- Manager, medium favour: *Would you mind if I left a bit early on Friday?* / *Could we go through this together?*
- Manager or client, big favour: *I was wondering if there is any chance we could extend the deadline by a day.*
- Email: *Could you please send the signed form by Friday? Many thanks.* / *I'd be grateful if you could confirm by Monday.*
- Giving a task to a team member: *Could you take this on? It would really help.* A "please do X" sounds like an order unless the relationship is already friendly. For giving feedback and criticism in the same polite way, see [how to give feedback in English at work](/en/blog/how-to-give-feedback-in-english-at-work/).

**In daily life.**

- On a bus: *Excuse me, would you mind moving your bag?*
- To a neighbour: *Would you mind keeping the music down after ten? I've got an early start.*
- To a stranger: *Sorry to bother you, could you tell me where the station is?*

Say *Could I…?* for your own action and *Could you…?* for theirs.

## Mistakes learners make with polite requests

1. **Do you can…?** Wrong: *Do you can help me?* Modals do not take *do*. Right: *Can you help me?* / *Could you help me?* (See our guide to [forming questions](/en/blog/how-to-form-questions-in-english/).)
2. **I want you to…** *I want you to check this* is grammatical but sounds like an order from a boss. Use *Could you check this for me?* or *I'd appreciate it if you could check this.*
3. **Imperative plus "please" only.** *Please send me the file* is fine in a form or an instruction sheet, but spoken to a colleague it can sound cold. Add a frame: *Could you please send me the file?*
4. **Would you mind + to-verb.** Wrong: *Would you mind to open…?* Right: *Would you mind opening…?*
5. **Answering "Would you mind…?" with "Yes".** It means "I do mind". Say *Sure, go ahead* or *No problem*.

## Cultural notes: how direct is too direct?

Politeness customs differ, and this is general experience rather than a rule for every person.

- **British English** tends to be the most indirect. Understatement and softeners are common (*I was wondering if…*, *Would you mind…*, *a bit*). A British person who says *That's quite interesting* may be politely disagreeing.
- **American English** is usually friendly and direct. *Can you…?* is normal at work, and *Could you…?* is polite but not formal. People often say what they need quickly and add *thanks!* A very long, heavily softened request may sound slightly unnatural.
- **German speakers** are often used to a more direct style, where a short request is clear and respectful. In English, a bare *I need the report today* can seem demanding. Add a frame: *Could you send me the report today? I need it for a meeting.*
- **Japanese and Korean speakers** may be used to honorifics and to a clear hierarchy, so requests to seniors are carefully softened. In English the hierarchy is less visible in the grammar, so you may over-apologise or hint too much. A short, clear request with *Could you…?* is usually enough, even to a manager.
If you are unsure, copy the other person's level. If your manager writes *Can you send this over?*, you can write the same back.

## Self-check: choose the best polite request

1. Which is more polite for a request to your manager: *Give me the day off* or *I was wondering if I could have Friday off*?
2. Correct the sentence: *Would you mind to open the window?*
3. Someone asks *Would you mind moving your bag?* You are happy to do it. Which answer is clear: *Yes, of course* or *No, not at all*?
4. Correct the sentence: *Do you can help me?*
5. Which word makes this softer: *Could you check this?* → *Could you ___ check this for me?*
6. Write a polite refusal for: *Could you work on Saturday?* (give a reason and an alternative)

**Answers**

1. *I was wondering if I could have Friday off.* The first is an order. The second is tentative and gives the manager room to answer.
2. *Would you mind opening the window?* After *mind* use a gerund (*-ing*), not *to*.
3. *No, not at all.* Because *Would you mind?* asks whether it is a problem, *No* means "no problem". *Yes, of course* is confusing.
4. *Can you help me?* / *Could you help me?* Modal verbs do not use *do*.
5. *just* (*Could you just check this for me?*). *Quickly* would also work. Both make the favour sound smaller.
6. For example: *I'm afraid I can't this Saturday, as I have a family commitment. Could I make up the hours on Monday instead?* Any answer with a soft start, a reason and an alternative works.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is "Could you" more polite than "Can you"?

Usually yes. *Could you…?* sounds a little softer and is safer with strangers, customers and seniors. *Can you…?* is natural between friends and colleagues, especially in American English. Both are correct.

### What does "Would you mind" really mean?

It asks "Is it a problem for you?", so *No, not at all* means you agree. Do not answer *Yes* unless you really do mind. Follow it with *-ing* (*Would you mind helping me?*) or with *if I* plus a past tense (*Would you mind if I sat here?*).

### Can I use "please" instead of the longer frames?

Yes, in many everyday cases (*Two coffees, please*). But for a favour or a request to a colleague or manager, a frame such as *Could you…?* is more natural than the bare command with *please*. Tone matters as much as the words.

### How do I refuse a request politely in English?

Start with *I'm afraid…* or *I'd love to, but…*, give a short reason, and offer an alternative if you can. *I'm afraid I can't today, but I could do it tomorrow morning.* A clear, kind refusal is better than an unclear yes.
