The most common **IELTS Writing mistakes** are not exotic. Most lost marks come from a handful of habits: answering part of the question, writing a position that disappears halfway through, linking words used mechanically, and small grammar slips that repeat across the whole essay. Each of these is fixable once you can name it. This guide sorts the mistakes by marking criterion, shows wrong and corrected examples for many of them, and ends with a short self-edit routine you can adapt to the time you have in the exam.

## How IELTS writing mistakes map to the four criteria

{{diagram: d1 | Each mistake damages one criterion most; knowing which helps you choose what to fix first}}

Writing is marked on task achievement or response, coherence and cohesion, lexical resource, and grammatical range and accuracy, each given equal weight in the final band. Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1, so a mistake in Task 2 is more expensive. For the overall picture of marking, see [IELTS band scores explained](/en/blog/ielts-band-scores-explained/).

## Task response mistakes

**1. Not answering every part of the question.** In a question such as *Discuss both views and give your opinion*, an essay that discusses only one view will lose marks.

**2. No clear position.** *There are advantages and disadvantages* is not a position. Wrong: *Some people like cars and some people don't.* Better: *Although cars are convenient, I believe their disadvantages outweigh their benefits in large cities.*

**3. A position that changes.** Candidates agree in the introduction and disagree in the conclusion. Plan the argument first; the guide to [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/) shows how.

**4. Ideas without support.** A body paragraph that lists three ideas in one sentence each is thin. Develop one idea with an explanation and an example.

**5. Task 1: opinions or reasons.** In a report, writing *this happened because people were poorer* adds information that the data does not give. Describe, do not explain. For a full method see [IELTS Writing Task 1 bar chart](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-bar-chart/) and [the line graph guide](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/).

**6. Under the word limit.** Under 150 words in Task 1 or under 250 in Task 2 lowers the task score.

## Coherence and cohesion mistakes

**7. Mechanical linking.** *Firstly... Secondly... Moreover... In addition... Furthermore...* in every sentence makes writing sound like a list. Wrong: *Cars are expensive. Moreover, they pollute. Furthermore, they cause traffic. In addition, they are dangerous.* Better: *Cars are expensive to run and, because they add to congestion and pollution, they impose costs on everyone.* For correct use, see [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/).

**8. Overusing "However".** Use contrast words where there is a real contrast.

**9. Paragraphs that do not match their topic sentence.** Check that each sentence in a body paragraph supports the first sentence.

**10. Unclear pronouns.** *This causes problems* is unclear if *this* could refer to three earlier ideas. Add a noun: *This trend causes problems.*

## Lexical resource mistakes

**11. Memorised rare words in the wrong place.** Wrong: *The government should eradicate the myriad paradigms of education.* Better: *The government should reform the education system.* The guide to [the sophistication trap in IELTS writing](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) explains why accuracy beats difficulty.

**12. Repeating the same word.** Using *important* five times shows limited range. Use *significant*, *essential* or *a key factor*, but only if you are sure of the meaning.

**13. Wrong word forms.** *The economy growth* should be *economic growth*. *Success* is a noun, *successful* an adjective.

**14. Weak collocations.** *Make a research* should be *carry out research*. *Do a mistake* should be *make a mistake*. See [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) and [make vs do](/en/blog/make-vs-do/).

**15. Informal language.** Wrong: *kids*, *a lot of stuff*, *gonna*. Better: *children*, *many things*, *going to*, or a more formal alternative in an essay.

## Grammar mistakes

**16. Articles.** Wrong: *Pollution harms environment.* Better: *Pollution harms the environment.* See [a, an, the rules](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/).

**17. Subject-verb agreement.** Wrong: *The number of cars are growing.* Better: *The number of cars is growing.*

**18. Comma splices and run-ons.** Wrong: *Public transport is cheap, it is also clean.* Better: *Public transport is cheap, and it is also clean.* Or use a full stop.

**19. Tense errors in Task 1.** Past data needs past tenses. Keep tense consistent.

**20. Sentence fragments.** *Although many people prefer cars.* is not complete. Add the main clause or remove *although*.

**21. Only simple sentences.** If every sentence has one clause, grammatical range suffers. Practise [complex sentences](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/) in small steps.

## Find your own top three mistakes

A list of twenty mistakes is not a plan. Most candidates repeat three or four of them again and again, and fixing those few brings more than studying all twenty. Here is a way to find yours:

1. Collect three essays or reports you wrote recently, ideally under timed conditions.
2. Highlight every error in a different colour for each category: task, linking, vocabulary, grammar.
3. Count them. If you have twelve article errors and two spelling errors, articles are your first target.
4. Write one rule and three correct examples for each of your top three mistakes on a single card.
5. Write a fresh paragraph using those rules, then check it again.

Be honest about *why* each mistake happens. Some come from your first language: speakers of languages without articles often drop them. Others come from speed: you know the rule but forget it when rushing. Knowing the cause tells you whether you need to learn something new or to slow down and proofread.

If after several weeks the same errors appear, ask a teacher or a language partner to mark one essay against the four criteria. An outside reader will spot patterns you cannot see.

## A five-minute self-edit

{{diagram: d2 | Read the essay in layers, each time looking for only one thing}}

Do not just reread for "mistakes". Read in layers: task, verbs, nouns, punctuation, words. In five minutes you may only manage some layers, so start with the layer where your own errors are most frequent. That information comes from your error log. The method is described in [the IELTS feedback loop](/en/blog/the-feedback-loop-method-for-ielts/): collect the errors you make, group them and review them until they stop appearing.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most common IELTS Writing mistake?

Not answering the whole question is one of the most costly, followed by inaccurate grammar that repeats across the essay. Read the instruction carefully and plan before you write.

### Do small spelling mistakes matter?

A few do not stop a high band, but frequent errors reduce the lexical resource score and can make a sentence hard to read. Check spelling of topic words you use often.

### Should I use rare vocabulary to impress the examiner?

Only if you are sure of the meaning and the collocation. Accurate everyday academic vocabulary scores better than a rare word used wrongly.

### How do I stop making the same mistake?

Record it in a log, write the correct version, and test yourself a few days later. Awareness during writing comes from repeated review, not from a single correction.
