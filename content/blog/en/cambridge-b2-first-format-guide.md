The **Cambridge B2 First format** has four papers, Reading and Use of English, Writing, Listening and Speaking. Cambridge averages five equally weighted scores (Reading, Use of English, Writing, Listening and Speaking), so the combined Reading and Use of English paper counts for about 40 per cent of the result and each of the other three papers for about 20 per cent. B2 First, formerly known as First Certificate in English or FCE, tests English at level B2 of the CEFR, which is an upper-intermediate level. This guide explains each paper and part, the tasks you meet and the habits that help. Exam formats can change, so confirm timings and task numbers in the current handbook on the official Cambridge English website before you plan your test day.

## The four papers of the Cambridge B2 First format

{{diagram: d1 | Four papers; Reading and Use of English carries the largest share}}

The papers are Reading and Use of English (about 75 minutes), Writing (about 80 minutes), Listening (about 40 minutes) and Speaking (about 14 minutes, taken with another candidate). Results are reported on the Cambridge English Scale, and you can read what your level means in [CEFR levels explained](/en/blog/cefr-levels-explained/).

## Reading and Use of English: seven parts

{{diagram: d2 | Parts 1 to 4 test language use, parts 5 to 7 test reading}}

**Part 1: multiple-choice cloze.** A short text has gaps, and for each gap you choose one word from four options. It tests vocabulary: collocations, phrasal verbs, fixed phrases and words with similar meanings. Read the whole sentence, because the clue is often after the gap.

**Part 2: open cloze.** You fill each gap with one word and no options are given. Most answers are grammar words such as prepositions, articles, auxiliaries, pronouns and linkers. Decide what kind of word is needed before guessing.

**Part 3: word formation.** A word is given in capitals at the end of a line, and you change it to fit the gap. Decide the part of speech, then check for a negative prefix or a plural.

**Part 4: key word transformation.** You rewrite a sentence using a given word so that it means the same as the original, using two to five words. Spelling and grammar must both be correct. Original example: *"I haven't seen her for a year." (LAST) → "The last time I saw her was a year ago."*

**Part 5: long text with multiple choice.** You read a text and answer questions on opinion, attitude, detail and the writer's purpose. Wrong options often use words from the text, so match meaning, not words.

**Part 6: gapped text.** Sentences have been removed from a text and you choose where they fit. Look at pronouns, linkers and time words before and after each gap.

**Part 7: multiple matching.** You match statements to short texts or sections. Scan quickly and check details, since several options may look right.

For vocabulary tasks, practise [collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) and [common phrasal verbs](/en/blog/common-phrasal-verbs-in-english/); for grammar, the guides to [conditional sentences](/en/blog/conditional-sentences-in-english/) and [passive voice](/en/blog/passive-voice-in-english/) help with Parts 2 and 4.

## A worked example: key word transformation

Part 4 needs precise grammar and spelling, so a closer look is worth it. Original item: *It is not necessary for you to bring a map. (NEED) → You ____ a map.* The answer is *don't need to bring*. Count the words: contractions count as two words, so *don't need to bring* is five words, exactly at the upper limit of the two to five allowed. Common errors are *don't need bring* (missing *to*) and *needn't to bring* (a mixed pattern).

The method is always the same. Identify the grammar pattern the key word forces (here, *need to* plus infinitive), check meaning is unchanged, then check the word count. Never change the key word, because an altered key word means zero marks even when the sentence is right.

## Writing: two parts

**Part 1 is a compulsory essay.** You respond to a prompt and two given ideas, add a third idea of your own, and give reasons for your view. Plan the structure: introduction, one paragraph per idea, conclusion.

**Part 2 offers a choice** of text types, such as an article, an email or letter, a report or a review. Each type has its own register and layout. An email to a friend can be informal; a report needs headings and a neutral tone.

Answers are marked on content, communicative achievement, organisation and language. Write to the word range given in the instructions, and keep the style that fits the reader. For connectors and structure, see [linking words](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/) and [how to write a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/).

## Listening: four parts

The recordings are played twice. Expect a mix of short extracts with multiple choice, a monologue with sentence completion, a task with five speakers matched to options, and a longer interview or discussion with multiple choice. Read each question during the pause, predict the kind of answer and write while you listen. The second hearing is for checking, not starting. See [how to improve English listening](/en/blog/how-to-improve-english-listening/) for daily habits.

## Speaking: four parts

You take the test with another candidate and two examiners. Parts include a conversation with the examiner about yourself, a long turn comparing two pictures, a collaborative task with your partner, and a discussion. You are marked on grammar and vocabulary, discourse management, pronunciation and interactive communication. Listen to your partner, respond naturally and invite their opinion; it is not a competition. [Shadowing](/en/blog/shadowing-technique/) and [speaking with confidence](/en/blog/speak-english-with-confidence/) help.

## Common reasons candidates lose marks

- **Spelling in Parts 2, 3 and 4.** A correct word spelled wrongly earns nothing, so write clearly and check each answer.
- **Ignoring the reader in Writing.** An article for a school magazine and a letter to a manager need different registers.
- **Running out of time in Reading and Use of English.** Seven parts in 75 minutes is tight, so do the quick grammar parts first and leave long texts for last.
- **Overusing memorised phrases.** Examiners can tell when a phrase does not fit the task.
- **Talking over your partner in Speaking.** Interaction is assessed; listening and responding is part of the score.

## How to prepare in eight weeks

| Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Learn the format; take a full practice test for a baseline |
| 3–4 | Use of English: collocations, word formation, transformations |
| 5–6 | Writing: one essay and one other text type each week, checked with a rubric |
| 7 | Listening and Speaking under test conditions |
| 8 | Full mock test, review errors, rest |

Keep an error log by part. Your own log will show which parts cost you the most marks, so target those first.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long is the Cambridge B2 First exam?

The four papers add up to roughly three and a half hours in total, with Speaking taken separately in some centres. Check the official handbook for current timings for your test type.

### What is the difference between B2 First and IELTS?

B2 First is a certificate exam targeting one CEFR level, reported as a score and a grade. IELTS reports a band score across a wider range and has different task types. Choose the one that your university, employer or visa authority accepts.

### Is B2 First the same as FCE?

Yes. FCE, First Certificate in English, was the older name of the exam that is now called B2 First.

### Which part is hardest?

It differs between candidates. Candidates often find Part 4 key word transformations and Part 7 multiple matching demanding because both need precise reading and grammar control. Check your own practice results to see which part costs you most.
