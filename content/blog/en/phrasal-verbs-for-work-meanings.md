Reading a job advert, joining a call or writing a quick message to a manager: phrasal verbs are everywhere, and they are rarely explained in a business course. This guide covers the **phrasal verbs for work meaning** you will hear most, grouped by situation: meetings, tasks and deadlines, working with people, and email. Each one comes with a plain definition, an example you can adapt, and a note when the verb is informal or differs between American and British English.

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word (a particle such as *up*, *out*, *on* or *off*) whose meaning is often different from the verb alone. *Put* and *off* separately say little, but *put off a meeting* means to postpone it. You cannot always guess the meaning, so the efficient way to learn them is in situations, not alphabetical lists.

## How phrasal verbs for work meaning change with the context

Many phrasal verbs have more than one meaning. *Take on* can mean "accept a job" (*She took on extra clients*) or "compete with" (*The startup is taking on the big banks*). *Pick up* can mean "learn casually", "collect" or "improve". When you meet one in a message, use the object and the situation to decide. If it is still unclear, a dictionary with example sentences settles it faster than a translation, and our guide to [using an English-English dictionary](/en/blog/how-to-use-an-english-english-dictionary/) shows how.

In formal writing, a single verb often does the same job: *postpone* for *put off*, *investigate* for *look into*. Phrasal verbs are natural in speech, chat and everyday email, but a formal report, a contract or a cover letter may prefer the one-word version.

## Meetings and communication

1. **bring up** – to mention a topic. *Could I bring up the budget before we finish?*
2. **go over** – to review something carefully. *Let's go over the figures once more.*
3. **run through** – to review quickly or rehearse. *I'll run through the agenda in two minutes.*
4. **walk (someone) through** – to explain step by step. *Can you walk me through the process?*
5. **point out** – to draw attention to a fact. *She pointed out a mistake in the forecast.*
6. **sum up** – to give the main points briefly. *To sum up, we need two more weeks.*
7. **wrap up** – to finish. *Let's wrap up the call; we are over time.*
8. **follow up (on)** – to take further action or contact again. *I'll follow up on this tomorrow.*
9. **get back to (someone)** – to reply later. *Let me check and get back to you by noon.*
10. **set up** – to arrange or create. *Can you set up a call with the design team?*

Notice the polite patterns: *Could I bring up...?*, *Can you walk me through...?* They make requests sound softer than a bare command. For full phrases to use in meetings, see our [business English meeting phrases](/en/blog/business-english-meeting-phrases-guide/).

## Tasks, deadlines and problems

11. **carry out** – to perform a task. *The team carried out a survey last month.*
12. **figure out** – to find the solution by thinking. *We need to figure out why sales dropped.*
13. **sort out** – to resolve or organise. *Could you sort out the invoice error?*
14. **look into** – to investigate. *I'll look into the delay today.*
15. **deal with** – to handle. *She deals with customer complaints.*
16. **take on** – to accept a task or responsibility. *He took on the new project in March.*
17. **hand in** – to submit (British); **turn in** is the common American form. *Please hand in your timesheet by Friday.*
18. **fall behind** – to be slower than planned. *We are falling behind schedule.*
19. **catch up (on)** – to reach the expected level after delay; also to learn what you missed. *I need to catch up on emails after the holiday.*
20. **stick to** – to follow a plan or limit. *Let's stick to the budget.*

Some of these look similar and are easy to mix up. *Hand in* means to give something to a person in authority (a report, a form), while *hand over* means to transfer something to another person (*She handed over the project to Ana*). *Call off* means cancel (*The event was called off*), and *put off* means postpone. Learning the pairs together, with one example each, helps because you notice the difference at once.

## Working with people

21. **report to** – to have someone as your manager. *I report to the head of finance.*
22. **take over** – to assume control from someone. *She will take over my accounts next week.*
23. **step in** – to help when someone cannot. *Dan stepped in when the speaker was ill.*
24. **fill in for** – to do someone else's job temporarily. *Who is filling in for Maria?*
25. **back up** – to support or confirm; also to save a copy of data. *Can you back me up in the meeting?*
26. **team up (with)** – to work together. *We teamed up with marketing on the launch.*
27. **step down** – to leave a senior role. *The director stepped down in June.*
28. **sign off on** – to approve formally. *Legal needs to sign off on the contract.*
29. **loop in** – informal, to include someone in a conversation. *Let's loop in finance.*
30. **reach out (to)** – to contact someone, especially a person you do not yet know. *I'll reach out to the supplier.* (Very common in American business writing.)

*Report to*, *deal with* and *step down from* are inseparable, so the object follows them directly, as the rules below explain.

## Separable or inseparable: where does the object go?

{{diagram: d1 | A pronoun object always goes inside a separable phrasal verb, but after an inseparable one.}}

Most mistakes with phrasal verbs are about word order. With a **separable** verb, a noun object can go in the middle or at the end (*set up a meeting / set a meeting up*), but a pronoun must go in the middle (*set it up*, not *set up it*). With an **inseparable** verb the object always comes after the particle (*look into the problem*, *look into it*). Three-part phrasal verbs such as *keep up with*, *get on with* and *look forward to* are inseparable too: *I look forward to meeting you*, with a gerund after *to*, because *to* is a preposition here.

If a dictionary shows *sth* or *sb* in the middle of the entry, such as "hand sth in", the verb is separable. This is the kind of detail shown in a good dictionary entry, which is why checking one verb a day beats memorising a list.

## Common mistakes with phrasal verbs at work

1. **Using a phrasal verb that is too casual.** *Get ahead of the deadline* is fine in a chat; in a formal report use *meet the deadline early*.
2. **Splitting an inseparable verb.** *We will deal it with* is wrong; *We will deal with it* is correct.
3. **Choosing the wrong particle.** *Hand off* (pass a task on) and *hand in* (submit) differ from *hand out* (distribute).
4. **Translating word by word.** *Come up with* means "think of an idea", not "arrive with something".
5. **Forgetting the preposition after the verb.** You *report to* a manager, *deal with* a problem, *sign off on* a plan.

If you write many work messages, the habit of keeping a small notebook of 3 to 5 phrasal verbs per week and using each in a real sentence is more effective than studying 100 in one evening. ## Self-check

1. The CEO has ____ (left her role). Choose: stepped down / stepped in.
2. We cannot ____ the project because we have no budget. Choose: take on / take off.
3. Rewrite with a pronoun: "She set up the meeting."
4. Which is correct: "deal it with" or "deal with it"?
5. What is a more formal word for "put off a decision"?
6. "I'll get back to you tomorrow." What will the speaker do?

### Answers

1. **stepped down.** *Step down* means leave a senior position; *step in* means help temporarily.
2. **take on.** It means accept a project or responsibility.
3. **She set it up.** A pronoun object goes between the verb and the particle of a separable verb.
4. **deal with it.** *Deal with* is inseparable.
5. **postpone** (or *defer*).
6. **Reply to you tomorrow.** It does not mean return in person; it means respond later.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are phrasal verbs too informal for business English?

Not generally. Many, such as *carry out*, *follow up* and *set up*, are standard in everyday workplace English. A few are casual, such as *loop in* or *touch base*. In formal documents, a single-word verb may sound more neutral.

### How many phrasal verbs do I need to know for work?

A working set of 30 to 50 is enough for most office conversations, and the lists above cover many of the most common. Learn them in groups of three to five and use each one in a real sentence.

### Why are phrasal verbs so hard to learn?

Because the particle changes the meaning in ways that are not always logical, and the same verb has several meanings. Context, example sentences and repetition work better than translation.

### What is the difference between hand in and hand over?

*Hand in* means to submit something to a person in authority, such as a report. *Hand over* means to give control or possession to someone else, such as a project, a key or a task.
