Renting your first home in a new country is stressful enough without the language worry. The good news: **English phrases for renting an apartment** follow a very predictable script: you enquire, you view, you apply, you agree the terms, and later you report problems. Learn the key lines for each stage and most conversations become routine.

The harder part is that the same thing has different names depending on where you land. A *flat* in London is an *apartment* in Chicago and a *unit* in Sydney. What Americans call a *security deposit*, Australians and New Zealanders call a *bond*. This guide gives you phrases that work everywhere, then shows you the regional words you will actually see in listings and contracts.

## English phrases for renting an apartment: lease, tenancy, deposit and bond words

Before you read a single listing, sort out the vocabulary. Using the local word makes you sound like you belong, and it stops you misreading a contract.

{{diagram: d1 | The same renting ideas have different names in different countries. Use the local word in listings, emails and conversations.}}

A few notes the table cannot show:

- **Lease vs tenancy agreement.** Both are the written contract between you and the landlord. In the US and Canada, people say *lease* ("I signed a one-year lease"). In the UK, *tenancy agreement* is the normal phrase, and in Australia and New Zealand you will hear both. A *lease* can also be the verb: *to lease a car*.
- **Tenant, landlord, letting agent.** You are the *tenant* (or *renter* in the US); the owner is the *landlord*. In the UK, a *letting agent* manages the property for the owner. In Australia and New Zealand the same job is a *property manager*, often working for a *real estate agency*. In larger US apartment buildings you may also meet a *super* (the *superintendent*).
- **Deposit vs bond vs holding deposit.** In the US and Canada you pay a *security deposit*. In Australia and New Zealand it is a *bond*. In the UK it is simply the *deposit*, and a separate *holding deposit* may be taken to reserve a place while checks happen. Ask what is refundable and when.
- **Rent is a noun and a verb.** *I rent an apartment from my landlord* (you are the tenant). *My landlord rents out the apartment* (the owner). Learners often mix up *rent from* and *rent out*.
- **Utilities and bills.** In North America *utilities* means water, gas, electricity, sometimes internet. In the UK people say *bills*, and *council tax* is a separate local tax. *Bills included* or *all bills inclusive* means the rent covers them; check which ones.
- **Furnished, studio, one-bed.** *Furnished* means furniture is included (ask for a list). A *studio* is one room with a kitchenette; *1BR/1BA* means one bedroom, one bathroom.

## Stage 1: Enquiring about a place

Your first contact is usually a message or short call. Keep it polite, specific and short.

- *Hi, I'm calling about the flat on Oak Street. Is it still available?*
- *How much is the rent?* (In Australia and New Zealand it is often quoted *per week*: *$520 a week*.)
- *Are utilities included in the rent?* / *Are the bills included?*
- *Is it furnished or unfurnished?*
- *How long is the lease?* / *Are pets allowed?*
- *Could I arrange a viewing?*

Use *Is it still available?* (not *Is it free?*, which asks whether it costs nothing) and *Could I…?* or *Would it be possible to…?* rather than *I want…*. To practise question forms, see our guide to [how to form questions in English](/en/blog/how-to-form-questions-in-english/).

## Stage 2: The viewing

At a viewing (US: *showing*; Australia: *inspection* or *open for inspection*; NZ: *open home*), you look and ask. Do not feel rude about asking questions; agents expect them.

- *Is the washing machine included?*
- *How does the heating work?* (gas, electric, central heating, heat pump)
- *Which utilities are in my name?* / *Which bills would I pay?*
- *Is there storage, parking or laundry in the building?*
- *Who handles repairs, you or the owner?*

A typical conversation at a viewing:

> **Agent:** Come on in. This is the living room, and the kitchen's just through there.
>
> **You:** It's lovely and bright. Is the heating electric or gas?
>
> **Agent:** It's gas central heating. The boiler was serviced in September.
>
> **You:** Great. And are the bills included, or would I set those up myself?
>
> **Agent:** Water is included in the rent. You'd arrange electricity, gas and broadband yourself.
>
> **You:** Understood. Could I take a photo of the bathroom ceiling? There's a small patch of damp I'd like to ask about.

Notice the last line: *a small patch of damp*, raised calmly as a question. Spotting problems at the viewing is easier than arguing about them later.

## Stage 3: Applying and agreeing the terms

Landlords check that you can pay and are reliable. Words you will meet:

- **Application form / fee** (fees are common in the US; rules differ by place).
- **Proof of income** (payslips, employment letter), **references** from a previous landlord or employer, and a **credit check** (US/Canada) or **referencing** (UK).
- **Guarantor / co-signer**: someone who agrees to pay if you cannot. *Do you accept a guarantor if I have no local credit history?*
- **Holding deposit**: *Is it refundable if the application is declined?*
- **Fixed term**, **month-to-month**, **notice period**, **move-in date**.

Useful sentences when you are applying as a newcomer:

- *I've just moved to the country, so I don't have a local rental history. Could I give you an employer reference instead?*
- *When will I hear back about my application?*

When the offer comes, read before you sign. These questions are always reasonable:

- *Could I take the lease home and read it overnight?*
- *How much is the deposit, and where will it be held?*
- *How do I get it back at the end, and how long does that take?*
- *Is there a condition report or inventory at move-in?* (Photograph everything with dates.)
- *What notice do I have to give if I want to move out?*
- *Who do I contact in an emergency?*

## What your rights usually look like (and where to check)

Tenant law is set by each country and often by each state, province or territory, so a rule from a forum about another place may not apply to you, and this article cannot tell you what your lease means. In general, most places expect a written agreement, a condition report at move-in, a safe and reasonably maintained home, notice before the landlord visits, and a formal process for ending a tenancy. For anything specific, go to the official source for your location.

- **England:** [GOV.UK private renting](https://www.gov.uk/private-renting) explains tenancy types and what to expect. Rules in England have been changing, so use the current page rather than older guides. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own rules.
- **United States:** rules are mostly set by states and cities. [USAGov's tenant rights page](https://www.usa.gov/tenant-rights) explains how to raise a problem with a landlord and points you to state and local help.
- **Canada:** rules are set by each province or territory. For example, [Ontario's renting guide](https://www.ontario.ca/page/renting-ontario-your-rights) covers that province; search for your own province's residential tenancies service.
- **Australia:** each state and territory has its own tenancy law and bond authority. [NSW Government's rental bond page](https://www.nsw.gov.au/housing-and-construction/renting-a-place-to-live/residential-rental-bonds) shows how one state handles it; other states have equivalent pages.
- **New Zealand:** [Tenancy Services](https://www.tenancy.govt.nz/) covers bonds, tenancy agreements, maintenance and disputes.

If you are unsure, ask a local tenant advice service or a legal professional.

## Reporting a problem: a maintenance request email

Sooner or later something breaks. A clear, polite and written message protects you: it gives the date you reported the problem and the details. Phone for emergencies, but follow up in writing.

{{diagram: d2 | Four parts of a good repair request: who and where, the problem, details, and a polite request.}}

Here is a template you can copy and adapt:

> **Subject:** Repair request – no heating, Flat 4B, 12 Elm Road
>
> Dear Ms Patel,
>
> I'm writing to let you know that the heating in Flat 4B has stopped working. It has been off since Sunday evening, and there is also no hot water in the kitchen or bathroom.
>
> Could you please arrange for someone to take a look at it this week? I am at home most evenings and am happy to give access at any time that suits. If it is easier, you can reach me on 07700 900142.
>
> I have attached two photos of the boiler display. Please let me know when to expect a visit.
>
> Thank you for your help.
>
> Kind regards,
> Linh Tran

Why it works: the **subject line** names the problem and address; the facts come first (*stopped working*, *since Sunday evening*) with no blame; *Could you please arrange…* is polite but clear; and the photos and dates become your record.

Handy phrases for the same situation by phone or text:

- *There's something wrong with the heating.* / *The heating isn't working.* (Better than *The heating is broken*, which is understood, but sounds less neutral.)
- *There's a leak under the kitchen sink.* / *The smoke alarm keeps beeping.*
- *Could someone come and take a look this week?*
- *When can I expect someone to come?*

For a more general writing frame, our [guide to writing a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/) shows greetings, closings and tone for situations like this.

## Moving out: ending a tenancy

- *I'd like to give notice that I'll be moving out on 30 June.* (Check the notice period in your agreement.)
- *Could we arrange a final inspection?* / *Could we do a walk-through?*
- *Could you confirm in writing how much of the deposit will be returned?*
- *Could you explain this deduction?* (If you disagree, see the official links above.)

## Mistakes learners make when renting

1. **Sounding too blunt.** *I want to see the apartment* is understandable but abrupt. Try *I'd like to arrange a viewing.*
2. **Saying "contract".** It is understood, but for a home people say *lease* or *tenancy agreement*.
3. **Saying "the guarantee" for deposit.** Use *deposit* or *bond*.
4. **Forgetting articles.** *I'm looking for a flat* (not *I'm looking for flat*), *the heating* (not *heating* in a specific sentence). More in our [guide to a, an and the](/en/blog/articles-a-an-the-rules/).
5. **Agreeing to everything on the phone.** It is fine to say *Could you send that in writing?* and take time to read.

## Self-check: choose the best word or phrase

1. In Sydney, the money you pay at the start of a tenancy, which a state authority holds, is called the ____.
2. In London, the written agreement between you and the owner is usually called a ____ agreement.
3. Your listing says "bills included". What should you ask?
4. Which is more polite: *I want to see the flat* or *I'd like to arrange a viewing*?
5. Fix this sentence: *The heating is broken since Sunday.*
6. In a US apartment building, who is the "super"?

**Answers**

1. **bond.** In Australia (and New Zealand) the word is *bond*; in the US and Canada, *security deposit*.
2. **tenancy** agreement. The word *lease* is more common in the US and Canada.
3. *Which bills are included?* Water, electricity, gas, internet and council tax may or may not be covered.
4. *I'd like to arrange a viewing.* It is a polite request rather than a statement of wants.
5. *The heating hasn't been working since Sunday.* Use the present perfect with *since*; *It has been off since Sunday* also works.
6. The building superintendent: the person who looks after the building and handles day-to-day repairs.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a lease and a tenancy agreement?

Both are the written contract for renting a home. *Lease* is the usual word in the US and Canada, *tenancy agreement* in the UK, and Australia and New Zealand use both. Terms vary by location, so read yours and check your area's official page.

### What is a bond, and is it the same as a deposit?

In Australia and New Zealand, a *bond* is the money you pay as security at the start of a tenancy, similar to a security deposit in the US and Canada. In the UK it is called a *deposit*. In several places it must be held by a government body or approved scheme, so ask where yours is held.

### What does "bills included" mean?

It means the rent includes some or all of the household bills, such as water, electricity, gas and internet. It may not mean all of them, so ask which ones.
