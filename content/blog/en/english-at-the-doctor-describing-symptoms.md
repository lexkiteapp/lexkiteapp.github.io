To **describe symptoms in English**, answer four questions in order: *where* does it hurt, *what* does it feel like, *how long* has it lasted and *how bad* is it. For example: *I have a sharp pain in my lower back. It started three days ago, and it is worse at night.* Add your allergies and medicines, and a doctor or pharmacist can understand you quickly.

This guide is about language only. It is not medical advice and it cannot tell you what is wrong or what to do. If you think it is an emergency (trouble breathing, chest pain, heavy bleeding, sudden weakness, a serious injury), do not read an article: call your local emergency number straight away, such as 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia, or 112 in many countries. Say your location and the problem in the simplest words you can.

## Who you see and where: GP, family doctor, walk-in, pharmacy

Health systems have different names, and the wrong word can send you to the wrong place.

{{diagram: d1 | The same health ideas have different names by country. Learn the local word for where you live.}}

Some notes:

- **GP, family doctor, primary care.** This is the first doctor you usually see for ordinary problems. In the UK and Australia, *GP* is the normal word; in the US and Canada, people say *family doctor*, *primary care doctor* or *my doctor*. The GP can send you to a *specialist* (a doctor for one area, such as skin or heart) with a *referral*.
- **Walk-in clinic and urgent care.** These are places where you may be seen without an appointment, usually for non-serious problems. The names and what they offer vary, so check locally.
- **Pharmacy, chemist, drugstore.** A *pharmacist* can often answer simple questions about medicines. *Chemist* is common in the UK and Australia; *drugstore* is common in the US and Canada, where it may also sell snacks and cosmetics.
- **Emergency room, A&E, emergency department.** For serious emergencies. Do not use it for a routine appointment.
- **Insurance and fees.** In some countries you show an *insurance card* or *health card*. Ask at reception: *Do I need to pay today?* Rules differ widely, so check with the clinic or your insurer.

## Making an appointment

Most clinics let you book by phone, online or in person. The phone call is short and predictable.

> **Receptionist:** Good morning, Riverside Medical Centre. How can I help?
>
> **You:** Hello, I'd like to make an appointment to see a doctor, please.
>
> **Receptionist:** Are you registered with us?
>
> **You:** Yes, I am. My name is Anna Weber, date of birth the fourth of May, 1990.
>
> **Receptionist:** What is the appointment for?
>
> **You:** I've had a bad cough and a sore throat for about a week.
>
> **Receptionist:** I can offer you Thursday at ten past nine.
>
> **You:** Thursday at nine ten is fine. Thank you.

Useful lines:

- *I'd like to book an appointment.* / *Do you have anything available today?*
- *Is it possible to see a female doctor?* (a reasonable request in many places)
- *I'm a new patient. Do I need to register first?*
- *Could I have an interpreter, please?* (many clinics can arrange one, but ask in advance)
- *I need to cancel or change my appointment.* / *Could I move it to next week?*
- *It's urgent. Is there a doctor I can see today?*

If you are not sure what to say to the receptionist, a short reason is enough. You do not need medical vocabulary: *a bad cough*, *a skin rash*, *a pain in my knee*.

## Body parts and common symptoms

Learn the body words first. They make every sentence easier.

| Part | Common problem | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| head | headache, dizzy | *I have a headache.* / *I feel dizzy.* |
| throat | sore throat, cough | *My throat is sore.* |
| chest | tight, painful | *My chest feels tight.* |
| stomach | stomach ache, nausea | *I feel sick* (UK) / *I feel nauseous.* |
| back | back pain | *I've hurt my back.* |
| skin | rash, itchy | *I have a rash on my arm.* |
| eye / ear | sore, blocked | *My ear hurts.* / *My nose is blocked.* |
| whole body | fever, tired | *I have a temperature.* / *I feel very tired.* |

More small points that matter:

- **Ache vs pain.** *Ache* is usually a dull, continuing feeling (*a headache*, *a stomach ache*, *my back aches*). *Pain* is more general and can be sharp or strong (*a sharp pain in my side*). Both are fine. *It hurts* is the simplest: *My knee hurts.*
- **Fever, temperature.** *I have a fever* and *I have a temperature* mean much the same; *a temperature* is especially common in British English. Say the reading if you know it.
- **Feel sick.** *I feel sick* usually means you feel you may vomit, especially in British English; in the US *sick* also means ill in general, and *nauseous* is the clearer word for needing to vomit.
- **Swollen, itchy, numb, dizzy, bruised.** Words that add detail: *My ankle is swollen.* *My fingers feel numb.*

## Describe symptoms in English: where, what, how long, how bad

Now put the words into a pattern. This is the heart of the visit.

{{diagram: d2 | A clear symptom description has four parts: where, what kind, how long and how bad.}}

**Where:** *I have a pain in my right shoulder.* / *It hurts here* (point).

**What kind:** *It's a sharp pain.* / *a dull ache* / *a burning feeling* / *throbbing* / *it comes and goes* / *it's constant.*

**How long (since and for).** This is where many learners make mistakes. Use *for* with a length of time and *since* with a starting point:

- *I've had a headache **for three days**.*
- *I've had a headache **since Monday**.*
- *It started **two days ago**.* (past simple with *ago*)

Do not say *I have a headache since Monday* or *I am sick since two days.* Use the present perfect: *I've had…*, *I've been feeling…* See our guide to [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) for why.

**How bad:** doctors often say *On a scale of one to ten, how bad is the pain?* A good answer is *About a six.* You can also say *mild*, *moderate* or *severe*, or *It's getting worse / better / staying the same.* Add *when* and *what makes it worse*: *It's worse when I climb stairs.* *It's worse at night.* *It gets better when I rest.*

A typical consultation:

> **Doctor:** Hello, come in. What seems to be the problem?
>
> **You:** I've had a pain in my stomach for about three days.
>
> **Doctor:** Can you show me where it hurts?
>
> **You:** Here, on the lower right side. It's a sharp pain, and it comes and goes.
>
> **Doctor:** How bad is it, on a scale of one to ten?
>
> **You:** About a five. It's worse after I eat.
>
> **Doctor:** Have you had a fever, or been sick?
>
> **You:** I've felt a bit nauseous, but I haven't been sick. No fever, I think.
>
> **Doctor:** Do you have any allergies, or are you taking any medicine?

Tip: if you do not understand a word, say so. *Sorry, could you say that again more slowly?* and *What does that mean?* are normal and expected.

## Allergies, medicines and medical history

The doctor will almost always ask about these, so prepare your answers in advance.

- *I'm allergic to penicillin.* / *I don't have any known allergies.*
- *I'm taking medication for high blood pressure.* / *I take a daily vitamin.*
- *I'm on the contraceptive pill.* (an example; say only what you wish to share)
- *I've had this before, two years ago.*
- *My mother has diabetes.* (family history)
- *I'm pregnant.* / *I might be pregnant.*

Write your medicines (the exact names and doses) on your phone or on paper. If you have a *condition* (a long-term health problem), say it near the start: *I have asthma.* *I have type 2 diabetes.* Remember: *allergic to*, not *allergic with*. And *medicine* is the thing you take; *medication* is a slightly more formal word for the same idea; *drug* can sound like illegal drugs in everyday speech, so use *medicine* for ordinary tablets.

## Understanding what the doctor says

Doctors give instructions quickly. Know these patterns:

- *I'm going to examine you.* / *Could you take a deep breath?* / *Does this hurt?*
- *I'll give you a prescription.* (written permission to get a medicine)
- *I'd like to send you for a blood test / an X-ray.*
- *I'd like to see you again in a week if it's not better.*
- *Take one tablet twice a day, after food.* (*twice a day* means two times each day)
- *Drink plenty of fluids and get some rest.*

If you are unsure about the instructions, check them back: *So I take one tablet in the morning and one in the evening, with food. Is that right?* Ask also: *How long should I take it for?*, *Are there any side effects?* and *When should I come back, or what should I do if it gets worse?* Writing down the answer is perfectly normal.

## At the pharmacy

A pharmacist can help with simple problems and explain how to use your medicine. Useful lines:

- *Do you have anything for a sore throat?*
- *I have a prescription from my doctor. Here it is.*
- *How often should I take this?* / *Can I take it with food?*
- *Is it safe to take this with my other medicine?*
- *Could you write the instructions down for me, please?*
- *Do I need a prescription for this?*

Medicine labels use words like *dose* (the amount), *tablet*, *capsule*, *drops*, *cream*, *side effects* and *expiry date*. Read the label and ask the pharmacist if anything is unclear. This article cannot say which medicine is right for you; follow the advice of your own doctor or pharmacist.

## Mistakes learners make at the doctor

1. **Using "since" and "for" wrongly.** *I've had a cough for a week* (not *since a week*).
2. **Saying "I have pain" without a place.** Add the location: *I have pain in my left ear.*
3. **Saying "I am hurt".** For an injury, *I hurt my ankle* or *I've hurt my ankle* sounds natural. *I'm hurt* sounds more dramatic.
4. **Mixing "sick" and "ill".** In the UK, *ill* is the usual word for being unwell and *sick* often means vomiting; in the US, *sick* more often means ill. Both are understood.
5. **Staying silent when you do not understand.** Ask for repetition. Clear communication is the doctor's goal too.
6. **Using translation apps without checking.** They can help, but confirm important words such as allergies and medicine names.

4. You have a dull, continuing pain in your back. Which word fits: *ache* or *burn*?
5. What does *twice a day* mean?
6. How do you say that a medicine causes a bad reaction in you?

**Answers**

1. *I've had a headache since Monday.* Use the present perfect with *since*.
2. *On a scale of one to ten?* It asks for the level of pain. *How long?* asks about time.
3. A general practitioner: the family doctor you usually see first.
4. *ache* (*My back aches.*). *Burn* describes a hot feeling.
5. Two times in each day, for example morning and evening.
6. *I'm allergic to it.* or *It gives me a rash / makes me feel sick.* For a serious reaction, tell the doctor straight away.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between ache and pain?

*Ache* usually means a dull, steady discomfort, such as a headache or a stomach ache. *Pain* is a more general word and can be sharp or strong. In speaking, *It hurts* is the easiest and most natural form.

### Is it "since" or "for" when I describe symptoms?

Use *for* with a length of time (*for three days*) and *since* with a starting point (*since Monday*). Both usually go with the present perfect: *I've had a cough for three days.*

### What is the difference between a GP, a family doctor and a walk-in clinic?

A GP (UK, Australia) or family doctor (US, Canada) is the doctor who knows your history and sees you for ordinary problems. A walk-in clinic usually sees people without a booking for minor problems. Names and services differ by place, so check locally.

### What should I do if I cannot explain my problem in English?

Say *I'm sorry, my English isn't very good* and show a short note or photo on your phone. Ask for an interpreter if one is available. In an emergency, call your local emergency number and say your location and the problem in simple words.
