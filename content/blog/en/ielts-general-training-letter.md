An **IELTS General Training letter** is Writing Task 1 of the General Training test: you read a short situation and write a letter of **at least 150 words** that covers three bullet points. You have 60 minutes for the whole Writing test, and the usual advice is to spend about 20 of them on the letter, because Task 2 (the essay) is worth more. The letter can be **formal**, **semi-formal** or **informal**, and choosing the right tone is half the task.

This guide explains how to recognise the letter type, how to plan one paragraph per bullet point, which phrases suit each tone, and what a strong answer looks like.

## What the IELTS General Training letter task looks like

A typical prompt has three parts: a situation, an instruction and three bullet points.

> *You recently bought a coffee machine online, but it stopped working after a week.*
> *Write a letter to the company. In your letter:*
> - *describe what you bought and when*
> - *explain what the problem is*
> - *say what you would like the company to do*
>
> *Write at least 150 words. You do NOT need to write any addresses. Begin your letter as follows: Dear Sir or Madam,*

Four details matter here. You must cover **all three bullets**; you must not write addresses; you should begin exactly as instructed; and you should write a full letter in paragraphs, not a list.

## Formal, semi-formal or informal?

{{diagram: d1 | Decide the tone from the reader: stranger or organisation, someone you know a little, or a friend}}

The prompt rarely says "write a formal letter". You decide from **who the reader is** and the relationship:

- **Formal:** a company, an official, a newspaper editor, anyone you don't know. Begin *Dear Sir or Madam,* (or *Dear Mr Brown,* if a name is given) and keep a polite distance.
- **Semi-formal:** someone you know but not as a friend, such as your manager, landlord, teacher or a neighbour. Use their surname with a title (*Dear Mrs Okafor,*), be polite and friendly, avoid slang.
- **Informal:** a friend or relative. Use the first name (*Dear Ali,* or *Hi Ali,*), contractions, phrasal verbs and a warm, personal tone.

The most common tone mistakes are mixing styles (a slangy line in a formal complaint) and writing to a friend as if to a bank (*I am writing to inform you that I will visit*). Our guide to [writing a formal email](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-formal-email/) covers formal register in more depth; the same rules apply here.

### Openings and closings

Traditional British usage, which IELTS examiners know well, links the opening and the closing:

- *Dear Sir or Madam,* → *Yours faithfully,*
- *Dear Ms Lee,* → *Yours sincerely,* (or *Kind regards,* in a semi-formal letter)
- *Dear Sam,* → *Best wishes,* / *All the best,* / *See you soon,*

Then write your name on the next line. For formal letters, use your full name; for informal letters, your first name is enough.

## A five-part structure that works for every letter

{{diagram: d2 | One short opening, one paragraph per bullet point, then a closing line and sign-off}}

1. **Opening (1–2 sentences).** State why you are writing. Formal: *I am writing to complain about a coffee machine I purchased from your website.* Informal: *Thanks so much for your message, and sorry it's taken me a while to reply.*
2. **Bullet 1 paragraph.** Develop it with two or three details: what, when, where.
3. **Bullet 2 paragraph.** This is often the heart of the letter (the problem, the reason, the description). Give a clear example.
4. **Bullet 3 paragraph.** Often a request or a suggestion. Make it specific: what, by when, how.
5. **Closing line + sign-off.** One sentence that looks forward (*I look forward to your prompt reply.* / *Can't wait to see you!*), then the closing phrase and your name.

This gives about 160–190 words: enough to develop every bullet without wasting time you need for Task 2.

## Useful phrases by purpose

| Purpose | Formal / semi-formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|
| Reason for writing | *I am writing with regard to…* | *I just wanted to let you know…* |
| Complaining | *I was disappointed to find that…* | *I was a bit annoyed when…* |
| Requesting | *I would be grateful if you could…* | *Could you possibly…?* |
| Apologising | *Please accept my apologies for…* | *I'm really sorry about…* |
| Suggesting | *It might be helpful to…* | *Why don't we…?* |
| Thanking | *Thank you for your prompt assistance.* | *Thanks a million for…* |
| Ending | *I look forward to hearing from you.* | *Write back soon!* |

Use these as starting points, not a script. Examiners recognise memorised blocks, and a letter built from fixed phrases that don't fit the situation reads as unnatural. Phrases are useful when you can adapt them; [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) helps you build natural word combinations for the details in each paragraph.

## A model formal letter (band 8 style)

*Dear Sir or Madam,*

*I am writing to complain about a coffee machine I ordered from your website and to request a replacement.*

*On 3 March I purchased the Aroma 300 espresso machine, order number 48217, which was delivered two days later. For the first week it worked perfectly, and I was very pleased with the quality of the coffee.*

*Unfortunately, on 12 March the machine stopped heating water. The power light still comes on, but the water remains cold, and the pump makes a loud grinding noise. I have followed every step in the troubleshooting section of the manual, including descaling the machine, but the problem continues.*

*As the machine is still well within its warranty period, I would be grateful if you could arrange for it to be collected and replaced with a new one. Alternatively, a full refund to my original payment method would be acceptable. I would appreciate a reply within seven days so that I can make other arrangements if necessary.*

*I look forward to your prompt reply.*

*Yours faithfully,*

*Daniel Mehta*

That is about 175 words. Notice how each bullet has its own paragraph, the details are concrete (date, model, symptoms), the request offers two options with a deadline, and the tone stays polite even though it is a complaint.

## How the letter is scored

Task 1 is assessed on four criteria, each equally weighted:

- **Task Achievement:** all bullets covered, developed, with a clear purpose and a consistent, appropriate tone.
- **Coherence and Cohesion:** logical paragraphs and linking that helps the reader, without overusing *firstly, secondly*. See [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/).
- **Lexical Resource:** precise vocabulary for the situation (*faulty*, *refund*, *warranty*), accurate spelling.
- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy:** a mix of sentence types, with few errors. Polite structures such as *I would be grateful if you could* and passives such as *it was delivered* show range naturally.

## Common mistakes in General Training letters

- **Missing or thin bullet points.** One sentence per bullet is not enough development.
- **Wrong tone for the reader.** Slang in a letter to a landlord, or stiff phrases in a letter to a friend.
- **Mismatched opening and closing.** *Dear Sir or Madam … Best wishes*.
- **Inventing irrelevant details.** Creative details are fine if they serve the bullet; a long backstory wastes words.
- **Under 150 words.** Short letters usually fail to develop the bullets, which affects your score.
- **Spending 30 minutes on Task 1.** Task 2 carries more weight; see our [Task 2 structure guide](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/) and keep at least 40 minutes for it.

## Self-check: choose the tone

Decide whether each prompt needs a formal, semi-formal or informal letter, and write a suitable opening line.

1. Write to your neighbour, whom you have met a few times, about noise from their late-night parties.
2. Write to a friend who has invited you to their wedding, but you cannot attend.
3. Write to a local council about a broken streetlight on your road.
4. Write to your manager asking for two days off next month.

### Answers

1. **Semi-formal.** *Dear Mr and Mrs Fernandes, I hope you are both well. I am writing about something that has been affecting my sleep recently.*
2. **Informal.** *Dear Leena, Thank you so much for the beautiful invitation; I was thrilled to hear your news!*
3. **Formal.** *Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to report a broken streetlight on Hill Road that has been out of order for two weeks.*
4. **Semi-formal.** *Dear Ms Wong, I am writing to ask whether I could take two days of leave next month.*

## Frequently asked questions

### How many words should an IELTS General Training letter be?

At least 150 words. Around 160–190 words is a good target: enough to develop all three bullet points without taking time from Task 2.

### Do I need to write addresses or a date?

No. The prompt usually states that you do not need to write any addresses, and a date is not required either. Start directly with the greeting given in the prompt.

### Can I use contractions?

In informal letters, yes; they make the tone natural. In formal letters, avoid them. In semi-formal letters, a few are acceptable, but full forms are safer.

### Is the General Training letter easier than Academic Task 1?

It is different rather than easier. Academic Task 1 tests describing data; the General Training letter tests choosing the right tone and developing everyday situations. Both are scored with the same four criteria.
