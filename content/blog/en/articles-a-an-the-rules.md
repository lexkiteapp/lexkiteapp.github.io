The core **a, an, the rules** fit into two questions. First, does your listener know *which one* you mean? If yes, use **the**. If not, ask the second question: is the noun a single countable thing? If yes, use **a** or **an**; if it is plural or uncountable and you mean things in general, use **no article**. *A* or *an* depends on the first *sound* of the next word, not the first letter. Almost every article choice you will make comes from these steps, and the rest of this guide shows how to apply them quickly and where the exceptions lie.

## Why articles are hard for so many learners

Articles are among the most frequent words in English, and among the most frequently misused by learners. The reason is usually the first language.

- **Languages with no articles at all**, including Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Russian, Polish, Hindi, Urdu, Thai, Vietnamese and Indonesian, show "known or unknown" through context, word order or demonstratives such as *this* and *that*. Speakers of these languages tend to **drop articles**: *I bought new laptop. Laptop is very fast.*
- **Languages with articles used differently**, such as Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian and Arabic, often use the definite article for general statements. Their speakers tend to **add "the" where English uses none**: *The life is difficult. The children need the love.*

Knowing which group you belong to tells you which error to hunt for when you check your writing. The decision tree below works for both.

## A, an, the rules as a decision tree

Ask these questions in order and stop at the first "yes".

1. **Does the listener or reader know exactly which one(s) I mean?** → **the**
   *Can you close the window?* (there is one obvious window)
2. **Is it a singular countable noun?** → **a / an**
   *She's applying for a scholarship.* (one of many, not yet identified)
3. **Is it plural or uncountable, used in a general sense?** → **no article (Ø)**
   *Scholarships are competitive. Information is power.*

The first question does most of the work, so it helps to know *why* a reader would know which one you mean.

## When to use "the"

A noun is "known" for one of these reasons:

- **Mentioned before.** *I ordered a pizza and a salad. The pizza arrived cold.*
- **Unique in the context.** *the sun, the internet, the government* (of the country being discussed), *the manager* (in a company with one manager).
- **Defined by what follows.** *The book that you lent me was brilliant.* The relative clause tells you which book.
- **Superlatives and ordinals.** *the best option, the first step, the only solution.*
- **Shared situation.** *Pass me the salt* (at the table). *I'll meet you at the station* (the one we both use).
- **Some fixed groups.** Rivers, oceans and mountain ranges (*the Nile, the Pacific, the Andes*); plural country names and those with *Republic*, *Kingdom* or *States* (*the Philippines, the United Kingdom*); musical instruments in the sense of playing them (*she plays the guitar*).

A common pattern in data writing: *the number of students* (one specific number) takes *the*, while *a number of students* means "several students". The two phrases have different meanings and need different verbs: *The number of students **has** risen; a number of students **have** complained.* You will see the first pattern constantly when describing an [IELTS line graph](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-1-line-graph/).

## "A" or "an": listen, don't look

Use **an** before a vowel *sound* and **a** before a consonant *sound*.

| Word | First sound | Article |
|---|---|---|
| university, European, one-way ticket | /j/ or /w/ (consonant sounds) | **a** |
| hour, honest, heir | silent *h*, so a vowel sound | **an** |
| MBA, X-ray, SUV | letter names /em/, /eks/, /es/ | **an** |
| UFO, US company | letter name /juː/ | **a** |

The first two rows begin with vowel *letters* and the third with consonant letters, which is exactly why spelling misleads: only the opening sound counts.

The same principle explains why *the* is often pronounced /ði/ before a vowel sound (*the apple*) and /ðə/ before a consonant sound. Our guide to [connected speech](/en/blog/connected-speech-in-english/) covers how these weak forms link into the next word.

## When to use no article

- **General plural or uncountable nouns:** *Dogs are loyal. Water boils at 100°C. Technology changes fast.*
- **Most proper names:** *Ahmed, Brazil, Lagos, Mount Kilimanjaro, Lake Victoria.*
- **Meals, days, months, languages, school subjects:** *We had lunch on Monday. She speaks Tagalog. I'm studying economics.*
- **Some institutions when used for their main purpose:** *go to school* (as a student), *be in hospital* (BrE, as a patient), *go to bed*, *go to prison*. Compare *I went to the school to collect my son*, where you visit the building.
- **Fixed phrases:** *by bus, at night, at home, on foot, in time.*

Note: *in hospital* and *at university* without an article are standard British usage; American English often prefers *in the hospital* and *in college*.

## Three uses that grammar summaries often skip

- **The + adjective for a whole group of people:** *the elderly, the unemployed, the rich*. The phrase is plural: *The elderly **need** better public transport* (not *needs*, and not *elderly people* with *the* unless you mean specific ones).
- **A/an meaning "per":** *twice a week, 60 kilometres an hour, $12 a ticket*. Here *a* replaces *per* and is completely natural in speech and writing.
- **A/an before a name:** *A Ms Ortega called for you* means "someone called Ms Ortega, whom I don't know". Without context, proper names take no article, so this use always signals the speaker is unfamiliar with the person.

## Generalising: three ways to talk about a whole class

English has three ways to make a general statement, and they are not equally common:

- **Plural, no article** (most common, neutral): *Smartphones have changed how we communicate.*
- **"A/an" + singular** (any single example): *A smartphone is a powerful tool for learning.*
- **"The" + singular** (formal, a type or invention): *The smartphone has transformed modern life.*

What you should **not** write is *The smartphones have changed how we communicate* if you mean smartphones in general. With *the*, the sentence refers to specific phones already identified. This is exactly the error Spanish, French and Arabic speakers need to watch for, and it appears often in essays, so check it whenever you plan an [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/) around general claims.

## Paired examples: wrong and right

| ✗ Incorrect | ✓ Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| She is engineer. | She is an engineer. | Jobs take *a/an* when singular. |
| The pollution is serious problem. | Pollution is a serious problem. | General uncountable noun; singular countable *problem*. |
| I have a good news. | I have some good news. | *News* is uncountable. |
| He gave me an useful tip. | He gave me a useful tip. | *Useful* starts with a /j/ sound. |
| Moon was bright last night. | The moon was bright last night. | Unique object. |
| I go to the work by the bus. | I go to work by bus. | Fixed phrases with no article. |
| It's the most expensive city in world. | It's the most expensive city in the world. | *The world* is unique. |

Several of these errors overlap with countable and uncountable nouns. *Advice*, *equipment*, *furniture*, *information*, *luggage* and *research* are uncountable in English even if they are countable in your language, so never use *a* with them or add *-s*. For more nouns that behave unexpectedly, see [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

## How to check your articles when you write

Articles are easier to fix when proofreading than to get right while drafting. A two-minute routine:

1. **Find every singular countable noun.** Each one needs a determiner (*a/an, the, my, this, every…*). A bare *student*, *problem* or *reason* is almost always wrong.
2. **Find every "the".** Ask: does the reader know which one? If not, change it to *a/an* or delete it.
3. **Check general statements.** If you mean a whole category, use plural or uncountable nouns without *the*.

Learners from article-less languages should spend most of their checking time on step 1; learners from languages with generic *the* should focus on steps 2 and 3. Combine this with the tense check from our [present perfect vs past simple](/en/blog/present-perfect-vs-past-simple/) guide and you will catch a large share of everyday grammar slips.

## Self-check

Fill each gap with *a*, *an*, *the* or Ø (no article).

1. My sister works as ___ architect in Manila.
2. ___ honesty is important in any relationship.
3. Could you turn off ___ lights when you leave?
4. She has ___ MSc in data science.
5. ___ Amazon is ___ longest river in South America.
6. I usually have ___ breakfast at seven.
7. We stayed in ___ hotel. ___ hotel had ___ amazing view.
8. He wants to study at ___ university in Europe.

### Answers and explanations

1. **an** – a job, singular, and *architect* starts with a vowel sound.
2. **Ø** – *honesty* is uncountable and used generally.
3. **the** – specific lights in the shared situation.
4. **an** – *MSc* is said "em-es-see", starting with a vowel sound.
5. **The, the** – a river name and a superlative.
6. **Ø** – meals normally take no article.
7. **a, The, an** – first mention, then a known hotel, then a singular countable noun beginning with a vowel sound.
8. **a** – *university* starts with the /j/ sound.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is it "a university" and not "an university"?
Because *university* begins with the sound /j/ ("you"), which is a consonant sound. The rule depends on pronunciation, not spelling, which is also why we say *an hour* and *an MBA*.

### Should I use "the" when talking about things in general?
Usually not. For general statements, use a plural or uncountable noun without an article: *Cars cause pollution.* *The* + singular (*the car*) is a formal option for a whole class or invention.

### Can an uncountable noun ever take "a"?
Not directly, but you can use a unit word: *a piece of advice, an item of news, a cup of coffee*. Some nouns have both uses: *a coffee* (one cup) and *coffee* (the drink in general).

### Do article mistakes affect my IELTS score?
They count as grammatical errors. Occasional slips are expected even at higher bands, but frequent article errors make writing harder to read and can limit your Grammatical Range and Accuracy score.
