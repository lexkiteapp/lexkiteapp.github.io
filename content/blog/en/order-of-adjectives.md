Native speakers rarely think about the **order of adjectives**, yet they notice at once when it is wrong. *A wooden old table* is understood, but it sounds off, while *an old wooden table* sounds natural. The good news is that the order is not random: English follows a fairly stable sequence, and a short chart plus a few tests will cover almost every sentence you write.

This guide gives the chart, explains when it applies, shows how to use commas and *and*, covers the real exceptions, and ends with practice you can check yourself.

## The order of adjectives chart

When two or more adjectives come **before a noun**, they usually follow this sequence. Many teachers remember it as OSASCOMP: Opinion, Size, Age, Shape, Colour, Origin, Material, Purpose.

{{diagram: d1 | A tabletop of eight slots. You will almost never use more than three or four of them in one phrase.}}

| Category | What it tells us | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Opinion | what the speaker thinks | lovely, awful, delicious, comfortable |
| Size | how big | small, huge, tall, tiny |
| Age | how old or new | old, new, ancient, young |
| Shape | form | round, square, flat, narrow |
| Colour | colour | brown, red, dark, pale |
| Origin | where from | Italian, Canadian, Roman, northern |
| Material | what made of | wooden, cotton, plastic, stone |
| Purpose | what for | dining, running, sleeping, shopping |

Some examples with the chart in action:

- *a **lovely small** cottage* (opinion, size)
- *an **old round** mirror* (age, shape)
- *a **long black** coat* (size, colour)
- *a **beautiful red Italian** car* (opinion, colour, origin)
- *a **small old wooden** box* (size, age, material)
- *a **comfortable leather** armchair* (opinion, material)
- *a **big cotton sleeping** bag* (size, material, purpose)

Notice the last item in the chart, **purpose**, is often a noun used like an adjective and is joined tightly to the noun: *a running shoe*, *a shopping bag*, *a dining table*. These are close to compound nouns, so nothing else can come between them and the noun.

## Why this order, and how strict is it?

The order moves from adjectives that describe the speaker's view (opinion) to adjectives that describe objective, essential qualities of the thing (material, purpose). The closer an adjective is to the noun, the more it is part of what the thing *is*. A *wooden table* is a kind of table; a *lovely table* is only your opinion of it. This is why opinion words come first and material and purpose words sit next to the noun.

It is a strong tendency and not a law of grammar. Speakers do change the order for emphasis or style, and writers do it deliberately. But for everyday and exam English, following the chart is safe. When in doubt, the safest tip is to keep the phrase short: two or three adjectives is natural, and a long chain of five sounds heavy even if the order is correct. Our [guide to complex sentences](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/) shows other ways to add detail without piling up adjectives.

## Commas and "and" between adjectives

Not all adjectives are in a chain. Two types behave differently.

**Cumulative adjectives** build up, each one modifying the combination that follows it: *a small wooden box*. You do **not** use a comma or *and*, and you cannot swap the order.

**Coordinate adjectives** describe the noun separately and equally: *a cold, wet night*. You can use a comma or *and* between them, and you can often swap them: *a wet, cold night*.

{{diagram: d2 | Two quick tests separate the two types.}}

Two quick tests: (1) Can you put **and** between them? *A cold and wet night* works, so it is coordinate. *A small and wooden box* does not feel natural, so it is cumulative. (2) Can you reverse the order? *A wet, cold night* works. *A wooden small box* does not.

### Colours and "and"

With two or more colours, use *and* for a pattern or a mixture: *a black and white photograph*, *a red, white and blue flag*. When there is a single colour with other adjectives, do not use *and*: *a large red balloon*.

### After a linking verb

The chart is for adjectives **before a noun**. After *be*, *look*, *seem* or *feel*, adjectives are separated by commas, with *and* before the last one: *The room was big, bright and cheerful.* The sentence *The table is old, wooden and beautiful* is fine, because there is no noun for the chain to attach to. This is a place where learners over-apply the chart.

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

| Mistake | Better |
|---|---|
| a wooden old table | an old wooden table |
| a red big car | a big red car |
| a plastic blue bottle | a blue plastic bottle |
| a French beautiful song | a beautiful French song |
| a leather brown, comfortable sofa | a comfortable brown leather sofa |
| a very big and old house | a very big, old house or a big old house |

Another common error is to use too many adjectives. *She wore a beautiful, long, red, silk, summer dress* is correct in order but heavy. Choose the two or three that matter most, and put the rest in a second sentence.

## Exceptions and special cases

1. **Emphasis.** Writers may break the order for effect: *Beautiful, the garden was. Silent, the house stood.* These are stylistic, so do not copy them in an exam unless you are sure.
2. **Fixed phrases.** *Big bad wolf*, *good old days* and *tall, dark and handsome* have their own order.
3. **Numbers and determiners.** Determiners (*a, the, my, those*) come first, then numbers (*three, first*), then adjectives: *my first big job*, *those three old houses*.
4. **Adverbs before adjectives.** *A very old wooden bridge*, *a surprisingly comfortable chair*. The adverb modifies the adjective after it, so it sits directly in front of it.
5. **Participles.** *Broken* and *-ing* adjectives usually go with other qualities: *a beautiful broken vase*, *a small sleeping dog*. The same chart logic works: opinion before condition and noun.

## Using adjective order in writing and exams

In IELTS Writing and Speaking, accuracy in small phrases matters for the grammar and vocabulary criteria. Natural adjective order does not earn marks on its own, but wrong order in a noun phrase can be counted as a small error, and several errors across an essay add up. Keep phrases short and precise: *a significant environmental problem*, *a rapid technological change*, *a wide range of options*. In these, the opinion or degree word comes before the descriptive one. ## Self-check

Put the adjectives in the natural order.

1. a (wooden / old / lovely) cabinet
2. a (red / big / plastic) bucket
3. a (Japanese / delicious / small) cake
4. a (leather / black / new) jacket
5. Which is correct: (a) a tall, thin man or (b) a tall thin, man?
6. Fix: *an English wonderful old castle*.

### Answers

1. **a lovely old wooden cabinet** (opinion, age, material).
2. **a big red plastic bucket** (size, colour, material).
3. **a delicious small Japanese cake** (opinion, size, origin).
4. **a new black leather jacket** (age, colour, material).
5. **(a) a tall, thin man.** Both describe the man separately, so they are coordinate and take a comma or *and*.
6. **a wonderful old English castle** (opinion, age, origin).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the correct order of adjectives in English?

Opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose, then the noun. For example: *a lovely small old round brown Italian wooden dining table*, though you should rarely use that many.

### Do I need commas between adjectives?

Only between coordinate adjectives, that is, adjectives that can swap order or take *and*: *a cold, wet day*. Do not add commas to cumulative adjectives such as *a small wooden box*.

### Is it wrong to break the order?

Not in poetry or for emphasis, but in everyday and exam writing, natural order is safer and sounds more fluent.

### How many adjectives can I use before a noun?

Two or three is natural. More than three can sound heavy, so consider moving detail to another sentence.
