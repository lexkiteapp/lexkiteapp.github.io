IELTS collocations are combinations of words that native speakers naturally use together, such as *heavy traffic*, *make a decision* or *pose a threat*. Examiners notice them because the Lexical Resource criterion rewards natural, precise word choice, and even a correct word in the wrong partnership (*strong rain*, *do a mistake*) sounds foreign at once. The fastest way to improve is to stop learning single words and start learning the phrases they live in.

This guide explains the main types of collocation, gives 45 useful pairs grouped by common IELTS topics, shows the errors that learners from different language backgrounds make most often, and ends with a short correction exercise.

## What is a collocation?

A collocation is a word partnership that is more frequent than chance and usually cannot be explained by grammar alone. Nothing in grammar stops you saying *fast food* and *quick food*, yet only the first is normal English. Likewise we *take* a photo, *catch* a cold and *pay* attention, but nobody *makes* a photo in English, even though many languages use that verb.

Collocations are not the same as idioms. An idiom has a meaning you cannot work out from its parts (*a piece of cake* = something easy), which is why [idioms for IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/idioms-for-ielts-speaking/) need far more care than collocations. A collocation is transparent in meaning; the challenge is only in choosing the expected word. That is exactly why it is easy to get wrong: you understand *heavy rain* the first time you see it, so you never notice you would have written *big rain*.

## Why IELTS collocations matter for your band score

In both Writing and Speaking, Lexical Resource is one of four equally weighted criteria. The public band descriptors mention an awareness of style and collocation at band 7, while lower bands describe more frequent errors in word choice. In practice, collocation affects your score in three ways.

1. **Precision.** *A sharp decline* tells the reader more than *a big decrease*.
2. **Naturalness.** Accurate collocations make writing sound fluent even when the vocabulary is not rare.
3. **Error density.** A Task 2 essay with five mistakes like *raise awareness about* or *give an effect* signals limited control, however advanced the individual words are. This is one of the classic [IELTS writing vocabulary traps](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) that cost candidates their target band.

The good news: collocations are learnable in chunks, and because IELTS topics repeat (environment, education, technology, health, work), a few hundred well-chosen pairs go a long way.

## The main types of collocation

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| Adjective + noun | *heavy traffic, a narrow margin, a growing concern, scarce resources* |
| Verb + noun | *make progress, conduct research, pose a threat, meet demand* |
| Noun + noun | *a sense of community, a lack of investment, public transport* |
| Verb + adverb | *rise steadily, decline sharply, vary considerably* |
| Adverb + adjective | *highly unlikely, deeply concerned, widely available* |
| Verb + preposition | *depend on, contribute to, result in, cope with* |
| Noun + preposition | *an increase in, the impact of X on Y, a solution to* |

Verb + noun combinations cause the most errors because the verbs are short, common words (*make, do, take, have, give*) whose choice in English often feels arbitrary to learners.

## 45 useful IELTS collocations by topic

These are high-utility pairs for Task 2 and Speaking Part 3. Each one is neutral or formal enough for writing.

**Environment**
- *reduce carbon emissions*, *renewable energy sources*, *pose a serious threat to*, *combat climate change*, *a sustainable alternative*, *irreversible damage*, *raise public awareness of*, *deplete natural resources*

**Education**
- *acquire knowledge*, *a well-rounded education*, *academic performance*, *gain practical experience*, *drop out of school*, *a heavy workload*, *compulsory subjects*

**Technology**
- *rapid technological advances*, *have access to the internet*, *a heavy reliance on*, *pose privacy risks*, *bridge the digital divide*

**Health**
- *lead a sedentary lifestyle*, *a balanced diet*, *take regular exercise*, *a leading cause of*, *put a strain on healthcare systems*, *mental well-being*

**Work and economy**
- *job security*, *a competitive salary*, *strike a balance between work and life*, *boost economic growth*, *create employment opportunities*, *a high cost of living*, *meet consumer demand*

**Society and government**
- *tackle a problem*, *implement a policy*, *allocate funding to*, *take effective measures*, *a growing gap between rich and poor*, *play a vital role in*, *have a detrimental effect on*

**Describing data (Task 1)**
- *rise steadily*, *fall sharply*, *remain stable*, *reach a peak*, *a slight fluctuation*

### Collocations for Speaking: same idea, lighter register

[Speaking Part 1](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-tips/) and Part 2 are conversational, so the formal pairs above can sound stiff there. Natural spoken collocations are often built on everyday verbs: *get on well with someone*, *keep in touch*, *a tight budget*, *a hectic schedule*, *grab a quick bite*, *take up a hobby*. Save *implement a policy* or *allocate funding* for Part 3 and Task 2, where the discussion is abstract.

Do not try to memorise all 45 at once. Choose the topics you find hardest and learn five pairs a day, each with a sentence of your own. To see collocations in full essays, browse the [band 8 Task 2 sample essays](/en/samples/), where useful phrases are highlighted.

## Common collocation errors by first language

Many collocation errors are translated directly from the learner's first language. Knowing your likely pattern helps you check your own writing.

| Typical error | Natural English | Often seen among |
|---|---|---|
| *do a mistake* | *make a mistake* | Speakers of Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic and Hindi |
| *learn knowledge* | *gain / acquire knowledge* | Chinese and Vietnamese speakers |
| *open / close the light* | *turn on / turn off the light* | Speakers of French, Italian, Arabic, Tagalog and many Asian languages |
| *discuss about the issue* | *discuss the issue* | South Asian and Arabic speakers |
| *strong rain* | *heavy rain* | Speakers of many European and Asian languages |
| *make a party* | *have / throw a party* | Spanish, Italian and Portuguese speakers |
| *high knowledge of* | *extensive / in-depth knowledge of* | Speakers of many languages |

The pattern is predictable: if a phrase feels like a word-for-word copy of something in your first language, check it before you use it in the exam.

## How to look up and record collocations

**Use a learner's dictionary, not a translation app.** Good learner dictionaries show collocations and example sentences under each entry. Look for the words that appear repeatedly around your target word.

**Record the whole pattern.** Write *have an impact on sth*, not just *impact*. Include the preposition and, when useful, a typical adjective: *a significant / lasting / negative impact on*.

**Group by headword.** On one page or card, collect several partners for the same noun:

> **problem**: *tackle / address / solve a problem*; *a serious / growing / persistent problem*; *the root of the problem*

**Check your own combinations.** If you are not sure whether *conduct a survey* or *make a survey* is better, look at the example sentences in a dictionary, or search a large text corpus. When in doubt, use the pair you have actually seen.

**Review them like vocabulary.** Collocations fade just like single words. Add them to flashcards and review them on a schedule, as described in [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/). The [Academic Word List](/en/blog/academic-word-list/) is a good source of headwords to build collocations around.

## Self-check: correct the collocation

Each sentence contains one unnatural collocation. Rewrite it.

1. Governments should take more attention to protecting endangered species.
2. Many students do a lot of mistakes in their first essays.
3. Fast food restaurants have a big influence to children's diets.
4. The number of tourists raised sharply in 2019.
5. Online courses allow people to learn new knowledge at home.
6. We need to discuss about how to reduce traffic.

**Answers:**
1. *pay more attention to*. English *pays* attention; it never *takes* it.
2. *make a lot of mistakes*. English uses *make* with *mistake*.
3. *have a strong / significant influence on*. The preposition is *on*, and *big influence* is weaker in writing.
4. *rose sharply*. *Rise* is intransitive; *raise* needs an object.
5. *gain / acquire new knowledge* or simply *learn new skills*.
6. *discuss how to reduce traffic*. *Discuss* takes a direct object with no *about*.

Questions 4 and 6 also overlap with word-choice problems covered in [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How is learning collocations different from learning vocabulary?

Vocabulary learning often stops at meaning. Collocation learning adds how the word combines with others: the verb, adjective or preposition that naturally goes with it. It turns a word you understand into a word you can use accurately.

### How many collocations do I need for band 7?

There is no official number. What matters is accurate, natural use across common topics. A personal bank of a few hundred well-practised collocations, reused in your writing and speaking, is a realistic target for most candidates.

### Should I memorise full sample sentences?

Memorising whole sentences to reproduce in the exam is risky because they may not fit the question. Memorise the collocations and a flexible sentence pattern instead, then build new sentences for each task.

### How can I tell if a combination I made up is correct?

Check whether it appears in example sentences in a reliable learner's dictionary or in published texts. If you cannot find it, choose a combination you have already seen used.
