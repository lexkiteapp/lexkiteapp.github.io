A good **IELTS study schedule** starts with one honest number: how many weeks you have between today and the test date. A four-week plan is mostly about technique and polish, an eight-week plan can raise accuracy and range in every skill, and a twelve-week plan has room to rebuild foundations before exam practice begins. This guide gives all three, with the weekly rhythm, the hours each one assumes and the checkpoints that show whether the plan is working.

## Choose your IELTS study schedule before you pick books

Start with two measurements: your target band for each skill (see [IELTS band scores explained](/en/blog/ielts-band-scores-explained/) if the rules for the overall are unclear) and a timed practice result. The gap between them, together with the weeks you have, decides the plan.

{{diagram: d1 | Match the plan to the time you have and the distance still to travel}}

Two cautions apply to every plan below. First, nobody can promise a band improvement in a fixed number of weeks; progress depends on your starting point, your first language and how closely you follow feedback. Second, a plan only works if you can actually keep it. A schedule of ten hours a day that collapses in week two is worse than five hours a week done properly. If you want a longer road map from band 5 to 7, read the [IELTS self-study plan](/en/blog/ielts-self-study-plan/); this page is about squeezing a plan into a fixed deadline.

## The four ingredients every schedule needs

Whatever the length, a plan that raises scores contains the same four parts:

1. **Input**: reading and listening to real English at the right level.
2. **Output**: writing and speaking tasks, produced regularly.
3. **Feedback**: every output is checked against the criteria, by a teacher, a partner or a clear checklist.
4. **Review**: the words and errors you meet come back on a schedule instead of disappearing.

Plans fail when they are all input. Watching lectures and reading articles feels productive, but Writing and Speaking only improve when you produce answers and receive feedback. The method in [the IELTS feedback loop](/en/blog/the-feedback-loop-method-for-ielts/) shows how to make that cycle small enough to run every day.

## The 4-week schedule: technique and polish

Use this when you are close to the target and need exam-specific skill. Assume about 10 to 14 hours a week.

| Week | Focus | Typical tasks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnose | One full timed practice test; mark every answer; list errors by type |
| 2 | Fix the weakest skill | Daily 45-minute block on that skill; two others get 30 minutes |
| 3 | Task types and timing | One Task 2 essay and one Task 1 report; Speaking parts 1–3 recorded and replayed; Reading time drills |
| 4 | Simulate and rest | Two full practice tests early in the week; light review only in the last three days |

Keep the last two days gentle. Sleeping well and arriving calm is worth more than one extra essay.

## The 8-week schedule: balanced improvement

This plan suits a candidate about a band away from the goal. Assume 8 to 12 hours a week.

| Weeks | Goal | Weekly rhythm |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Baseline and habits | Placement or practice test; set up an error log; learn question types |
| 3–4 | Accuracy | Grammar for the errors in your log; paraphrase drills; Reading question-type practice |
| 5–6 | Range | Linking and complex sentences for Writing; answer extension for Speaking; topic vocabulary |
| 7 | Full-length work | Two timed practice tests; targeted fixes after each |
| 8 | Consolidate | One last practice test; review log; sleep, rest, logistics |

Weeks 3 to 6 are where gains are most likely, because they attack your own recurring errors rather than generic topics. Pair them with specific guides: [how to paraphrase in IELTS](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) for Reading and Writing, [complex sentences for IELTS writing](/en/blog/complex-sentences-for-ielts-writing/) and [linking words for IELTS writing](/en/blog/linking-words-for-ielts-writing/) for range, and [extending answers in Speaking](/en/blog/the-domino-effect-in-ielts-speaking/) for fluency.

## The 12-week schedule: foundations first

Choose this when you are more than a band away, or when your English has been dormant for years. Assume 6 to 10 hours a week, rising toward the end.

| Weeks | Goal | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Foundations | Core grammar (tenses, articles, clauses), daily reading at a comfortable level, pronunciation basics |
| 4–6 | Skills with support | Untimed tasks with models; build topic vocabulary; first full-length Writing and Speaking attempts with feedback |
| 7–9 | Exam control | Timed sections; Reading question types; Listening distractors; Task 1 and Task 2 under 20 and 40 minutes |
| 10–11 | Simulation | Full mock tests weekly; compare skill by skill; repair the lowest skill |
| 12 | Taper | One last practice test, review, rest |

In the 12-week plan, keep the early weeks free of full timed tests after your first diagnostic. Doing three mock tests in week one only shows you that you are not ready, and it eats time that foundations need.

## A weekly rhythm that works for all three plans

Seven days, a template to adapt (add a second short session on days when your weekly hours allow):

- **Day 1 – Listening**: one section, then check every miss against the script.
- **Day 2 – Reading**: one passage, timed, then an error review.
- **Day 3 – Writing Task 2**: plan five minutes, write, use the checklist from [IELTS Writing Task 2 structure](/en/blog/ielts-writing-task-2-structure/).
- **Day 4 – Speaking**: record Parts 1 to 3, listen back for fillers, hesitation and repeated words.
- **Day 5 – Writing Task 1 or vocabulary**: a graph report or topic words.
- **Day 6 – Mixed review or a mock section**.
- **Day 7 – Rest or a very light review.**

Vocabulary should be steady, not heroic. Reviewing words at spaced intervals beats long weekend cram sessions, as explained in [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/).

## How to tell the plan is working

Check these every two weeks, not daily:

- Your error log has fewer repeated entries than before.
- Timed Reading finishes with a minute or two to check answers.
- Task 2 essays reach the word count with a clear position and two developed body paragraphs.
- Speaking answers extend naturally without long silences.
- A full practice test shows the weakest skill moving, even by half a band.

If nothing moves for two weeks, change the method, not just the quantity. More of the same practice with no feedback seldom helps.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many weeks do I need to prepare for IELTS?

It depends on the gap between your current level and your target. If you are close, four weeks of focused preparation may be enough; if you need a whole band or more, eight to twelve weeks gives you room to improve accuracy and range. Results vary, so measure with a timed practice test.

### How many hours a day should I study for IELTS?

Many candidates do well with one to two focused hours a day on most days, plus a longer session at the weekend. Quality and feedback matter more than the total number of hours.

### Should I take mock tests every week?

Not at the start. Take one early to diagnose, then do targeted practice, and move to full mock tests every one to two weeks in the final month. Review every mock test carefully, because the review is where the learning happens.

### Can I change plans halfway through?

Yes. If a practice test shows a different weakest skill than you expected, shift your hours toward it and keep the rhythm. A plan is a tool, not a contract.
