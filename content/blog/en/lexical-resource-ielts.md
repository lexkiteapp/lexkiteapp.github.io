When people say "I need a bigger vocabulary for IELTS," they usually mean a list of difficult words. But **lexical resource IELTS** scoring is more than hard words. It is one of the four marking criteria in both Writing and Speaking, and it measures how well you can use words and phrases to say what you mean. Learning to improve this one criterion can raise your overall band, and it often needs a smarter method, not more hours.

This guide explains what examiners look for, the difference between range and accuracy, common traps and a practical weekly plan.

## What is lexical resource IELTS marking?

In both Writing and Speaking, each criterion is worth the same share of the mark. Lexical resource is the criterion that looks at the **words and phrases** you use. According to the public band descriptors on the official IELTS website, examiners consider:

- **Range:** whether you can use a variety of words and expressions without repeating the same ones.
- **Precision:** whether you choose words that fit the exact meaning.
- **Natural use and collocation:** whether your word combinations sound natural.
- **Less common vocabulary:** whether you can use idiomatic or topic-specific words, with awareness of style.
- **Errors:** spelling and word formation errors, and whether they cause difficulty for the reader.

Always check the latest official descriptors, because wording may change. You can also see a plain-language summary in the [criteria section of the app](/app/#/criteria).

## Range vs accuracy

Candidates often think "range" means complicated words. In practice, examiners expect you to show a **range you can control**.

A band 6 pattern usually shows adequate vocabulary with some errors that do not stop understanding. A band 7 pattern shows flexibility, some less common words and collocations used with awareness of style, even if there are occasional errors. Higher bands show skilful, natural and precise use.

The key idea: **accuracy limits range**. If every "advanced" word you try is slightly wrong, your range does not help. Our guide to [the sophistication trap in IELTS writing](/en/blog/the-sophistication-trap-in-ielts-writing/) explores this problem.

## Five ways to raise your lexical resource IELTS score

### 1. Learn collocations, not single words

Instead of *"significant"*, learn *"a significant increase"*, *"significantly higher"*, *"play a significant role"*. Collocations are the fastest way to sound natural, and they are easy to reuse. Read our [IELTS collocations list](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) and [make vs do](/en/blog/make-vs-do/) for the most common traps.

### 2. Learn word families

One word gives you several forms: *decide, decision, decisive, decisively, undecided*. Knowing the forms lets you change sentence structures without changing the idea, which helps both range and grammar. See our guide to [prefixes and suffixes](/en/blog/prefixes-and-suffixes/) for how English builds new words.

### 3. Paraphrase

Paraphrasing is the main tool for avoiding repetition: *"children"* → *"young people"*; *"important"* → *"vital / crucial / of great significance"* (when natural). Our [paraphrasing guide](/en/blog/how-to-paraphrase-in-ielts/) shows four reliable methods.

### 4. Build topic vocabulary in context

Writing and Speaking return to topics like environment, health, technology, education and work. Learn small sets in context: see the guides to [environment and climate vocabulary](/en/blog/ielts-vocabulary-for-environment-and-climate-change/), [health and lifestyle](/en/blog/ielts-vocabulary-for-health-and-lifestyle/) and [technology and AI](/en/blog/ielts-vocabulary-for-technology-and-ai/).

### 5. Use idiomatic language carefully

A few natural idioms in Speaking can raise your score, but forced idioms backfire. Use only those you know well; see [idioms for IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/idioms-for-ielts-speaking/).

## Before and after: improving real sentences

Here are three of our own sample sentences, first as a typical learner version and then improved.

1. *Before:* "Many people do bad things to the environment." → *After:* "Many people damage the environment through their everyday habits." (A precise verb replaces a vague phrase.)
2. *Before:* "The internet is very very useful for students." → *After:* "The internet is an invaluable resource for students." (One precise adjective replaces a repeated intensifier.)
3. *Before:* "I like my job because it gives me many chances." → *After:* "I enjoy my job because it offers real opportunities to develop." (Natural collocation: *offers opportunities*.)

In each case the improvement is not a rare word. It is a **more exact** word that sits naturally with its neighbours. Try the same method on your own writing: underline every weak word (*good, bad, thing, very, do, make, get*) and replace it with a more specific one.

## Common traps

| Trap | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Memorised "high-level" words | Wrong context and unnatural collocation | Learn words with example sentences |
| Repeating the key word | Limited range | Use synonyms and paraphrase |
| Wrong word form | *"The educate system"* | Check part of speech |
| Spelling errors | Marked in Writing | Keep a personal error list |
| Informal words in Writing | *"kids", "stuff", "a lot of" repeated* | Use *children, issues, many* |
| Too much repetition of linking phrases | Lowers both range and coherence | Vary your language |

## Lexical resource in Speaking vs Writing

In **Speaking**, you get credit for speaking fluently with natural, flexible words. Clarifying when you cannot remember a word (*"it is a kind of…"*) is a good skill, not a weakness. In **Writing**, spelling, formal register and precise word choice matter more because the examiner can read and re-read your text. Both benefit from the same habit: learn phrases, not isolated words.

## A weekly plan

- **Day 1:** pick a topic and choose 10 collocations or phrases.
- **Day 2:** write 5 sentences using them; correct with a dictionary.
- **Day 3:** do a 10-minute spoken answer using at least five of the phrases.
- **Day 4:** review the list the next day, then after three days, then after a week. Our guides to [spaced repetition](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) and [active recall](/en/blog/active-recall-for-vocabulary/) explain the timing.
- **Day 5:** write a full paragraph with the topic words.
- **Day 6–7:** review mistakes and change one weak habit.

For long-term learning see [how to learn IELTS vocabulary](/en/blog/how-to-learn-ielts-vocabulary/) and the [academic word list](/en/blog/academic-word-list/).

## Self-check

1. Correct: *"She did an important decision."*
2. Give two ways to avoid repeating *"important"*.
3. Which is more natural: *"a high rate of unemployment"* or *"a big rate of unemployment"*?
4. Write the noun form of *decide* and the adjective form of *success*.
5. True or false: using rare words always raises your lexical resource.

### Answers

1. *She made an important decision.*
2. For example: *vital*, *crucial*, *of great significance* (in the right context).
3. **a high rate of unemployment** (collocation).
4. **decision**; **successful**.
5. **False.** Rare words that are wrong or unnatural lower the score.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is lexical resource the same as vocabulary size?

No. Size helps, but examiners judge range, precision, collocation and accuracy in what you actually use.

### Do I need academic words for Speaking?

Not necessarily. Natural, flexible vocabulary on everyday and topic subjects is more important than formal words.

### How many new words should I learn per day?

Fewer than you think: 5 to 10 well-reviewed phrases are better than 30 forgotten words.

### Can I use a synonym finder?

Use it carefully. Always check an example sentence in a dictionary before using a new word.
