**Common phrasal verbs** such as *find out*, *give up* and *come up with* are everywhere in spoken English, films, emails and conversations at work. In conversation, native speakers often choose them over one-word verbs: *find out* rather than *discover*, *put off* rather than *postpone*. This guide gives you 40 essential phrasal verbs grouped by situation, the word-order rules that cause most mistakes, and a quick way to make them stick.

## What a phrasal verb is

A phrasal verb is a verb plus one or two small words (particles such as *up, out, off, on, in, down, over*) that together create a new meaning, often one you cannot guess from the parts.

{{diagram: d2 | Verb, particle, sometimes a preposition: the parts together carry one meaning}}

- *Give* + *up* = stop trying, quit: *Don't give up now.*
- *Run* + *out* + *of* = have none left: *We've run out of milk.*

Many phrasal verbs have several meanings. *Work out* can mean *exercise* (*I work out three times a week*) or *solve, calculate* (*I can't work out the answer*). Learn the meaning together with an example, never alone.

## Word order: separable, inseparable and three-part verbs

This is where most grammar mistakes with phrasal verbs happen.

{{diagram: d1 | A pronoun object must split a separable verb; inseparable and three-part verbs never split}}

**Separable verbs** (such as *turn off, put on, pick up, fill in, hand in, put off, turn down, give up*) allow a noun object in two places: *turn off the light* or *turn the light off*. But a **pronoun** (*it, them, him, her, me*) must go in the middle:

- ✓ *Turn it off.* ✗ *Turn off it.*
- ✓ *I'll pick you up.* ✗ *I'll pick up you.*

**Inseparable verbs** (such as *look after, deal with, come across, get over, look into*) keep the object after the particle: *She looks after them.* ✗ *She looks them after.*

**Three-part verbs** (*come up with, run out of, cut down on, put up with, look forward to, get along with*) are almost always inseparable: *We ran out of it.*

**No object at all:** some phrasal verbs are intransitive: *get up, hang out, hold on, break down, grow up, go up*. You cannot add an object directly: ✗ *I grew up my children* → ✓ *I brought up my children*.

### The "to" trap in look forward to

In *look forward to*, *to* is a preposition, so it takes a noun or an *-ing* form: *I look forward **to hearing** from you.* ✗ *to hear from you*. The same applies to *object to* and *be used to*. Our guide to [gerunds and infinitives](/en/blog/gerunds-and-infinitives/) explains the rule in full.

## Phrasal verbs in speaking and writing exams

In **IELTS Speaking**, natural phrasal verbs show the examiner flexible, idiomatic vocabulary: *I grew up in a coastal town*, *I've cut down on fast food*. They are often better than idioms, which can sound forced; see our advice on [idioms for IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/idioms-for-ielts-speaking/).

In **academic writing**, a one-word equivalent is often more appropriate:

| Spoken style | Formal writing |
|---|---|
| find out | discover, determine |
| look into | investigate, examine |
| cut down on | reduce |
| go up / go down | increase / decrease |
| put off | postpone |
| come up with | devise, propose |
| deal with | address, handle |

Phrasal verbs are not *wrong* in essays; many appear in academic texts. But choosing *investigate* over *look into* in a formal report shows control of register. Pair this with natural word partnerships from our [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) guide.

## How to learn phrasal verbs so they stick

1. **Learn in context, not lists of particles.** Group by situation, as in this article, not by verb (*get up, get over, get along…*), which mixes unrelated meanings and causes confusion.
2. **Write your own example.** A sentence about your life is far easier to recall than a textbook line.
3. **Retrieve, don't reread.** Cover the meaning column and test yourself, a technique explained in [active recall for vocabulary](/en/blog/active-recall-for-vocabulary/).
4. **Notice them in reading.** Every time you meet one in an article, add the full sentence to your notebook; the method is in [learn vocabulary from reading](/en/blog/learn-vocabulary-from-reading/).

Our [topic vocabulary lists](/en/vocabulary/) are a good source of example sentences to mine for phrasal verbs.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many phrasal verbs do I need to know?

There are thousands, but a few hundred cover most everyday use. Start with the 40 in this article; they appear constantly in conversation and media. Add new ones as you meet them in context rather than from long lists.

### Are phrasal verbs informal?

Many are neutral and appear in all registers (*set up, carry out, deal with*). Some are informal (*hang out, mess up*). In formal writing, prefer a one-word verb when a precise one exists.

### How can I tell if a phrasal verb is separable?

A good learner's dictionary marks it, usually by placing *sth* or *sb* between or after the parts (*turn sth off* versus *look after sb*). When in doubt, keep the object after the whole verb, unless the object is a pronoun and the verb is separable.

### Is "pick up" the same as "pick"?

No. *Pick* means choose (*Pick a card*), while *pick up* means lift or collect (*Pick up your bag; I'll pick you up at eight*). The particle changes the meaning.
