**IELTS Speaking Part 3** is the final section of the Speaking test: a 4–5 minute discussion in which the examiner asks broader, more abstract questions linked to your Part 2 topic. If Part 2 was about a book you enjoyed, Part 3 might ask whether reading habits are changing or how schools could encourage reading. This is where many candidates who did well in Parts 1 and 2 drop marks, usually because their answers stay short and personal. Here is how to go deeper without memorising scripts.

## What happens in IELTS Speaking Part 3

The whole Speaking test takes 11–14 minutes in three parts. Part 1 covers familiar personal topics; Part 2 is a short talk from a task card (one minute to prepare, one to two minutes to speak); Part 3 is a two-way discussion. Our guides to [Part 1](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-1-tips/) and [Part 2](/en/blog/ielts-speaking-part-2-tips/) cover the first two parts.

Three features make Part 3 different:

1. **Abstract topics.** Questions move from *you* to *people*, *society*, *the future*.
2. **Follow-up questions.** The examiner may challenge you: *But don't you think…?*, *Why is that?*
3. **Higher level of thinking.** You are asked to compare, predict, evaluate and speculate.

All four marking criteria apply as usual: Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Part 3 is where you have the most opportunity to show range, because the questions *invite* complex ideas.

## The four-step method for IELTS Speaking Part 3 answers

A good Part 3 answer is usually 3–6 sentences, or roughly 20–40 seconds. Use four steps:

{{diagram: d1 | Answer first, then explain why, show a typical case and extend with a contrast or prediction}}

1. **Answer** directly: *I think it depends mainly on age.*
2. **Reason:** explain the logic. *Older people tend to trust what they can see in person…*
3. **Example:** a typical case, trend or situation, not necessarily from your own life. *For instance, my grandparents still prefer to buy clothes in shops because they want to feel the fabric.*
4. **Extend:** add a contrast, an exception or a look at the future. *That said, I expect the gap to narrow as today's young adults get older.*

You do not need all four steps every time, and you should not sound like you are following a formula. The method's job is to stop you ending after one sentence. Our article on [the domino effect in IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/the-domino-effect-in-ielts-speaking/) shows how each sentence can naturally trigger the next.

## Six question types and how to handle them

{{diagram: d2 | Recognise the question type, and the right grammar and structure follow naturally}}

### Opinion

*Do you think children should learn to cook at school?* Take a position, then qualify it: *Yes, at least the basics, although I wouldn't make it as important as maths or languages.*

### Comparison (past and present, or two groups)

*How has the way people shop changed?* Use contrasting tenses and a reason for the change: *A generation ago, people **used to** shop locally… Nowadays, many **have switched** to online stores, largely because…*

### Prediction

*Will people still read printed books in the future?* Use a range of certainty: *will probably*, *is likely to*, *I doubt that*, *might*. Then justify.

### Cause and effect

*Why do so many young people move to big cities?* Give two causes, then develop the stronger one with an example rather than listing five.

### Solution

*How could governments encourage people to use public transport?* Offer one solution, explain how it works, then mention a limitation: *…although this would only work if services were reliable.*

### Hypothetical

*What would happen if all cars were banned from city centres?* Use the **second conditional**: *If cars were banned, the air would be cleaner, but deliveries would become more difficult.* Our guide to [conditional sentences](/en/blog/conditional-sentences-in-english/) covers mixed forms that add even more range.

## Two model answers

**Question:** *Do you think people read less than they did in the past?*

> I'd say people read just as much, but they read differently. Most of us spend hours a day reading messages, news and posts on our phones, so in terms of quantity, we probably read more than ever. What has declined, I think, is long-form reading: sitting down with a novel for an hour. Shorter content fits into busy schedules, and apps are designed to keep us scrolling. That said, I've noticed that audiobooks are becoming popular among my friends, so perhaps people are finding new ways to enjoy long stories.

Why it works: a direct, nuanced answer; a clear distinction (quantity versus type); a reason; a real trend; and an extension that shows flexible thinking. The vocabulary is precise (*long-form*, *in terms of quantity*) without being forced.

**Question:** *What could be done to reduce the amount of food that is wasted?*

> I think the most effective change would be at the supermarket level. Shops often throw away food that is perfectly safe simply because it's past its "best before" date or doesn't look perfect. If supermarkets were required to donate that food to charities, a huge amount could be saved. Some countries have already introduced rules like this. Of course, it would need careful organisation, because food has to be stored and transported safely, but I think the benefits would outweigh the costs.

Why it works: one solution, developed; a conditional; a passive (*were required*, *be stored*); a limitation; and a balanced conclusion.

## Phrases for buying time (used sparingly)

Abstract questions are hard to answer instantly, and a short, natural pause is fine. A few phrases help you think while still speaking English:

- *That's an interesting question. I suppose…*
- *I've never really thought about that, but I'd say…*
- *It's hard to generalise, but on the whole…*
- *Well, it depends on what we mean by…*

Use one occasionally, not before every answer; examiners recognise memorised openers quickly. The same is true of full sample answers, as explained in [why memorising sample answers ruins IELTS Speaking](/en/blog/why-memorizing-sample-answers-ruins-ielts-speaking/).

## Common mistakes in Part 3

- **Answering only about yourself.** *I don't like shopping online* answers a Part 1 question. In Part 3, widen it: *Many people my age prefer…*
- **One-sentence answers.** The examiner then has to work harder, and you lose chances to show range.
- **Long, memorised speeches.** If your answer sounds like an essay, fluency and naturalness suffer.
- **Absolute claims.** *Everyone uses smartphones* is easy to challenge. Soften with *most*, *tend to*, *in many cases*; the [hedging guide](/en/blog/hedging-in-academic-writing/) applies to speech too.
- **Panicking at a follow-up.** A challenge such as *But isn't that expensive?* is an invitation, not an attack. Agree partly and add your view: *That's true to some extent, but…*

## Self-check

Decide which answer is better for each question, and why.

1. *Should museums be free?*
 (a) *Yes, I like museums.*
 (b) *I think national museums should be free, because they belong to everyone and help people learn about their history.*
2. *How will transport change in the future?*
 (a) *Transport will change a lot.*
 (b) *I expect electric vehicles to become the norm, mainly because many governments are setting targets to reduce emissions.*
3. *Why do some people prefer working from home?*
 (a) *Mainly to avoid commuting. For example, someone who spends two hours a day travelling gains that time back.*
 (b) *Because of reasons like commuting, family, money, health, flexibility and comfort.*
4. Which grammar would best answer *What would happen if everyone worked a four-day week?*
 (a) *If everyone works four days, they are happy.*
 (b) *If everyone worked four days a week, people would probably be less stressed.*

### Answers

1. **(b)** – it answers, qualifies (national museums) and gives a reason.
2. **(b)** – a clear prediction with a reason; (a) is vague.
3. **(a)** – one cause developed with an example beats a list.
4. **(b)** – second conditional for a hypothetical situation.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should my IELTS Speaking Part 3 answers be?

Usually 3–6 sentences, or around 20–40 seconds. Long enough to give a reason and an example, short enough to keep a natural conversation going. The examiner may interrupt to move on; that is normal and not a sign of a problem.

### Can I disagree with the examiner in Part 3?

Yes. You are assessed on your English, not on your opinions. Disagreeing politely (*I see the point, but I'd argue that…*) shows good fluency and coherence.

### Is it okay to say "I don't know"?

It is better to avoid ending there. If you truly don't know, say so and then speculate: *I'm not sure about the figures, but I imagine it's because…* This keeps the discussion going and still shows your language.

### Do I need advanced vocabulary for band 7?

You need *flexible and precise* vocabulary, which is different from rare words. Accurate collocations and the ability to paraphrase when you don't know a word count for more than an idiom used incorrectly.
