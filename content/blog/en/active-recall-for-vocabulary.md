Active recall means pulling information **out** of your memory instead of putting it **in** again. Covering the meaning of *reluctant* and trying to say it before you check is active recall. Reading a word list for the fifth time is not. For vocabulary, the difference is large: words you have retrieved several times stay available for weeks or months, while words you have only reread tend to feel familiar but disappear when you need them in speech or writing.

This guide explains why retrieval works, how to apply it to English vocabulary at four levels of difficulty, which popular habits only look like active recall, and how to build it into 20 minutes a day.

## What active recall is

There are two basic ways to study a word:

- **Input (passive review):** reading the word and its meaning, highlighting, copying it out, listening to a list.
- **Output (active recall):** seeing a cue and producing the answer from memory, before you look.

The cue can be the English word (*What does "reluctant" mean?*), the meaning (*What's the English for "unwilling and hesitant"?*), a gap in a sentence (*She was ___ to sign the contract*), or a situation (*How would you describe someone who doesn't want to help?*).

The key is the moment of effort: you try, you produce an answer or fail, **then** you check.

## Why testing yourself works better than rereading

Memory researchers have studied this for a long time, and the finding is consistent enough to have a name: the **testing effect**, or **retrieval practice**. Practising retrieval strengthens memory more than spending the same time restudying.

One study is especially relevant to vocabulary. In a 2008 experiment published in *Science*, Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger had students learn 40 Swahili–English word pairs. Some students stopped testing a pair once they had recalled it; others kept testing it. A week later, the students who kept retrieving remembered roughly 80% of the words, while those who dropped testing and kept only restudying remembered about a third. Extra restudying after the first success added almost nothing; extra retrieval made the difference. This matters most under timed conditions, for example in the [new TOEFL format](/en/blog/new-toefl-ibt-format-2026/), where words must come back instantly.

There are a few reasons:

1. **Retrieval strengthens the path to the memory.** Each successful recall makes the next one easier. Rereading strengthens recognition (*I've seen this*), which is not the same as recall (*I can produce this*).
2. **Testing shows you what you don't know.** Rereading creates an illusion of knowledge: the word looks familiar, so you assume you know it. A self-test gives you honest feedback.
3. **Retrieval matches real use.** When you speak or write, nobody shows you the word first. You have to find it. Active recall practises exactly that.

### Harder recall, stronger memory, up to a point

The psychologist Robert Bjork used the term *desirable difficulties* for learning conditions that feel harder but produce better long-term results. Recall that requires some effort is one of them. If the answer comes instantly, the review adds little; if you struggle a bit and succeed, the memory gets a strong boost.

But there is a limit. If you fail completely most of the time, you mainly practise frustration. The sweet spot is a success rate high enough to keep you motivated, with enough effort to make each success count. This is exactly what scheduling systems try to achieve, as explained in our guides to [spaced repetition for language learning](/en/blog/spaced-repetition-for-language-learning/) and the [FSRS algorithm](/en/blog/fsrs-algorithm-explained/).

## Applying active recall to English vocabulary

Knowing a word has several layers, so practise recall at increasing levels of difficulty.

### Level 1: recognition (English → meaning)

You see *reluctant* and say the meaning before checking. This is the easiest form, and it is enough for reading and listening comprehension.

### Level 2: multiple choice

You choose the right meaning or the right word from options: *She was ___ to sign: (a) eager (b) reluctant (c) relevant (d) reliable.* Good options are close in form or meaning, which trains precision. It is particularly useful for [commonly confused words](/en/blog/commonly-confused-words-in-english/).

### Level 3: production (meaning → English)

You see a definition or a translation and produce the English word, ideally saying and spelling it. This is much harder than Level 1 and much closer to what speaking and writing need. Many learners skip it and then find that "known" words never appear in their essays.

### Level 4: use in a sentence

You write or say a new sentence of your own with the word: *I was reluctant to change jobs because I liked my team.* This retrieves not just the word, but its grammar and collocations: *reluctant **to** do*, *a reluctant **decision***. Our guide to [IELTS collocations](/en/blog/ielts-collocations/) explains why those partner words matter.

A practical rule: new words start at Level 1–2; after a few successful reviews, move them to Level 3; words you want to use actively in exams or at work go to Level 4.

### Fake active recall to avoid

Some habits look like self-testing but are not:

- **Flipping the card too quickly.** If you glance at the word and turn the card in one second, you have recognised it, not recalled it. Say the answer aloud, or at least clearly in your head, first.
- **Marking "I knew it" after seeing the answer.** Hindsight makes everything seem familiar. Decide before you flip.
- **Testing only in one direction.** English → meaning only builds recognition. Mix in meaning → English.
- **Rewriting a list ten times.** Copying is input, not retrieval. Write from memory instead, then check.
- **Reviewing words in a fixed order.** You start remembering position, not meaning. Shuffle.

Many learners grew up in school systems where vocabulary meant memorising long lists by repetition: reading aloud, copying, chanting definitions before a test. That approach can get you through tomorrow's quiz, but the words rarely survive the month. If that is how you learned, the switch to retrieval will feel slower and more uncomfortable at first, because you see your gaps immediately. That discomfort is a sign the method is working.

A final tip on cues: make them rich. A card that says only *reluctant = unwilling* gives you one weak path to the word. A card with a short example sentence, the pronunciation and a typical partner word gives you several, and each recall strengthens all of them.

## Building active recall into your daily study

### The blank-page technique

After a lesson, an article or a podcast, close everything and write on a blank page all the new words and phrases you remember, with an example for each. Then open your notes and compare. Words you forgot go to the top of tomorrow's review. It takes five minutes and is one of the most honest tests you can do.

### Teach it to someone

Explain a word to a friend, a classmate or even an imaginary student: what it means, how it differs from a near-synonym, an example. If you cannot explain *reluctant* versus *unwilling*, you have found a gap.

### A 20-minute daily routine

1. **5 minutes:** review due flashcards, answering before you flip.
2. **5 minutes:** multiple-choice or matching practice on recent words.
3. **5 minutes:** production practice: from meaning to English, typed or spoken.
4. **5 minutes:** write three sentences with words you want to use actively.

## Self-check

Which of these are real active recall?

1. Reading your word list three times before bed.
2. Covering the meanings column and saying each meaning aloud before checking.
3. Turning a flashcard immediately and thinking "yes, I knew that".
4. Writing every new word you remember from a lesson on a blank page, then comparing.
5. Copying ten new words into a notebook five times each.
6. Seeing a definition and typing the English word.

### Answers and explanations

1. **No.** It is passive review; it builds familiarity, not recall.
2. **Yes.** You produce the answer before seeing it.
3. **No.** Without an attempt before flipping, this is recognition plus hindsight.
4. **Yes.** This is the blank-page technique.
5. **No.** Copying is input. Write from memory instead.
6. **Yes.** Production (Level 3) is one of the strongest forms of recall.

## Frequently asked questions

### What's the difference between active recall and flashcards?

Active recall is the method; flashcards are a tool. Flashcards support active recall only if you answer before flipping. Used passively, by reading both sides at once, they become another form of rereading.

### What if I can't remember anything when I test myself?

Check the answer, study it briefly, and test again later in the same session. Failing a recall attempt and then seeing the answer still helps memory, often more than not testing at all. If you fail most items, make the cue easier or review sooner.

### Can I use active recall for grammar?

Yes. Cover the rule and explain it from memory, then write your own examples. Or complete gapped sentences before checking the answers. The same principle applies: produce first, check second.

### Is active recall the same as spaced repetition?

No, but they work best together. Active recall is *how* you review (produce the answer first); spaced repetition is *when* you review (at growing intervals, just before you would forget). Recall without spacing tends to be crammed into one session; spacing without recall turns into rereading at good intervals. Schedulers such as FSRS, available in Anki since version 23.10, handle the timing so your effort goes into the recall itself.

### How often should I test myself?

Daily short sessions are ideal. The best gaps between reviews grow over time: a day, a few days, a week, and so on. A spaced repetition app handles that scheduling for you, so you can focus on answering honestly.
