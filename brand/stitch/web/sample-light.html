Q Official Essay Prompt
Cambridge IELTS Academic
“Some people believe that university education should be completely free for everyone, regardless of their financial background. Others argue that students should contribute to their tuition fees because higher education primarily benefits the individual.”

Discuss both views and give your own opinion. Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your knowledge or experience.

account_tree

Essay Structure Outline

4-Paragraph Architecture
1 Introduction

Paraphrase the tertiary education funding dilemma + state clear thesis that government subsidy with nominal contributions yields optimal social mobility.

2 Body Paragraph 1

View 1 (Free access): Democratic equalization, preventing generational socio-economic inequality, and cultivating skilled workforce.

3 Body Paragraph 2

View 2 (Personal fees): Private salary returns, institutional quality retention, avoiding excessive burden on taxpayer coffers.

4 Conclusion

Synthesize both viewpoints: propose an income-contingent repayable loan mechanism balancing meritocratic equity and state solvency.

Official Transcribed Essay

Model Answer (Band 8.0)

Whether tertiary education ought to be entirely subsidised by the state or co-funded by undergraduates remains a fiercely debated subject in modern socio-economic discourse. While proponents of state-sponsored tuition champion the principle of meritocratic access, others contend that graduates reap substantial personal dividends that justify financial contribution. In my estimation, a balanced framework encompassing income-contingent student contributions offers the most sustainable pathway for social mobility and institutional vitality.

On the one hand, advocates for free higher education argue that intellectual capital is a public utility rather than a commodified privilege. When university admissions are liberated from tuition costs, individuals from underprivileged backgrounds are no longer deterred by prohibitive debt burdens, thereby dismantling entrenched generational inequality. Furthermore, not only does widespread graduation stimulate overall technological productivity, but it also equips the domestic economy with vital specialists, such as epidemiologists and software engineers, whose collective contributions far outweigh initial exchequer outlays.

On the other hand, compelling arguments underline the necessity of direct student financing. Given that tertiary degree holders statistically command significantly higher lifetime earning potential compared to non-graduates, it is frequently regarded as inequitable to expect the general tax base—many of whom never entered university—to underwrite private professional ascension. Moreover, public universities reliant entirely on government coffers frequently encounter severe budgetary bottlenecks, which inevitably compromise infrastructural standards and teaching caliber unless supplemental revenue streams are preserved.

In conclusion, while the idealistic vision of universal gratis tuition is commendable, practical fiscal constraints and fairness dictate that students share the burden. Were governments to implement flexible, state-underwritten loans payable only after reaching a prosperous income threshold, society would successfully preserve both uncompromised educational equity and fiscal solvency.
